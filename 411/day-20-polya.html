How these work. Each walk-through takes one problem through Pólya’s four phases. You answer one step at a time and get feedback right away; when something isn’t right, read why and try again. Hints are there when you ask for them. Your place is saved in this browser, so you can stop and come back.

Some steps ask you to name the \(N\). Where a step asks for \(N\) as a formula in \(\varepsilon\) (type eps), the page plays the adversary from the Day 17 game: it throws challenges \(\varepsilon\) at your formula, big and small, and checks every term past your \(N\). Any \(N\) that works is right, not only the one in the model proof. And if yours fails, you’ll see the exact challenge it lost.

Most steps today are about the limit laws rather than \(N\): which theorem justifies a step, and what has to be known before it applies. Name the theorem, then check its hypotheses. The walk-throughs end in a full proof, so the proof you bring to class should still be your own, in your own words.

Pólya walk-through · Problem 6.2.11

Pull the constant out of the limit

Problem 6.2.11. Prove Corollary 6.2.10. Corollary 6.2.10: If \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty} a_n = a\) and \(c \in \mathbb{R}\), then \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty} c\cdot a_n = c\cdot a\).

Goal: Feed the definition a challenge that suits \(c\), then deal with the one value of \(c\) that the challenge can’t use. It rests on Problem 6.2.1 (a constant sequence converges to its value) and on the product theorem, Theorem 6.2.6. Its partner, the sum rule (Theorem 6.2.4), completes the linearity of limits.

Pólya walk-through · Problem 6.2.13

Bound the reciprocal, then multiply

Problem 6.2.13. Prove Theorem 6.2.12 (the quotient theorem for limits). Suppose \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty} a_n = a\) and \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty} b_n = b\), with \(b \ne 0\) and \(b_n \ne 0\) for all \(n\). Then \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{a_n}{b_n} = \frac{a}{b}\).

Goal: Get a fixed bound on \(\frac{1}{|b_n|}\) from one well-chosen challenge, show that \(\frac{1}{b_n} \to \frac{1}{b}\), and finish with the product rule. It uses the reverse triangle inequality (Lemma 6.2.2) and the product theorem (Theorem 6.2.6). The key move is the one the Day 20 page highlights: feed the definition a positive challenge chosen for its usefulness, not only the one you were handed.

Pólya walk-through · Problem 6.2.14

Name the theorem behind each equals sign

Problem 6.2.14. Identify all of the theorems implicitly used to show that \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{3n^3-100n+1}{5n^3+4n^2-7} = \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n^3\left(3-\frac{100}{n^2}+\frac{1}{n^3}\right)}{n^3\left(5+\frac{4}{n}-\frac{7}{n^3}\right)} = \frac{3}{5}\). Notice that this presumes that all of the individual limits exist. This will become evident as the limit is decomposed.

Goal: Say which theorem justifies each equality in a chain, and what has to be known before that theorem applies. It is the Chapter 6 toolkit at work. Each limit law can be used without going back to the definition, but only once its hypotheses are checked, and this problem asks you to check them.

Pólya walk-through · Problem 6.2.16

Trap it between two sequences

Problem 6.2.16. Prove Theorem 6.2.15 (the Squeeze Theorem for sequences). Let \((r_n)\), \((s_n)\), and \((t_n)\) be sequences of real numbers with \(r_n \le s_n \le t_n\) for all positive integers \(n\). Suppose \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty} r_n = s = \lim_{n\to\infty} t_n\). Then \((s_n)\) must converge, and \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty} s_n = s\).

Goal: Turn the two outer bounds into two challenges, combine the two N’s, and let the chain trap \(s_n\) in the band around \(s\). The conclusion has two parts, and the proof has to deliver both. It is the move of Example 6.1.3, where \(\frac{\sin n}{n}\) is bounded by \(\pm\frac{1}{n}\), made into a theorem. Unlike the sum, product and quotient rules, it produces convergence rather than assuming it.
