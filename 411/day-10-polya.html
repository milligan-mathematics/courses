How these work. Each walk-through takes one problem through Pólya’s four phases. You answer one step at a time and get feedback right away; when something isn’t right, read why and try again. Hints are there when you ask for them. Your place is saved in this browser, so you can stop and come back.

Something to watch for. Euler works with infinite series and infinite products as if they were polynomials. The steps are formal, which is how Euler and the Day 7 and Day 8 readings handle them. Where a step is taken on trust, the walk-through says so, and the Look back steps say plainly what is assumed.

The walk-throughs end in a full derivation, so the proof you bring to class should still be your own, in your own words.

Pólya walk-through · Problem 3.2.17

Factor it over its roots

Problem 3.2.17. (a) Show that the power series for \(\frac{\sin x}{x}\) is given by \(1-\frac{1}{3!}x^2+\frac{1}{5!}x^4-\cdots\).
(b) Use (a) to infer that the roots of \(1-\frac{1}{3!}x^2+\frac{1}{5!}x^4-\cdots\) are given by \(x=\pm\pi,\,\pm 2\pi,\,\pm 3\pi,\,\ldots\)
(c) Suppose \(p(x)=a_0+a_1x+\cdots+a_nx^n\) is a polynomial with roots \(r_1,\,r_2,\,\ldots,r_n\). Show that if \(a_0\neq 0\), then all the roots are non-zero and \(p(x)=a_0\left(1-\frac{x}{r_1}\right)\left(1-\frac{x}{r_2}\right)\cdots\left(1-\frac{x}{r_n}\right)\).
(d) Assuming that the result in part (c) holds for an infinite polynomial (power series), deduce that \(1-\frac{1}{3!}x^2+\frac{1}{5!}x^4-\cdots=\left(1-\left(\frac{x}{\pi}\right)^2\right)\left(1-\left(\frac{x}{2\pi}\right)^2\right)\left(1-\left(\frac{x}{3\pi}\right)^2\right)\cdots\).
(e) Expand this product to deduce \(\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{1}{n^2}=\frac{\pi^2}{6}\).

Goal: Factor a power series over its roots the way you would a polynomial, then compare coefficients to find a sum that had resisted every earlier attempt. It builds on the series for \(\sin x\) from Day 8 and on the idea from Day 7 of treating a power series as an infinite polynomial. It leads to 3.2.18, where the same formal manipulation turns the exponential into trigonometry.

Pólya walk-through · Problem 3.2.18

Put i theta into the series

Problem 3.2.18. (a) Use the power series expansion of \(e^x\), \(\sin x\), and \(\cos x\) to derive Euler’s formula: \(e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta\).
(b) Use Euler’s formula to derive the addition and subtraction formulas: \(\sin(\alpha\pm\beta) = \sin\alpha\cos\beta\pm\sin\beta\cos\alpha\) and \(\cos(\alpha\pm\beta) = \cos\alpha\cos\beta\mp\sin\alpha\sin\beta\).
(c) Use Euler’s formula to show that \(\sin 2\theta = 2\cos\theta\sin\theta\) and \(\cos 2\theta = \cos^2\theta-\sin^2\theta\).
(d) Use Euler’s formula to show that \(\sin 3\theta = 3\cos^2\theta\sin\theta-\sin^3\theta\) and \(\cos 3\theta = \cos^3\theta - 3\cos\theta\sin^2\theta\). (The reading prints this without the 3 in the second term; the walk-through’s last steps check it.)
(e) Find a formula for \(\sin n\theta\) and \(\cos n\theta\) for any positive integer \(n\).

Goal: Derive Euler’s formula from the power series, then get the trigonometric identities by comparing real and imaginary parts. It builds on the series for \(e^x\), \(\sin x\), and \(\cos x\) from Days 7 and 8, and on the rule \(E(x+y) = E(x)E(y)\) from Day 7. It is the last problem of the day, and its Look back asks what the argument takes for granted.
