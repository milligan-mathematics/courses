Walk-through · Part A

A table of 1,000 people

Part A. Do Part A on paper first, then check in here.
The screening test. A disease affects 2% of people. A screening test catches 90% of people who have it (they test positive), but it also gives a false alarm to 5% of healthy people.
1. Imagine 1,000 people. Fill in the counts: rows “test positive \((+)\)”, “test negative \((-)\)”, total; columns sick, healthy, total. (Start with the bottom row, then the column for the sick people.)
2. Divide every count by 1000. The four numbers inside the table are joint probabilities, like \(p(\text{sick},+)\): the chance a random person is sick and tests positive. What do the four add up to?
3. The sum rule. The chance of a positive test, \(p(+)\), is in the margin. Explain why \(p(+) = p(\text{sick},+) + p(\text{healthy},+)\). In words: to get rid of a variable you don’t care about, add over all of its values.
4. The product rule. The chance of being sick and testing positive is \(p(\text{sick},+) = p(+\mid\text{sick})\,p(\text{sick})\), where \(p(+\mid\text{sick})\), read “probability of \(+\) given sick,” is the 90%. Check it with your table. Then write the matching product for \(p(\text{healthy},+)\) and check that one too.

Goal: Turn three percentages into a table of counts, read joint probabilities off it, and see the sum rule and the product rule as two ways of reading the same table. Every rule today comes out of this one table. Part B reads one row of it and gets Bayes’ theorem; Day 27 runs the same arithmetic with bell curves in place of the 90% and the 5%.

Walk-through · Part B

Bayes’ theorem

Part B. Do Part B on paper first, then check in here.
1. You test positive. Look only at the positive row of your table. Of the people in that row, what fraction are sick? That fraction is \(p(\text{sick}\mid +)\).
2. Bayes’ theorem (MML equation (6.23)) does the same thing with a formula: \[\underbrace{p(\text{sick}\mid +)}_{\text{posterior}} = \frac{\overbrace{p(+\mid\text{sick})}^{\text{likelihood}}\;\overbrace{p(\text{sick})}^{\text{prior}}}{\underbrace{p(+)}_{\text{evidence}}}.\] Compute it with the numbers from Part A. Does it match problem 1? Which part of your table is the numerator, and which is the denominator?
3. A friend says, “The test is right at least 90% of the time, so a positive result means about a 90% chance you’re sick.” In one or two sentences, using your table, what is the friend forgetting?
4. You take a second, independent test, and it’s positive too. Now your old posterior is the new prior: replace \(p(\text{sick}) = 0.02\) with your answer to problem 1. Compute the new \(p(\text{sick}\mid +)\). (The evidence \(p(+)\) changes too: use the sum and product rules.)
5. A specialist’s clinic sees people who already have symptoms, and 20% of them have the disease. Same test. Make a new 1,000-person table (or use the formula) and find \(p(\text{sick}\mid +)\) there. Why is it so different from problem 1?

Goal: Read Bayes’ theorem off one row of the table, see why a positive test from a good test can still mean “probably fine,” and watch the answer move when the prior moves. Part A’s two rules make the evidence; Bayes divides by it. Next week, on Day 27, the same formula decides whether a car is a V8 or a small car, with bell heights as the likelihoods.

Walk-through · Part C

Mean, variance, covariance

Part C. Do Part C on paper first, then check in here.
Five cars. Weight \(x\) in thousands of pounds and fuel economy \(y\) in mpg: \((2, 32),\ (2.5, 30),\ (3, 24),\ (3.5, 20),\ (4, 19)\). As in MML §6.4.2 (and ddof=0 in our notebooks), divide by \(n = 5\): \(\operatorname{Var}[x] = \frac1n\sum_i (x_i - \bar x)^2\), \(\operatorname{Cov}[x, y] = \frac1n\sum_i (x_i - \bar x)(y_i - \bar y)\).
1. Find the means \(\bar x\) and \(\bar y\). (They are the dashed lines in the picture.)
2. Find \(\operatorname{Var}[x]\) and \(\operatorname{Var}[y]\), and the standard deviations (their square roots). What are the units of each?
3. Make a column of \((x_i - \bar x)(y_i - \bar y)\) and find \(\operatorname{Cov}[x, y]\). The picture labels each corner with the signs of the two factors. Which corners give a positive product, which give a negative one, and which corners are these five cars in? Explain why the covariance came out negative.
4. Put your numbers into the covariance matrix \(\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix}\operatorname{Var}[x] & \operatorname{Cov}[x,y]\\ \operatorname{Cov}[x,y] & \operatorname{Var}[y]\end{pmatrix}\). It’s the same kind of matrix you built for the tilted cloud on Day 18. Why must it be symmetric?
5. The correlation is \(\operatorname{Cov}[x,y]/\bigl(\text{sd}(x)\,\text{sd}(y)\bigr)\). Compute it. If weight were measured in pounds instead of thousands of pounds, what would happen to \(\operatorname{Var}[x]\), to \(\operatorname{Cov}[x, y]\), and to the correlation? Which of the three would you quote to someone who wants to know “how strongly do weight and mpg go together”?
6. Compute \(\operatorname{Cov}[x,y]/\operatorname{Var}[x]\), with units. Here’s a surprise: it’s the slope of the least-squares line through these five cars (Days 10–11). Check: the least-squares line always passes through \((\bar x, \bar y)\), so write the whole line and see whether it predicts \(y\) well at \(x = 2\) and \(x = 4\).
Skeptic. “The covariance of these two variables is 0, so they have nothing to do with each other.” Test that claim on the five points \((-2, 4),\ (-1, 1),\ (0, 0),\ (1, 1),\ (2, 4)\): compute \(\operatorname{Cov}[x, y]\), then look at the points. Find the flaw. What kind of relationship does covariance measure, and what kind can it miss? (MML §6.4.5 has a picture of this.)

Goal: Fill the work table, read the covariance’s sign off the picture, and meet three relatives of the covariance: the covariance matrix, the correlation, and the least-squares slope. \(\Sigma\) is Day 18’s covariance matrix, now with a name from probability. On Wednesday’s lab it becomes the shape of a 2-D bell curve, and Day 27’s bells each get their own mean and variance.
