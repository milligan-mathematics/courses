# §14.6: Substitutions and Multiple Integrals

In single-variable calculus, a $$u$$-substitution turns a hard integral into an easy one, and the factor $$du = g'(x)\,dx$$ pays for the change. Polar, cylindrical, and spherical coordinates did the same job in several variables, with the factors $$r$$ and $$\rho^2\sin\varphi$$. This section is the general version: *any* change of variables $$x = g(u,v)$$, $$y = h(u,v)$$ can be used, and the factor that pays for it is the **Jacobian**.

A good substitution does two things at once. It simplifies the integrand, and it turns an awkward region (a slanted triangle, a parallelogram) into a simple one (a rectangle). The Jacobian measures how much the substitution stretches or shrinks area.

By the end of this section you should be able to:

1. Compute the Jacobian of a transformation in two and three variables.
2. Explain why the Jacobian appears in the change-of-variables formula, and why it appears with an absolute value.
3. Choose a substitution from the integrand and the boundary of the region, and find the new region.
4. Evaluate a double or triple integral by a change of variables.

### The Jacobian

A transformation $$T$$ takes a point $$(u,v)$$ to a point $$(x,y)$$ by $$x = g(u,v)$$, $$y = h(u,v)$$. Its **Jacobian** is the determinant of the matrix of partial derivatives:

$$\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)} = \det\begin{pmatrix} \dfrac{\partial x}{\partial u} & \dfrac{\partial x}{\partial v} \\[2mm] \dfrac{\partial y}{\partial u} & \dfrac{\partial y}{\partial v} \end{pmatrix} = \frac{\partial x}{\partial u}\frac{\partial y}{\partial v} - \frac{\partial x}{\partial v}\frac{\partial y}{\partial u}.$$

Here is what it means. A tiny rectangle in the $$uv$$-plane with sides $$\Delta u$$ and $$\Delta v$$ is sent to a nearly flat parallelogram in the $$xy$$-plane whose sides are the vectors $$\left\langle \tfrac{\partial x}{\partial u}, \tfrac{\partial y}{\partial u}\right\rangle\Delta u$$ and $$\left\langle \tfrac{\partial x}{\partial v}, \tfrac{\partial y}{\partial v}\right\rangle\Delta v$$. The area of that parallelogram is the absolute value of the determinant times $$\Delta u\,\Delta v$$. So

$$\text{area of the image cell} \approx \left\lvert \frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}\right\rvert \,\Delta u\,\Delta v.$$

You have already met one Jacobian: for polar coordinates $$x = r\cos\theta$$, $$y = r\sin\theta$$ it is $$r\cos^2\theta + r\sin^2\theta = r$$, the factor from §14.3.

> **Worked example.** The transformation $$x = u^2 - v^2$$, $$y = 2uv$$ has
>
> $$\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)} = \det\begin{pmatrix} 2u & -2v \\ 2v & 2u \end{pmatrix} = 4(u^2 + v^2).$$
>
> This is not a constant. Near $$(1, 0)$$ areas are multiplied by $$4$$, and near $$(2, 1)$$ by $$20$$. The image of the rectangle $$1 \le u \le 2$$, $$0 \le v \le 1$$ therefore has area
>
> $$\int_1^2 \int_0^1 4(u^2 + v^2)\,dv\,du = \int_1^2 \left(4u^2 + \tfrac43\right) du = \frac{32}{3}.$$

If you know the Jacobian of the inverse transformation, the two are reciprocals: $$\dfrac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)} = \dfrac{1}{\partial(u,v)/\partial(x,y)}$$. That can save you from solving for $$x$$ and $$y$$.

### Change of variables in a double integral

**Theorem.** Let $$T$$ send a region $$S$$ in the $$uv$$-plane one-to-one onto a region $$R$$ in the $$xy$$-plane (except possibly along boundaries), with continuous first derivatives and a Jacobian that is not zero. Then

$$\iint_R f(x,y)\,dA = \iint_S f\bigl(g(u,v), h(u,v)\bigr)\,\left\lvert \frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}\right\rvert \,du\,dv.$$

The absolute value is there because area is never negative, even when the transformation flips orientation.

To use it: look at the integrand and at the boundary lines or curves of $$R$$ for expressions worth calling $$u$$ and $$v$$; find $$x$$ and $$y$$ in terms of $$u$$ and $$v$$; compute the Jacobian; **transform the boundary** to find $$S$$; and integrate. A good $$S$$ is a rectangle or another easy region. Skipping the boundary step is the most common way to go wrong.

> **Worked example.** Evaluate $$\displaystyle\iint_R \frac{3x + 2y}{x + 4y}\,dA$$ where $$R$$ is the triangle bounded by $$x = 0$$, $$y = 0$$, and $$3x + 2y = 2$$.
>
> The expressions $$3x + 2y$$ and $$x + 4y$$ appear in the integrand and one boundary line, so let $$u = 3x + 2y$$ and $$v = x + 4y$$. Solving, $$x = \dfrac{2u - v}{5}$$ and $$y = \dfrac{3v - u}{10}$$, and
>
> $$\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)} = \frac25\cdot\frac3{10} - \left(-\frac15\right)\left(-\frac1{10}\right) = \frac1{10}.$$
>
> (Check by the shortcut: $$\partial(u,v)/\partial(x,y) = 3\cdot4 - 2\cdot1 = 10$$.) The boundaries become $$x = 0 \Rightarrow v = 2u$$, $$y = 0 \Rightarrow v = u/3$$, and $$3x + 2y = 2 \Rightarrow u = 2$$. So $$S$$ is the triangle with vertices $$(0,0)$$, $$(2, \tfrac23)$$, $$(2, 4)$$, and
>
> $$\iint_R \frac{3x + 2y}{x + 4y}\,dA = \int_0^2 \int_{u/3}^{2u} \frac{u}{v}\cdot\frac1{10}\,dv\,du = \frac1{10}\int_0^2 u\,\ln 6\,du = \frac{\ln 6}{5} \approx 0.358.$$

### Three variables

For $$x = g(u,v,w)$$, $$y = h(u,v,w)$$, $$z = k(u,v,w)$$ the Jacobian is the $$3\times3$$ determinant

$$\frac{\partial(x,y,z)}{\partial(u,v,w)} = \det\begin{pmatrix} x_u & x_v & x_w \\ y_u & y_v & y_w \\ z_u & z_v & z_w \end{pmatrix},$$

and the theorem reads the same way: $$\displaystyle\iiint_R f\,dV = \iiint_S f(g,h,k)\,\left\lvert \dfrac{\partial(x,y,z)}{\partial(u,v,w)}\right\rvert \,du\,dv\,dw$$.

> **Worked example.** For spherical coordinates, take $$(u,v,w) = (\rho, \theta, \varphi)$$. Expanding along the bottom row $$(\cos\varphi,\ 0,\ -\rho\sin\varphi)$$,
>
> $$\det\begin{pmatrix} \sin\varphi\cos\theta & -\rho\sin\varphi\sin\theta & \rho\cos\varphi\cos\theta \\ \sin\varphi\sin\theta & \rho\sin\varphi\cos\theta & \rho\cos\varphi\sin\theta \\ \cos\varphi & 0 & -\rho\sin\varphi \end{pmatrix} = \cos\varphi\left(-\rho^2\sin\varphi\cos\varphi\right) - \rho\sin\varphi\left(\rho\sin^2\varphi\right) = -\rho^2\sin\varphi.$$
>
> The absolute value, with $$0 \le \varphi \le \pi$$ so that $$\sin\varphi \ge 0$$, gives $$dV = \rho^2\sin\varphi\,d\rho\,d\theta\,d\varphi$$, the factor from §14.5. (The minus sign only reflects the order we listed the variables. In the order $$\rho, \varphi, \theta$$ it comes out positive.)

> **Worked example.** Evaluate $$\displaystyle\iiint_R (x + y + z)\,dV$$ where $$R$$ is the solid bounded by the planes $$x + y = 0$$, $$x + y = 2$$, $$y + z = 0$$, $$y + z = 3$$, $$x + z = 0$$, and $$x + z = 1$$.
>
> Let $$u = x + y$$, $$v = y + z$$, $$w = x + z$$. Then $$R$$ becomes the box $$0 \le u \le 2$$, $$0 \le v \le 3$$, $$0 \le w \le 1$$. Going the easy direction,
>
> $$\frac{\partial(u,v,w)}{\partial(x,y,z)} = \det\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = 2, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \left\lvert \frac{\partial(x,y,z)}{\partial(u,v,w)}\right\rvert  = \frac12.$$
>
> Adding the three equations gives $$x + y + z = \tfrac12(u + v + w)$$, and therefore
>
> $$\iiint_R (x + y + z)\,dV = \int_0^1\!\!\int_0^3\!\!\int_0^2 \frac{u + v + w}{2}\cdot\frac12\,du\,dv\,dw = \frac14\cdot 6\cdot\left(1 + \tfrac32 + \tfrac12\right) = \frac92.$$
>
> The volume of $$R$$ is $$\tfrac12\cdot(2\cdot3\cdot1) = 3$$: the box has volume $$6$$ and the transformation halves it.

### Practice & reflection

1. Why does the change-of-variables formula use $$\left\lvert \partial(x,y)/\partial(u,v)\right\rvert $$ and not just $$\partial(x,y)/\partial(u,v)$$? What would happen to an area or a volume if you left out the absolute value?
2. You are given a double integral over a region bounded by four lines. What clues in the integrand and in the lines tell you what $$u$$ and $$v$$ should be?
3. Why are the two Jacobians $$\partial(x,y)/\partial(u,v)$$ and $$\partial(u,v)/\partial(x,y)$$ reciprocals? (Think about the matrices and what the determinant of an inverse matrix is.)
4. The transformation $$x = u + v^2$$, $$y = v$$ bends straight grid lines into parabolas, yet its Jacobian is $$1$$. What does that tell you about the areas of the image cells?
5. Transforming the boundary of $$R$$ is just as important as computing the Jacobian. What can go wrong if you find the Jacobian correctly but describe $$S$$ incorrectly?
