Polar Rectangles

§14.3 — cut a round region into polar rectangles, see why each has area r Δr Δθ, and watch the polar Riemann sum approach the integral.

The picture cuts the plane into m rings and n wedges. Each piece is a polar rectangle. A piece counts if its center lies in the green region. The polar Riemann sum adds f at each center times the piece’s area, and the exact integral is shown for comparison. Use the two inspect sliders to pick one piece (shown in orange) and compare its true area with Δr Δθ and with r* Δr Δθ.

Here r* is the middle of the ring, and the sample point is the center of each piece. Pieces along the edge of a curved region are not counted exactly, so the sum only closes in on the integral as m and n grow.

Things to try

  1. Pick the disk with m = 4 rings. Predict which ring’s pieces have the biggest area, the innermost or the outermost, and by what factor. Then inspect ring 1 and ring 4 (the wedge number does not matter). Compare the true area with r* Δr Δθ; what do you notice?
  2. Pick the annulus with f = 1 and tick “Leave out the factor r”. Predict the wrong answer (think: what does ∫∫ dr dθ give over 1 ≤ r ≤ 2 and 0 ≤ θ ≤ 2π?). Then check it against the exact area 3π.
  3. Pick the crescent (the region from the section page: inside r = 1/2, outside the cardioid r = 1 − cos θ) with f = 1. Raise m and n to 30 and 48. Does the sum approach 7√3/8 − 5π/12 ≈ 0.2065? Why does it get there slowly?
  4. Pick the disk with f = e−(x²+y²). The section page does this on the unit disk and gets π(1 − 1/e) ≈ 1.986. Predict whether the radius-2 disk gives more or less, and roughly what number the answers level off at as the radius grows. Then read the exact value.
  5. Pick the disk with f = y. Predict the exact integral from symmetry, then change m and n and watch the sum.
