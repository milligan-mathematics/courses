# §13.7: Extreme Values of Functions of Two Variables

In single-variable calculus you find the highest and lowest points of a curve by looking for places where the slope is zero, and then deciding from the bend of the curve whether you have a peak, a valley, or a flat spot that is neither. Functions of two variables ask the same question about a surface. Where is the highest hill? Where is the deepest valley? And there is a new twist: a spot can look like a peak when you walk east and west, and like a valley when you walk north and south.

Those questions matter because so many real problems depend on two inputs: profit from two products, the strength of a beam as a function of two dimensions, the error of a fit as a function of two parameters. If you can find where a surface peaks and bottoms out, you can find the best and worst choices. The good news is that the one-variable method carries over almost word for word. The new idea is the **saddle point**, a place that is a maximum in one direction and a minimum in another, and you need a new test to recognize one.

By the end of this section you should be able to:

1. Distinguish absolute from relative extrema, and explain why a relative extremum at an interior point must be a critical point.
2. Recognize a saddle point, and explain why it is neither a maximum nor a minimum.
3. Use the Second Partial Derivative Test to classify a critical point, and explain what it means when $$D = 0$$.
4. Find the absolute maximum and minimum of a continuous function on a closed, bounded region.

### Highs, lows, and the critical points where they hide

A function $$f(x,y)$$ has a **relative (local) maximum** at $$(a,b)$$ if $$f(a,b) \ge f(x,y)$$ for every point $$(x,y)$$ near $$(a,b)$$ in its domain, and a **relative minimum** if the inequality is reversed. If the inequality holds across the whole domain, the value is an **absolute** maximum or minimum. A function can have many relative extrema, but at most one absolute maximum value and one absolute minimum value.

The key fact matches Calc I: **if $$f$$ has a relative extremum at an interior point $$(a,b)$$ where the first partials exist, then $$(a,b)$$ is a critical point**, meaning $$f_x(a,b) = 0$$ and $$f_y(a,b) = 0$$. Geometrically, the tangent plane is horizontal there. The converse fails. A critical point can be a **saddle point**: a point where $$f$$ takes values both above and below $$f(a,b)$$ in every disk around $$(a,b)$$. Finding critical points gives you a list of candidates, and you still have to decide what each one is. On a contour map, a peak or a valley looks like a small closed loop of level curves, while a saddle looks like an X where the level curves cross.

> **Worked example.** Find and classify the critical points of $$f(x,y) = x^2 - y^2$$.
>
> $$f_x = 2x, \qquad f_y = -2y.$$ Both vanish only at $$(0,0)$$, where $$f(0,0) = 0$$. Along the $$x$$-axis, $$f(x,0) = x^2 \ge 0$$, so nearby values rise above $$0$$. Along the $$y$$-axis, $$f(0,y) = -y^2 \le 0$$, so nearby values fall below $$0$$. Every disk around the origin contains both kinds of values, so $$(0,0)$$ is a **saddle point**. This function has no relative extrema at all.

### The Second Partial Derivative Test

Finding critical points is the easy part. To classify them, look at how the surface bends. In Calc I, the second derivative compares the function with a parabola, and the same idea works here. Near a critical point $$(a,b)$$,

$$f(a+u, b+v) \approx f(a,b) + \tfrac12 f_{xx}(a,b)\,u^2 + f_{xy}(a,b)\,uv + \tfrac12 f_{yy}(a,b)\,v^2,$$

so the second partials describe the bending. The combination

$$D(a,b) = f_{xx}(a,b)\,f_{yy}(a,b) - \bigl[f_{xy}(a,b)\bigr]^2$$

is, up to sign, the discriminant of that quadratic piece, and its sign decides most cases. The test says:

- If $$D > 0$$ and $$f_{xx}(a,b) > 0$$, the point is a relative minimum (a bowl).
- If $$D > 0$$ and $$f_{xx}(a,b) < 0$$, the point is a relative maximum (a cap).
- If $$D < 0$$, the point is a saddle point.
- If $$D = 0$$, the test is **inconclusive**. Anything can happen, and you need another argument.

Calculate $$D$$ first. If it is negative, you are done: the point is a saddle. If it is positive, the sign of $$f_{xx}$$ decides between a maximum and a minimum (and $$f_{yy}$$ must have the same sign, since $$D > 0$$). The common mistake is reading $$D = 0$$ as "no extremum." It does not say that. It says the test has nothing to tell you, and a point with $$D = 0$$ can be a maximum, a minimum, or a saddle.

A quick check: if $$f_{xx}$$ and $$f_{yy}$$ have opposite signs, then $$D < 0$$ no matter what $$f_{xy}$$ is, so the point is a saddle.

> **Worked example.** Classify the critical points of $$f(x,y) = 6y + 3xy - 3x^2y - 2y^2 - xy^2 + x^2y^2$$.
>
> The first partials are $$f_x = 3y - 6xy - y^2 + 2xy^2$$ and $$f_y = 6 + 3x - 3x^2 - 4y - 2xy + 2x^2y$$. Setting both to zero gives five critical points: $$(-1,0)$$, $$(2,0)$$, $$(-1,3)$$, $$(2,3)$$, and $$(\tfrac12, \tfrac32)$$. The second partials are
>
> $$f_{xx} = -2y(3-y), \qquad f_{yy} = 2(x-2)(x+1), \qquad f_{xy} = (3-2y)(1-2x).$$
>
> - At $$(-1,0)$$, $$(2,0)$$, $$(-1,3)$$, and $$(2,3)$$: $$f_{xx} = f_{yy} = 0$$ and $$f_{xy} = \pm 9$$, so $$D = -81 < 0$$. Each is a **saddle point**, and each has $$f = 0$$.
> - At $$(\tfrac12, \tfrac32)$$: $$f_{xx} = f_{yy} = -\tfrac92$$ and $$f_{xy} = 0$$, so $$D = \tfrac{81}{4} > 0$$. Since $$f_{xx} < 0$$, this is a **relative maximum**, with value $$f(\tfrac12, \tfrac32) = \tfrac{81}{16}$$.

### Absolute extrema on closed, bounded regions

The Extreme Value Theorem says that if $$f$$ is continuous on a closed and bounded set $$D$$, then $$f$$ attains an absolute maximum and an absolute minimum somewhere in $$D$$. Those points can lie inside $$D$$ or on its boundary, so the search covers both. In one variable you checked the endpoints of an interval. Here the "endpoints" are the edges and corners of the region, which is why the boundary is usually the longest part of the work. Here is the strategy:

1. **Interior.** Find the critical points inside $$D$$, and evaluate $$f$$ at each one.
2. **Boundary.** Parametrize each piece of the boundary, so $$f$$ becomes a function of one variable. Find its critical points on that piece, and evaluate $$f$$ at the endpoints (the corners).
3. **Compare.** The largest value found is the absolute maximum, and the smallest is the absolute minimum.

> **Worked example.** Find the absolute extrema of $$f(x,y) = x^2y^2 - x^2 - y^2$$ on the square $$-2 \le x \le 2$$, $$-2 \le y \le 2$$.
>
> **Step 1.** $$f_x = 2x(y^2-1)$$ and $$f_y = 2y(x^2-1)$$. The interior critical points are $$(0,0)$$, with $$f = 0$$, and $$(\pm1,\pm1)$$, with $$f = -1$$. At the origin $$D = 4 > 0$$ and $$f_{xx} = -2 < 0$$, so it is a relative maximum. At $$(\pm1,\pm1)$$ we have $$D = -16 < 0$$, so these are saddles.
>
> **Step 2.** On the side $$x = 2$$, $$f(2,y) = 3y^2 - 4$$. Its critical point $$y = 0$$ gives $$f(2,0) = -4$$, and the corners give $$f(2,\pm2) = 8$$. The other three sides behave the same way by symmetry.
>
> **Step 3.** The candidates are $$0$$, $$-1$$, $$-4$$, and $$8$$. The absolute maximum is $$8$$, at the four corners. The absolute minimum is $$-4$$, at the four edge midpoints $$(\pm2,0)$$ and $$(0,\pm2)$$. The relative maximum at the origin is not absolute, because the corners beat its value $$0$$.

### Practice & reflection

1. A critical point has $$f_{xx} > 0$$ and $$f_{yy} < 0$$. Can $$D$$ be positive there? Explain, and say what kind of mistake a positive answer would signal.
2. Explain why the Second Partial Derivative Test only makes sense at a critical point. What would a positive $$D$$ at a point where $$f_x \ne 0$$ actually tell you?
3. Could an interior saddle point be the absolute minimum of $$f$$ on a region $$D$$? Explain using the values of $$f$$ very close to the saddle.
4. On a closed, bounded region, why do you still have to check the boundary when the interior contains a relative maximum?
