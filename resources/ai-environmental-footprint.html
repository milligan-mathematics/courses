AI’s Environmental Footprint, in Perspective

Many thoughtful people hesitate to use AI because of what it costs the planet. That hesitation deserves real numbers. This report gathers the best current evidence on the electricity, water, and emissions behind AI and the data centers it runs in, and sets each one beside other parts of the economy we rarely think twice about.

Prepared by Ian Lundholm for faculty conversations on AI · sources linked throughout · how this was made

The short version

  1. One person’s chatbot use is small. A typical text prompt uses roughly 0.24–0.34 watt-hours, about a second of a microwave or a few seconds of TV. Ten prompts a day for a year adds about one-hundredth of one percent to a typical U.S. household’s electricity.
  2. The build-out is large, and it is fast. Data centers use about 1.5% of the world’s electricity, a share projected to double to about 3% by 2030. In the U.S. it is about 4.7% today and could reach roughly 12% by 2030. Five tech companies now invest more each year than the whole world invests in oil and gas production.
  3. Modest globally, intense locally. Data centers draw about a quarter of the electricity in Virginia and Ireland. The real harms are local: strained grids, water in small or dry systems, higher bills in tight power markets, and air pollution from gas turbines near homes, as in South Memphis.
  4. Emissions are small as a share and growing fast. Data centers produce about 0.5% of energy-related CO₂, well behind cement, steel, aviation, or cars. But big tech companies’ own emissions are rising sharply, and much of the new power is coming from natural gas.
  5. Not all AI is alike. Images, long documents, “reasoning” models, and especially video use tens to thousands of times more energy than a short text answer. Energy per task is falling about tenfold a year, yet the total keeps climbing.

1Why the numbers seem to disagree

Headlines about AI’s footprint often seem to contradict each other: one says a question to a chatbot “drinks a bottle of water,” another says it uses five drops. Usually both are reporting real estimates that measure different things. Five distinctions explain most of the gap.

Per use vs. in total

A single prompt is tiny; billions of prompts plus training plus everything else a data center does is not. Both statements can be true at once.

Measured vs. projected

Today’s figures are estimates from good data. Figures for 2030 are scenarios, and they have swung widely as forecasters revise them.

Water withdrawn vs. consumed

Withdrawn water is borrowed and mostly returned. Consumed water evaporates. And “AI water” may count only cooling at the data center, or also the water used at power plants (often far larger).

Market-based vs. location-based carbon

Companies may subtract the clean energy they buy (market-based). Counting the actual local grid instead (location-based) can make the same data center’s emissions several times larger.

Who did the measuring

The best per-prompt numbers come from the companies themselves and are not independently verified. Outside estimates rely on models of hardware and usage.

2Electricity: small slice, fast growth

Data centers worldwide used about 485 TWh of electricity in 2025, slightly more than 1.5% of global demand. The International Energy Agency (IEA) expects that to roughly double, to about 950 TWh, by 2030, around 3% of the world’s electricity.2

Those totals cover everything data centers do: streaming, email, cloud storage, banking, and AI. AI is the main reason for the growth. In 2024, servers built for AI drew about 15% of data-center electricity, and their consumption is growing around 30% a year. They account for almost half of the increase expected by 2030.1 In 2025, total data-center use grew 17%, and AI-focused data centers grew 50%.2

Putting it beside the rest of the economy

  • Other drivers of new demand are bigger. The IEA finds data centers account for about one-tenth of global electricity demand growth to 2030, “less than the share from industrial motors, air conditioning in homes and offices, or electric vehicles.”1 From 2014 to 2024, space cooling added about 700 TWh of demand worldwide, while data centers added about 250 TWh.1
  • Bitcoin is a useful yardstick. Bitcoin mining alone used about 138 TWh a year in mid-2024, roughly 0.5% of the world’s electricity and about a third as much as every data center combined.8
  • In the U.S., the picture is starker. The IEA projects that by 2030 the country will use more electricity for data centers “than for the production of aluminium, steel, cement, chemicals and all other energy-intensive goods combined,” and that data centers will make up nearly half of U.S. demand growth through 2030.1 Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (LBNL) puts the figure at about a third, alongside a 24% rise in demand from everything else.4
  • The money is enormous. The largest tech companies spent more than $400 billion on capital investment in 2025, with another 75% jump expected in 2026. “Capital expenditure of just five technology companies is now larger than global investment in oil and natural gas production.”2

Treat forecasts with care

Projections are uncertain in both directions. Texas has received requests to connect about 474 gigawatts of new load, about 90% of it data centers. That is more than five times the state’s record peak demand, and many of those projects will never be built.7 LBNL revised its 2024 estimate to the low end of its earlier range because fewer AI chips shipped than expected.4 The IEA notes that shortages of grid equipment, chips and memory are making the most aggressive near-term scenarios less likely.2

3One prompt, compared with everyday life

In 2025 the major AI companies published per-prompt energy figures for the first time. They are much lower than the estimates many people remember.

  • Google: the median Gemini text prompt uses 0.24 Wh, emits 0.03 g CO₂e and consumes 0.26 mL of water (“about five drops”). Google says energy per prompt fell 33-fold in the previous year.9,10 The figure includes idle machines and cooling but not training. It uses market-based carbon accounting and has not been independently verified. Counting the local grid instead gives about 0.08 g CO₂e.
  • OpenAI: the average ChatGPT query uses about 0.34 Wh, according to Sam Altman. He gave no methodology.11
  • Independent estimate: Epoch AI’s model gives roughly 0.3 Wh for a typical GPT-4o answer. Long inputs cost much more: about 2.5 Wh for a 10,000-token input, and 40 Wh for a 100,000-token input (roughly a book chapter versus a whole book).12
  • The old “10× a Google search” figure (about 3 Wh) came from a 2023 estimate that assumed older hardware and long responses. The IEA now says “simple text queries now typically consume less electricity than running a television over the same period of time.”2

What one text prompt is roughly equal to

These comparisons use about 0.3 Wh and 0.03–0.1 g CO₂e per prompt. The carbon range runs from Google’s market-based figure to the same energy on an average grid.

Environmental scientist Hannah Ritchie of Our World in Data comes to a similar conclusion. For a typical person, text chatbots are a negligible part of their footprint. She adds that managing the growth in total demand is a real challenge.24

If prompts are so small, what are the data centers for?

ChatGPT users sent about 2.5 billion prompts a day in mid-2025.23 At 0.34 Wh each, that is about 310 GWh a year, about what 29,000 U.S. homes use and less than a tenth of a percent of global data-center electricity. The IEA runs the same arithmetic for 10 billion queries a day at 1 Wh each and gets under 1% of data-center use. It concludes that “the bulk of planned capacity must be destined for other workloads,” such as training, video and image generation, autonomous agents and enterprise systems. No company has disclosed how its capacity is split among them.2

Training is a one-time cost, but it keeps growing. Training GPT-3 in 2020 used about 1,287 MWh, about what 120 U.S. homes use in a year.17 Epoch AI estimates that a 2025 frontier model, Grok 4, took about 310 GWh, roughly 240 times as much. The power used by frontier training runs is growing about 2.1× a year.18

4Water: a minor national issue, a real local one

Data centers use water in two ways: evaporating it to cool servers on site, and indirectly through the power plants that supply their electricity. Which of these an estimate counts explains most of the disagreement.

Per prompt

  • “A bottle of water per conversation” traces to a 2023 paper by Shaolei Ren and colleagues at UC Riverside.25 As later revised, it estimates that GPT-3 “drinks” a 500 mL bottle for roughly 10–50 medium-length responses, not for each one, depending on location. The estimate includes power-plant water, which makes up most of the total, and assumes 2023 hardware.
  • Google reports 0.26 mL per median Gemini prompt, but counts only on-site cooling. Ren’s group calls that comparison apples to oranges.9,26
  • Mistral’s full life-cycle analysis, which includes hardware manufacturing and electricity, gives 45 mL per 400-token response.13

So a 500 mL bottle corresponds to somewhere between about a dozen and nearly 2,000 prompts, depending on what is counted. For scale, the water footprint of a single quarter-pound beef patty is roughly 1,700 liters, most of it rain falling on grazing land and feed crops.33

Spread across the country, data centers’ on-site and power-plant water comes to about 1.9 gallons per person per day. The average American uses about 82 gallons a day at home.3,32 The trend matters more than the level. Google’s water consumption rose 28% in 2024, to about 8.1 billion gallons, more than double its 2020 figure. Google describes this as “about what it takes to irrigate 54 golf courses annually” in the Southwest.27

Where water really is a problem

National totals hide local strain. In The Dalles, Oregon, Google’s data centers used about a third of the city’s water in 2024. Their usage figures became public only after a 13-month records fight, and city water rates are projected to roughly double by 2036. The mayor disputes that Google is the cause.29 Cooling towers evaporate the most water in hot, dry summers, exactly when water is scarcest.

The industry is responding with air-cooled and closed-loop designs. Google says its sites in Mesa, Arizona and Canelones, Uruguay will use air cooling.27 Microsoft says its new designs use no water for cooling, about 125 million liters a year less per data center, at the cost of somewhat more electricity.28 That trades on-site water for power-plant water and emissions.

5Emissions: compared with other sectors

Electricity use in data centers produces about 180 million tonnes of CO₂ a year, roughly 0.5% of global CO₂ from burning fuel. The IEA projects 300–350 million tonnes by 2035, still less than 1.5% of energy-sector emissions.1,2

Why the small share is not the whole story

  • Company emissions are rising fast. Google’s total emissions rose 18% in 2025, to about 81% above its 2019 baseline.37 Microsoft’s rose 25% in fiscal 2025, partly because it stopped counting certain clean-energy certificates.38 Amazon’s rose 16%, to 80.9 million tonnes.39
  • Accounting choices matter. A 2024 Guardian analysis recalculated Google, Microsoft, Meta and Apple’s in-house data-center emissions using the local grid instead of purchased certificates. Over 2020–22 the results came out about 7.6 times higher than reported.40
  • New gas is being built. The IEA expects renewables to supply over a third of data-center electricity by 2030, but gas-fired generation for data centers also doubles. More than 100 GW of gas capacity is planned specifically to power data centers on site.2 U.S. gas capacity in development rose 50% in the first half of 2026, to 378 GW. Of the 52 GW under construction, 16.9 GW is meant to power data centers directly.41
  • Coal is retiring more slowly. Only 4.6 GW of the 12.3 GW of U.S. coal capacity scheduled to retire in 2025 actually did, after federal emergency orders and amid rising demand.42
  • Clean-energy deals are real too. The tech sector signed about 40% of all corporate renewable-power contracts worldwide in 2025.2 The Three Mile Island Unit 1 reactor (renamed Crane) is slated to restart in 2027 under a 20-year contract with Microsoft.43
  • Hardware has a footprint as well. Large AI models alone could add 1.2–5.0 million tonnes of electronic waste from 2020 to 2030, against about 62 million tonnes of e-waste the world produced in 2022 alone. Reuse and recycling could cut that by up to 86%.44

6Who pays: bills, air, and neighbors

The strongest evidence of harm is local, and it falls on particular communities.

Close to home: the Tennessee Valley

  • TVA’s load is shifting. Data centers were 18% of TVA’s industrial load in 2025, and TVA expects that demand to double in the region by 2030. TVA is building 1.5-GW gas plants at Cumberland and Kingston as coal units there retire.45 In August 2026 its board created a separate, higher rate for data centers, reported as about 10%, so that households would not carry the cost.46
  • Memphis. xAI’s Colossus supercomputer began by running more than 400 MW of on-site gas turbines until a grid connection arrived.2 Flyovers counted as many as 35 turbines in South Memphis while xAI sought a permit for 15. The Southern Environmental Law Center, representing the NAACP and a local group, has appealed the 15-turbine permit the county granted in July 2025.47,48
  • Southaven, Mississippi (just across the state line). The NAACP and environmental groups sued xAI in April 2026 under the Clean Air Act over turbines powering its second site. On 19 October 2026 the court denied a bid to dismiss the case.49

Health effects

Researchers at UC Riverside and Caltech estimate that air pollution from power generation for U.S. data centers could carry public-health costs of more than $20 billion in 2028. In the most affected counties, the burden per household is about seven times the national average.50 These are model projections, but they point to a cost that national carbon totals leave out.

Electricity bills: it depends where you live

  • Where power is tight, bills rise. In PJM, the grid serving 13 mid-Atlantic and Midwest states, the independent market monitor attributes about $29 billion of $64 billion in capacity costs over four auctions to data-center demand.51 Virginia’s legislative auditors estimated that a typical Dominion household’s generation and transmission costs could rise $14–$37 a month by 2040, depending on how fast demand grows.52
  • Where there is spare capacity, they can fall. Studies by Berkeley Lab and Brattle found that the states adding the most load saw average real prices decline. An EPRI analysis estimates prices would be about 6% higher without data centers. Analysts note that those declines came where there was spare capacity, which tight markets like PJM no longer have.53
  • Contracts matter. Harvard’s Electricity Law Initiative reviewed about 50 regulatory proceedings. It found that confidential special contracts between utilities and tech companies can shift costs to ordinary customers.54

7Can AI help pay its own way?

The IEA estimates that widespread use of AI applications that already exist could cut about 1,400 million tonnes of CO₂ a year by 2035, by making industry, transport and the power grid more efficient. That is several times the emissions of data centers themselves. The IEA is blunt about the caveats: these gains are “far smaller than what is needed to address climate change,” barriers to adoption are significant, and rebound effects, such as autonomous cars pulling riders off public transit, “could undercut some of these benefits.” Its verdict: “AI can be a tool in reducing emissions, but it is not a silver bullet.”1

Other estimates run higher: a study from the London School of Economics’ Grantham Institute and Systemiq puts the figure at 3.2–5.4 billion tonnes a year by 2035 in power, food and transport.55 There are concrete examples too. Google DeepMind’s GraphCast beat the leading conventional weather model on 90% of 1,380 test measures, and it produces a 10-day forecast in under a minute.56 Still, these are potential savings set against emissions that are already happening, and there is no guarantee the potential is realized.

8What is real, and what is overstated

Concerns the evidence supports

  • The speed and concentration of growth, which strains local grids (Virginia, Ireland, Texas).
  • New fossil-fuel infrastructure: gas plants and on-site turbines built for data centers, and coal retiring more slowly.
  • Local air pollution where turbines run near homes, as in South Memphis and Southaven.
  • Water stress in small or dry water systems, such as The Dalles.
  • Costs shifted onto households in tight power markets, and opaque special contracts.
  • Rising corporate emissions and thin disclosure, especially for the heavy uses (video, reasoning, agents) driving the build-out.

Claims that are often overstated

  • “Every ChatGPT question uses a bottle of water.” The original estimate was one bottle per 10–50 responses, mostly water at power plants, on 2023 hardware.
  • “AI is a major share of global emissions.” Data centers of every kind produce about 0.5% of energy-related CO₂.
  • “My chatbot use is a meaningful part of my footprint.” Ten prompts a day is about 0.01% of household electricity; one burger equals tens of thousands of prompts.
  • “A prompt uses ten times a Google search.” Current disclosures (0.24–0.34 Wh) are about the same as the oft-quoted 2009 search figure.
  • “Data centers are why everyone’s bill is rising.” The effect is real in some markets and absent in others.

The two columns fit together. Individual use of text chatbots is not where the environmental cost lies. That cost lies in how fast the industry is building, where it builds, what powers it, and who has a say. Those are questions for companies, utilities, regulators and communities more than for individual users. That is also why public conversation, including ours, matters.

9Questions for conversation

  1. Where does responsibility sit? If one person’s chatbot use is tiny but the industry’s build-out is large, how should responsibility be shared among users, companies, utilities and regulators?
  2. What makes energy use worthwhile? We accept footprints for things we value: travel to conferences, air conditioning, streaming, a hamburger. What standard would we apply to AI in teaching and scholarship?
  3. Compared with what? Is the right comparison AI versus no AI, AI versus a person doing the work, or AI versus some other tool? The answer changes the calculation.
  4. Efficiency and appetite. Energy per task has fallen roughly tenfold a year, yet total use keeps rising. Is that a failure, or simply what happens when something becomes useful?
  5. Our neighbors. The heaviest costs fall on particular places, such as South Memphis or a small Oregon town. What do care for creation and love of neighbor ask of the institutions and individuals who benefit?
  6. What should we ask for? Before our campus adopts AI tools, what would we want vendors to disclose, and which uses of AI deserve the most scrutiny?

10How this report was made

This report was researched and drafted with AI assistance (Claude, by Anthropic). Separate research passes covered electricity, per-use footprints, water, and emissions and local impacts. Figures were checked against the text of the original reports wherever it could be accessed, and key IEA quotations were checked against the full reports. A few items rely on news coverage of documents that could not be read directly; they are cited to that coverage. The work was directed and reviewed by Ian Lundholm.

This field moves quickly: several figures here were revised within the past year, and per-prompt energy is falling fast. If you spot an error or a better source, please say so.
