How these work. Each walk-through takes one problem through Pólya’s four phases. You answer one step at a time and get feedback right away; when something isn’t right, read why and try again. Hints are there when you ask for them. Your place is saved in this browser, so you can stop and come back.

Something new: what a proof may use. Today the completeness statements are proved from one another. In 9.4.12 and 9.4.15 some steps ask what you are allowed to assume. Some wrong choices quote the very statement being proved, and some quote a result that was itself proved from it. The page will tell you which is which.

The walk-throughs end in full proofs, so the proof you bring to class should still be your own, in your own words.

Pólya walk-through · Problem 9.4.11

Bounded, rational, and no rational limit

Problem 9.4.11. Find a bounded sequence of rational numbers such that no subsequence of it converges to a rational number.

Goal: Find a bounded sequence of rationals whose subsequences all converge to an irrational number, and prove that no subsequence has a rational limit. This is Bolzano–Weierstrass (Theorem 9.3.6) failing inside \(\mathbb Q\): the limit exists in \(\mathbb R\), but nothing makes it rational.

Pólya walk-through · Problem 9.4.12

The LUBP proves the NIP

Problem 9.4.12. Use the Least Upper Bound Property to prove the Nested Interval Property. That is, assume that every non-empty subset of the real numbers which is bounded above has a least upper bound; and suppose that we have two sequences of real numbers \((x_n)\) and \((y_n)\), satisfying:
1. \(x_1\le x_2 \le x_3 \le \ldots\)
2. \(y_1\ge y_2 \ge y_3 \ge \ldots\)
3. \(\forall\ n, x_n\le y_n\)
4. \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(y_n-x_n\right) = 0\).
Prove that there exists a real number \(c\) such that \(x_n\le c\le y_n\), for all \(n\). (Again, the \(c\) will, of necessity, be unique, but don’t worry about that.)

Goal: Build \(c\) from the LUBP, as the supremum of the left ends, and show it sits between the two sequences. The proof may not use the NIP, which is what is being proved, or anything proved from it. Problem 9.1.2 gives \(x_n \le y_m\) for all \(n, m\) from properties 1–3 alone. Property 4 is not needed to find \(c\).

Pólya walk-through · Problem 9.4.13

Rationals with an irrational supremum

Problem 9.4.13. Since the LUBP is equivalent to the NIP it does not hold for the rational number system. Demonstrate this by finding a non-empty set of rational numbers which is bounded above, but whose supremum is an irrational number.

Goal: Find a non-empty set \(S \subseteq \mathbb Q\), bounded above, whose supremum in \(\mathbb R\) is irrational. Then show that no rational number is a least upper bound of \(S\), which is the failure of the LUBP in \(\mathbb Q\). The supremum is found straight from Definition 9.4.2. The facts used are that \(\sqrt2\) exists in \(\mathbb R\) (Theorem 9.1.6), that it is irrational (Day 3), and density (Theorem 2.0.11).

Pólya walk-through · Problem 9.4.15

The Archimedean Property, from the supremum

Problem 9.4.15. Prove the Archimedean Property using the supremum.
Theorem 9.4.14 (Archimedean Property of \(\mathbb R\)). Given any positive real numbers \(a\) and \(b\), there exists a positive integer \(n\), such that \(na > b\).

Goal: Prove that the Archimedean Property follows from the LUBP, so it stops being an assumed principle. The proof may not use the Archimedean Principle (Principle 2.0.12) itself, or anything proved from it, such as density (Theorem 2.0.11). Day 4 accepted this property as obviously true, and Chapter 2 used it to prove density. Here it becomes a theorem.

Pólya walk-through · Problem 9.4.16

Is Q Archimedean?

Problem 9.4.16. Does \(\mathbb Q\) satisfy the Archimedean Property and what does this have to do with the question of taking the Archimedean Property as an axiom of completeness?

Goal: Decide whether \(\mathbb Q\) is Archimedean, using the rationals’ own arithmetic, then say what the answer means for taking the Archimedean Property as the completeness axiom. Problem 9.4.15 shows completeness implies the Archimedean Property. This problem shows the converse fails, so the Archimedean Property is strictly weaker.
