# §14.3: Double Integrals in Polar Coordinates

Try to integrate over a disk in Cartesian coordinates and you get limits like $$\pm\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}$$ and square roots that refuse to simplify. The disk is not a rectangle in $$x$$ and $$y$$, but it *is* a rectangle in polar coordinates: $$0 \le r \le a$$ and $$0 \le \theta \le 2\pi$$. When a region is round, or the integrand contains $$x^2 + y^2$$, switching to polar coordinates can turn an ugly integral into an easy one.

There is one new idea to learn, and one mistake to avoid. The new idea is that a small polar rectangle is not a small rectangle: it is bent, and its area depends on how far it is from the origin.

By the end of this section you should be able to:

1. Convert a region and an integrand to polar coordinates.
2. Explain why $$dA = r\,dr\,d\theta$$.
3. Set up and evaluate a double integral over a polar region, including regions between two polar curves.
4. Convert an iterated integral in $$x$$ and $$y$$ to polar form.
5. Decide whether Cartesian or polar coordinates are the better choice.

### Polar coordinates and the area element

Recall $$x = r\cos\theta$$, $$y = r\sin\theta$$, and $$x^2 + y^2 = r^2$$. Cut a region into **polar rectangles**: pieces where $$r$$ runs from $$r$$ to $$r + \Delta r$$ and $$\theta$$ from $$\theta$$ to $$\theta + \Delta\theta$$. One side of such a piece is a radial segment of length $$\Delta r$$. The other is an arc of a circle of radius $$r$$, and its length is $$r\,\Delta\theta$$. So

$$\Delta A \approx (\Delta r)(r\,\Delta\theta) = r\,\Delta r\,\Delta\theta.$$

The factor $$r$$ is there because the arc is longer the farther out you are. Taking the limit gives $$dA = r\,dr\,d\theta$$, which is also what the Jacobian determinant of $$(r,\theta) \mapsto (x,y)$$ gives:

$$\det\begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -r\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & r\cos\theta \end{pmatrix} = r\cos^2\theta + r\sin^2\theta = r.$$

**Theorem.** If $$R = \{(r,\theta) : a \le \theta \le b,\ g_1(\theta) \le r \le g_2(\theta)\}$$, then

$$\iint_R f(x,y)\,dA = \int_a^b \int_{g_1(\theta)}^{g_2(\theta)} f(r\cos\theta,\, r\sin\theta)\;r\,dr\,d\theta.$$

The common mistake: forgetting the $$r$$. Without it, every polar rectangle is counted as if it had the same area as the ones nearest the origin, and the answer is wrong. A quick test is $$f = 1$$ on the unit disk: with the $$r$$ you get $$\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^1 r\,dr\,d\theta = \pi$$, the area of the disk; without it you get $$\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^1 dr\,d\theta = 2\pi$$.

To set up a polar integral: sketch $$R$$; replace $$x$$, $$y$$, and $$x^2 + y^2$$ in the integrand; find the smallest and largest $$\theta$$ that reach $$R$$; for a fixed $$\theta$$ find where a ray from the origin enters ($$g_1$$) and leaves ($$g_2$$) $$R$$; and write $$r\,dr\,d\theta$$.

### Polar regions

> **Worked example.** Find the area of the region inside the circle $$r = \tfrac12$$ and outside the cardioid $$r = 1 - \cos\theta$$.
>
> The curves meet where $$1 - \cos\theta = \tfrac12$$, so $$\theta = \pm\tfrac{\pi}{3}$$. For $$\lvert \theta\rvert  < \tfrac\pi3$$ the cardioid is *inside* the circle, so along each ray $$r$$ runs from the cardioid out to the circle:
>
> $$A = \int_{-\pi/3}^{\pi/3} \int_{1-\cos\theta}^{1/2} r\,dr\,d\theta = \int_{-\pi/3}^{\pi/3} \tfrac12\left(-\tfrac54 + 2\cos\theta - \tfrac12\cos 2\theta\right) d\theta = \frac{7\sqrt3}{8} - \frac{5\pi}{12} \approx 0.2065.$$
>
> (The inner integral uses $$\cos^2\theta = \tfrac12(1 + \cos 2\theta)$$.) A thin crescent, smaller than the whole circle of area $$\pi/4 \approx 0.785$$, as it should be.

> **Worked example.** An annulus with inner radius $$a_1$$ and outer radius $$a_2$$ has constant density $$\rho$$ and mass $$M$$. Find its moment of inertia about the origin.
>
> In polar coordinates $$x^2 + y^2 = r^2$$ and the region is $$a_1 \le r \le a_2$$, $$0 \le \theta \le 2\pi$$:
>
> $$I_0 = \rho \int_0^{2\pi} \int_{a_1}^{a_2} r^2 \cdot r\,dr\,d\theta = \rho \cdot 2\pi \cdot \frac{a_2^4 - a_1^4}{4} = \frac{\pi\rho}{2}(a_2^4 - a_1^4) = \tfrac12 M(a_1^2 + a_2^2),$$
>
> using $$M = \pi\rho(a_2^2 - a_1^2)$$. It is the same answer that superposition gave in §14.2, and this way needs no trick.

### Converting an integral

> **Worked example.** Evaluate $$\displaystyle\iint_R e^{-(x^2+y^2)}\,dA$$ where $$R$$ is the unit disk.
>
> The integrand has no elementary antiderivative in $$x$$ or in $$y$$, but in polar form it is $$e^{-r^2}$$ and the disk is $$0 \le r \le 1$$, $$0 \le \theta \le 2\pi$$:
>
> $$\int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^1 e^{-r^2}\,r\,dr\,d\theta = 2\pi \cdot \tfrac12\left(1 - e^{-1}\right) = \pi\left(1 - \frac1e\right) \approx 1.986.$$
>
> The inner integral is the substitution $$u = -r^2$$, which works *because* of the extra $$r$$ from $$dA$$.

> **Worked example.** Evaluate $$\displaystyle\int_0^1 \int_{\sqrt{x - x^2}}^{\sqrt{1 - x^2}} (x^2 + y^2)^{3/2}\,dy\,dx$$.
>
> The integrand is $$r^3$$. Read the region from the limits. The upper curve $$y = \sqrt{1-x^2}$$ is the unit circle $$r = 1$$. The lower curve $$y = \sqrt{x - x^2}$$ squares to $$x^2 - x + y^2 = 0$$, the circle $$(x - \tfrac12)^2 + y^2 = \tfrac14$$, and in polar form $$r^2 = r\cos\theta$$, so $$r = \cos\theta$$. The region is in the first quadrant, so $$0 \le \theta \le \tfrac\pi2$$ and $$r$$ runs from $$\cos\theta$$ to $$1$$:
>
> $$\int_0^{\pi/2} \int_{\cos\theta}^{1} r^3 \cdot r\,dr\,d\theta = \frac15 \int_0^{\pi/2} \left(1 - \cos^5\theta\right) d\theta = \frac15\left(\frac\pi2 - \frac{8}{15}\right) = \frac{\pi}{10} - \frac{8}{75}.$$

### Volume, and when not to use polar

> **Worked example.** Find the volume of the solid above the cone $$z = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$$ and below the paraboloid $$z = 2 - x^2 - y^2$$.
>
> In polar form the cone is $$z = r$$ and the paraboloid is $$z = 2 - r^2$$. They meet where $$2 - r^2 = r$$, which gives $$r = 1$$. Inside that circle the paraboloid is on top, so the base is the unit disk and the height is $$(2 - r^2) - r$$:
>
> $$V = \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^1 (2 - r^2 - r)\,r\,dr\,d\theta = 2\pi\left(1 - \tfrac14 - \tfrac13\right) = \frac{5\pi}{6}.$$

Polar coordinates are not always better. The integrand can invite them while the *region* does not.

> **Worked example.** Evaluate $$\displaystyle\iint_R (x^2 + y^2)\,dA$$ over the rectangle $$R = [0,2] \times [0,1]$$.
>
> The integrand looks polar, but a rectangle is not. In Cartesian coordinates it is quick:
>
> $$\int_0^2 \int_0^1 (x^2 + y^2)\,dy\,dx = \int_0^2 \left(x^2 + \tfrac13\right) dx = \frac{10}{3}.$$
>
> In polar coordinates the far corner $$(2,1)$$ is at angle $$\arctan\tfrac12$$, so you must split $$R$$ in two: for $$0 \le \theta \le \arctan\tfrac12$$ the ray leaves through the side $$x = 2$$ ($$r$$ up to $$2\sec\theta$$), and for $$\arctan\tfrac12 \le \theta \le \tfrac\pi2$$ it leaves through the top $$y = 1$$ ($$r$$ up to $$\csc\theta$$). It gives the same $$\tfrac{10}{3}$$, with far more work. Always sketch the region before choosing.

### Practice & reflection

1. Describe two situations, one about the region and one about the integrand, where polar coordinates are clearly the better choice.
2. In your own words: why is the area element $$r\,dr\,d\theta$$ and not $$dr\,d\theta$$? What happens to an answer if you forget the $$r$$?
3. You want $$\iint_R \sin\left(\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\right) dA$$ over the region between the circle $$x^2 + y^2 = 9$$ and the square with vertices $$(\pm1, \pm1)$$. Would you use Cartesian or polar coordinates? Why?
4. Sketch the region $$0 \le \theta \le \tfrac\pi2$$, $$0 \le r \le \sin 2\theta$$. Could you describe it with $$\theta$$ as the inner variable? What would the limits look like?
