# §12.3: The Unit Normal and Binormal Vectors, Curvature, and Torsion

The unit tangent vector $$\mathbf{T}$$ tells you which way you are heading, but nothing about how the road bends. A highway cloverleaf, a roller-coaster loop, and a coil of wire all have tangent vectors, and the tangent vector alone cannot tell them apart. To describe the *shape* of a space curve, we need two more directions and two numbers: a vector that points into the bend, a vector that is perpendicular to both, one number for how sharply the curve turns, and one for how much it twists out of its plane.

These are the tools engineers use for track and road design, for the motion of satellites, and for the forces on a rider in a turn. The last part of the section turns them into something you can feel: the push you experience when you speed up, and the push you experience when you turn.

By the end of this section you should be able to:

1. Find the unit tangent, unit normal, and unit binormal vectors $$\mathbf{T}$$, $$\mathbf{N}$$, $$\mathbf{B}$$ of a space curve.
2. Write equations for the osculating, normal, and rectifying planes at a point.
3. Compute curvature $$\kappa$$ and torsion $$\tau$$ with the cross-product formulas.
4. Split acceleration into tangential and normal parts, $$a_T$$ and $$a_N$$.
5. State the three Frenet–Serret formulas and say what each one means.

### The TNB frame

Because $$\mathbf{T}$$ always has length $$1$$, it can change only its direction, never its length, and a vector that only turns has a derivative perpendicular to itself: $$\mathbf{T} \cdot \mathbf{T}' = 0$$. That perpendicular direction is the direction the curve is bending toward. Define

$$\mathbf{N}(t) = \frac{\mathbf{T}'(t)}{\lVert \mathbf{T}'(t) \rVert}, \qquad \mathbf{B}(t) = \mathbf{T}(t) \times \mathbf{N}(t).$$

$$\mathbf{N}$$ is the **unit normal** (needs $$\mathbf{T}' \ne \mathbf{0}$$) and $$\mathbf{B}$$ is the **unit binormal**. At every point $$\mathbf{T}, \mathbf{N}, \mathbf{B}$$ are three mutually perpendicular unit vectors that form a right-handed system, a little coordinate frame that travels along the curve.

> **Worked example.** Find $$\mathbf{T}$$, $$\mathbf{N}$$, $$\mathbf{B}$$ for the helix $$\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle \cos t, \sin t, t \rangle$$.
>
> From §12.1, $$\mathbf{r}'(t) = \langle -\sin t, \cos t, 1 \rangle$$ with speed $$\sqrt2$$, so $$\mathbf{T}(t) = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\langle -\sin t, \cos t, 1 \rangle$$. Then $$\mathbf{T}'(t) = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\langle -\cos t, -\sin t, 0 \rangle$$, with length $$\dfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$$, so
>
> $$\mathbf{N}(t) = \langle -\cos t, -\sin t, 0 \rangle.$$
>
> (It points horizontally, straight in toward the axis of the helix.) Finally $$\mathbf{B} = \mathbf{T} \times \mathbf{N}$$ is
>
> $$\mathbf{B}(t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\langle \sin t, -\cos t, 1 \rangle.$$

### Three planes

At a point of the curve, any two of the three frame vectors span a plane, and each has a name:

- the **osculating plane** is spanned by $$\mathbf{T}$$ and $$\mathbf{N}$$; its normal vector is $$\mathbf{B}$$. ("Osculate" means "to kiss": this plane is the flat sheet that fits the curve best.)
- the **normal plane** is spanned by $$\mathbf{N}$$ and $$\mathbf{B}$$; its normal vector is $$\mathbf{T}$$.
- the **rectifying plane** is spanned by $$\mathbf{B}$$ and $$\mathbf{T}$$; its normal vector is $$\mathbf{N}$$.

Each is a plane through a point with a known normal vector, so $$(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{r}(t_0)) \cdot \mathbf{n} = 0$$ is all you need.

> **Worked example.** Write the three planes for the helix above at $$t = \pi$$.
>
> At $$t = \pi$$: $$\mathbf{r} = \langle -1, 0, \pi \rangle$$, $$\mathbf{T} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\langle 0, -1, 1 \rangle$$, $$\mathbf{N} = \langle 1, 0, 0 \rangle$$, $$\mathbf{B} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\langle 0, 1, 1 \rangle$$. Scaling the normals to remove the $$\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$$:
>
> - osculating (normal $$\langle 0, 1, 1 \rangle$$): $$\;y + z - \pi = 0$$
> - normal (normal $$\langle 0, -1, 1 \rangle$$): $$\;-y + z - \pi = 0$$
> - rectifying (normal $$\langle 1, 0, 0 \rangle$$): $$\;x + 1 = 0$$

### Curvature and torsion

**Curvature** measures how fast the direction of travel turns, per unit of distance traveled:

$$\kappa = \left\lVert \frac{d\mathbf{T}}{ds} \right\rVert = \frac{\lVert \mathbf{T}'(t) \rVert}{\lVert \mathbf{r}'(t) \rVert} = \frac{\lVert \mathbf{r}'(t) \times \mathbf{r}''(t) \rVert}{\lVert \mathbf{r}'(t) \rVert^3}.$$

The last form needs only derivatives of $$\mathbf{r}$$, which is why it is the one you will use. A line has $$\kappa = 0$$. A circle of radius $$a$$ has $$\kappa = 1/a$$: for $$\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle a\cos t, a\sin t, 0 \rangle$$ we get $$\mathbf{T}' = \langle -\cos t, -\sin t, 0 \rangle$$ with length $$1$$, and $$\lVert \mathbf{r}' \rVert = a$$, so $$\kappa = 1/a$$. A tighter circle bends more.

**Torsion** measures how fast the curve twists out of its osculating plane, which is how fast $$\mathbf{B}$$ turns: $$\tau = -\dfrac{d\mathbf{B}}{ds} \cdot \mathbf{N}$$. In practice

$$\tau = \frac{(\mathbf{r}' \times \mathbf{r}'') \cdot \mathbf{r}'''}{\lVert \mathbf{r}' \times \mathbf{r}'' \rVert^2}.$$

A flat curve lies in one plane, so it never twists out of it.

The common mistake: the cross-product formula for $$\kappa$$ has $$\lVert \mathbf{r}' \rVert^3$$ in the denominator, not $$\lVert \mathbf{r}' \rVert$$. A circle of radius $$a$$ is a good test of any answer you get: it must come out $$1/a$$.

> **Worked example.** Find $$\kappa$$ and $$\tau$$ for the twisted cubic $$\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle t, t^2, t^3 \rangle$$, in general and at $$t = 0$$.
>
> The derivatives are $$\mathbf{r}' = \langle 1, 2t, 3t^2 \rangle$$, $$\mathbf{r}'' = \langle 0, 2, 6t \rangle$$, $$\mathbf{r}''' = \langle 0, 0, 6 \rangle$$. Then
>
> $$\mathbf{r}' \times \mathbf{r}'' = \langle 6t^2,\ -6t,\ 2 \rangle, \qquad (\mathbf{r}' \times \mathbf{r}'') \cdot \mathbf{r}''' = 12.$$
>
> So
>
> $$\kappa(t) = \frac{\sqrt{36t^4 + 36t^2 + 4}}{(1 + 4t^2 + 9t^4)^{3/2}}, \qquad \tau(t) = \frac{12}{36t^4 + 36t^2 + 4} = \frac{3}{9t^4 + 9t^2 + 1}.$$
>
> At $$t = 0$$: $$\kappa = 2$$ and $$\tau = 3$$. At $$t = 1$$: $$\kappa = \dfrac{\sqrt{76}}{14^{3/2}} \approx 0.166$$ and $$\tau = \dfrac{3}{19} \approx 0.158$$. The curve straightens out as you move away from the origin.

The three formulas that describe how the frame moves, with $$s$$ the arc length, are the **Frenet–Serret formulas**:

$$\frac{d\mathbf{T}}{ds} = \kappa\,\mathbf{N}, \qquad \frac{d\mathbf{N}}{ds} = -\kappa\,\mathbf{T} + \tau\,\mathbf{B}, \qquad \frac{d\mathbf{B}}{ds} = -\tau\,\mathbf{N}.$$

$$\mathbf{T}$$ turns toward $$\mathbf{N}$$ at rate $$\kappa$$; $$\mathbf{B}$$ turns toward $$-\mathbf{N}$$ at rate $$\tau$$; and $$\mathbf{N}$$ has to do both so that the frame stays perpendicular.

### Tangential and normal acceleration

Because $$\mathbf{v} = \lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert\,\mathbf{T}$$, the acceleration has two parts and no $$\mathbf{B}$$ part:

$$\mathbf{a} = a_T\,\mathbf{T} + a_N\,\mathbf{N}, \qquad a_T = \frac{\mathbf{r}' \cdot \mathbf{r}''}{\lVert \mathbf{r}' \rVert} = \frac{d}{dt}\lVert \mathbf{r}' \rVert, \qquad a_N = \kappa\,\lVert \mathbf{r}' \rVert^2 = \frac{\lVert \mathbf{r}' \times \mathbf{r}'' \rVert}{\lVert \mathbf{r}' \rVert}.$$

$$a_T$$ is the part of the acceleration that changes your speed, and $$a_N$$ is the part that changes your direction. The two are tied together by $$\lVert \mathbf{a} \rVert^2 = a_T^2 + a_N^2$$, which is often the quickest way to get $$a_N$$.

> **Worked example.** Find $$a_T$$ and $$a_N$$ for the spiral $$\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle t\cos t,\ t\sin t,\ 0 \rangle$$.
>
> $$\mathbf{r}' = \langle \cos t - t\sin t,\ \sin t + t\cos t,\ 0 \rangle$$ and $$\mathbf{r}'' = \langle -2\sin t - t\cos t,\ 2\cos t - t\sin t,\ 0 \rangle$$. Expanding, $$\lVert \mathbf{r}' \rVert^2 = 1 + t^2$$ and $$\lVert \mathbf{r}'' \rVert^2 = 4 + t^2$$. So
>
> $$a_T = \frac{d}{dt}\sqrt{1 + t^2} = \frac{t}{\sqrt{1 + t^2}}.$$
>
> Then
>
> $$a_N^2 = \lVert \mathbf{a} \rVert^2 - a_T^2 = 4 + t^2 - \frac{t^2}{1 + t^2} = \frac{(2 + t^2)^2}{1 + t^2},$$
>
> since $$(4 + t^2)(1 + t^2) - t^2 = 4 + 4t^2 + t^4$$. Therefore
>
> $$a_N = \frac{2 + t^2}{\sqrt{1 + t^2}}.$$
>
> Check at $$t = 1$$: $$a_T = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$$, $$a_N = \tfrac{3}{\sqrt2}$$, and $$a_T^2 + a_N^2 = \tfrac12 + \tfrac92 = 5 = \lVert \mathbf{a} \rVert^2$$.

### Practice & reflection

1. A roller-coaster car crests the top of a hill and is slowing down. Which way does $$\mathbf{N}$$ point there (up or down)? Is $$a_T$$ positive or negative?
2. Name the three planes at a point of a curve. Which one contains the "bend" of the curve, and which one would you use to slice the curve to see a cross-section perpendicular to the direction of motion? Give the normal vector of each.
3. Look at $$d\mathbf{B}/ds = -\tau\mathbf{N}$$. If a curve has $$\tau = 0$$ everywhere, what does that say about how $$\mathbf{B}$$ changes along the curve?
4. Describe the motion of a particle whose $$a_T = 0$$ but $$a_N \ne 0$$. What about $$a_N = 0$$ but $$a_T \ne 0$$?
