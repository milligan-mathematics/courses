Walk-through · Part A

Rows and columns

Part A. Do Part A on paper first, then check in here.
Machine learning stores data as a matrix \(X\): one row per example, one column per measurement.\[\begin{array}{c|ccc} \text{car} & \text{weight} & \text{hp} & \text{mpg}\\[-2pt] & \scriptstyle (1000\text{ lb}) & & \\ \hline \text{A} & 2.0 & 75 & 33\\ \text{B} & 2.5 & 85 & 27\\ \text{C} & 3.5 & 115 & 21\\ \text{D} & 4.0 & 125 & 15 \end{array}\](hp is horsepower.)
1. Write the data as a \(4\times 3\) matrix \(X\). What is the entry \(x_{2,3}\), and what does it mean in words?
2. Car B, as a vector, is row 2 of \(X\). Write it down. In MML’s language it is one example \(\mathbf{x}_n \in \mathbb{R}^3\).
3. Column 1 is the weight of every car. Which is more natural for asking “which car is heaviest?”: a row or a column?

Goal: Read a data matrix the way machine learning does: a row is one car, a column is one measurement. Everything today is done to this \(X\): Part B centers it, Part C standardizes it, and Part D compares its columns. On Day 6 the same rows-are-examples idea builds the matrix \(A\) for fitting a line.

Walk-through · Part B

Center

Part B. Do Part B on paper first, then check in here.\[\begin{array}{c|ccc} \text{car} & \text{weight} & \text{hp} & \text{mpg}\\[-2pt] & \scriptstyle (1000\text{ lb}) & & \\ \hline \text{A} & 2.0 & 75 & 33\\ \text{B} & 2.5 & 85 & 27\\ \text{C} & 3.5 & 115 & 21\\ \text{D} & 4.0 & 125 & 15 \end{array}\](hp is horsepower.)
1. Average each column to get the mean vector \(\boldsymbol\mu = (\bar w,\ \bar h,\ \bar y)\).
2. Subtract \(\boldsymbol\mu\) from every row to get the centered matrix \(X_c\). (Each entry now says how far a car is above or below average.)
3. Add up each column of \(X_c\). What do you get? Explain why this always happens, for any data.

Goal: Check your mean vector and centered matrix, and see why a centered column always adds up to 0. Centering moves the origin to the average car. Part C rescales the centered columns, and Part D dots them with each other, which only makes sense once they are centered.

Walk-through · Part C

Standardize

Part C. Do Part C on paper first, then check in here.\[\begin{array}{c|ccc} \text{car} & \text{weight} & \text{hp} & \text{mpg}\\[-2pt] & \scriptstyle (1000\text{ lb}) & & \\ \hline \text{A} & 2.0 & 75 & 33\\ \text{B} & 2.5 & 85 & 27\\ \text{C} & 3.5 & 115 & 21\\ \text{D} & 4.0 & 125 & 15 \end{array}\](hp is horsepower.)
The standard deviation of a column is \(\displaystyle s=\sqrt{\tfrac1n\sum(\text{centered values})^2}\), a typical distance from average.
1. Compute \(s\) for the weight column by hand. (The others are \(s_h \approx 20.6\) and \(s_y \approx 6.71\).)
2. Divide each centered column by its \(s\) to get z-scores. Do the weight column. What is car A’s weight z-score, and what does it mean in words?
3. Distance between cars A and B: with the raw numbers, the difference vector is \((0.5,\ 10,\ -6)\). Find its length. Which measurement dominates? Is a 10-horsepower gap really “20 times bigger” than a 500-pound gap?
4. With z-scores the difference is about \((0.63,\ 0.49,\ -0.89)\). Find its length. Which measurement matters most now?
Skeptic. Standardizing threw away the units. Did we lose any information about the cars? What would you need to get the original numbers back?

Goal: Check your standard deviation and z-scores, and see how standardizing changes which measurement “counts.” Part B centered each column. Here each centered column is divided by its own typical size, so every column is measured in the same currency: standard deviations. Part D shows why that matters for comparing columns.

Walk-through · Part D

Compare columns with a dot product

Part D. Do Part D on paper first, then check in here.\[\begin{array}{c|ccc} \text{car} & \text{weight} & \text{hp} & \text{mpg}\\[-2pt] & \scriptstyle (1000\text{ lb}) & & \\ \hline \text{A} & 2.0 & 75 & 33\\ \text{B} & 2.5 & 85 & 27\\ \text{C} & 3.5 & 115 & 21\\ \text{D} & 4.0 & 125 & 15 \end{array}\](hp is horsepower.)
1. Dot the centered weight column with the centered mpg column. Is the result positive or negative?
2. Do the same for weight with horsepower, and horsepower with mpg.
3. Look at the table. Explain why the sign of a centered dot product tells you whether two measurements rise together or move opposite. (Hint: when is each product in the sum positive?)
4. The sizes of these dot products depend on units. Guess what we might do to the columns so that the dot products can be compared fairly. (The notebook shows you.)

Goal: Check your three dot products, and read what their signs (and not their sizes) say about the cars. Parts B and C centered and standardized the columns of \(X\). Here two centered columns meet in a dot product, and the sign of one number sums up a whole scatterplot. The Day 4 notebook finishes the idea: standardize, dot, divide by \(n\), and you have the correlation.
