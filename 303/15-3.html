§15.3: The Fundamental Theorem for Line Integrals

Evaluating a line integral the long way means parametrizing the path, substituting, and integrating, and a messy path makes that messy. Some fields let you skip all of it. Start with a hike.

Two trails climb from trailhead A to the cabin B, which sits 300 m higher. The direct trail (orange) is steep; the switchbacks (green) are gentler and more than twice as long. Each hiker weighs 800 N with a pack. Contours every 50 m.
Predict, then check

Gravity does (negative) work on each hiker on the way up. On which trail does gravity do more work?

The hike is a line integral in disguise. Gravity is the vector field \(\mathbf F=\langle 0,0,-mg\rangle\), and the work along a trail is \(\int_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=\int_C -mg\,dz\): every step contributes \(-mg\) times its rise, so the total is \(-mg\) times the total rise. Gravity is a gradient field, \(\mathbf F=\nabla(-mgz)\), and that is the whole reason the trail did not matter.

Now recall the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus: \(\int_a^b f'(x)\,dx=f(b)-f(a)\). You never needed to know what \(f'\) did between \(a\) and \(b\), only what \(f\) was at the two ends. There is a version of that theorem for line integrals, and it applies exactly to the fields that are gradients. This section answers three linked questions. How do you evaluate \(\int_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r\) in one subtraction? When does the answer depend only on the endpoints? And how can you tell, quickly, whether a given field is conservative and find its potential?

By the end of this section you should be able to:
  1. State and use the Fundamental Theorem for Line Integrals.
  2. Explain path independence, and its connection with closed-path integrals and conservative fields.
  3. Use the component test to decide whether a field is conservative.
  4. Find a potential function for a conservative field in two or three variables.

The theorem

Theorem (Fundamental Theorem for Line Integrals). Let \(C\) be a smooth curve \(\mathbf r(t)\), \(a\le t\le b\). If \(f\) is differentiable and \(\nabla f\) is continuous on an open region containing \(C\), then \[\int_C \nabla f\cdot d\mathbf r = f\bigl(\mathbf r(b)\bigr)-f\bigl(\mathbf r(a)\bigr).\]

Why it is true. By definition, \(\int_C\nabla f\cdot d\mathbf r=\int_a^b\nabla f(\mathbf r(t))\cdot\mathbf r'(t)\,dt\). The integrand is

\[\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\frac{dx}{dt}+\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\frac{dy}{dt}+\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\frac{dz}{dt},\]

and that is exactly what the Chain Rule says \(\dfrac{d}{dt}f(\mathbf r(t))\) is. So the integral is \(\int_a^b\dfrac{d}{dt}f(\mathbf r(t))\,dt\), and the ordinary Fundamental Theorem of Calculus finishes it: \(f(\mathbf r(b))-f(\mathbf r(a))\). (The hike's tally works the same way: each step adds the change in \(-mgz\), and the changes telescope.)

The potential function plays the role of the antiderivative, and the answer depends only on the initial point and terminal point.

Worked example: \(\mathbf F(x,y)=\langle ye^{xy},\ xe^{xy}\rangle\) from \((0,1)\) to \((2,0)\)

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r\), where \(C\) is any smooth curve from \((0,1)\) to \((2,0)\).

Check. \(P_y=e^{xy}+xye^{xy}\) and \(Q_x=e^{xy}+xye^{xy}\). They agree, so \(\mathbf F\) looks conservative.

Find a potential. From \(f_x=ye^{xy}\), integrate in \(x\): \(f=e^{xy}+g(y)\). Then \(f_y=xe^{xy}+g'(y)\) must equal \(Q=xe^{xy}\), so \(g'=0\). Take \(f(x,y)=e^{xy}\).

Apply the theorem. \(\int_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=f(2,0)-f(0,1)=e^0-e^0=0.\) (This field is in the lab below: any two paths give 0.)

Worked example: \(\mathbf F(x,y,z)=\langle yz,\ xz,\ xy\rangle\) from §15.2

This field satisfies \(P_y=z=Q_x\), \(P_z=y=R_x\), \(Q_z=x=R_y\), and \(f(x,y,z)=xyz\) is a potential (\(f_x=yz\), \(f_y=xz\), \(f_z=xy\)). So the work along the straight segment from \(A=(1,0,0)\) to \(B=(0,2,0)\) is \(f(0,2,0)-f(1,0,0)=0-0=0\).

Look back at §15.2

In §15.2 the work of \(\langle yz,xz,xy\rangle\) along \(\mathbf r(t)=\langle t^2,t^3,t\rangle\), \(0\le t\le1\), took a page of algebra. With the potential \(f=xyz\) it takes one line.

Path independence

A line integral \(\int_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r\) is path independent if its value is the same for every path in the domain that starts at \(A\) and ends at \(B\), for every pair \(A\), \(B\). A closed path starts and ends at the same point, and \(\oint_C\) denotes an integral around one.

The theorem above says: if \(\mathbf F\) is conservative, then its line integrals are path independent. Test that in the lab. It starts with the field \(\langle 2x\cos y,\ -x^2\sin y\rangle=\nabla(x^2\cos y)\) from the worked example near the end of this page; the green curves are level curves of \(f\). Path 1 (orange) follows that example's curve \(\mathbf r(t)=\langle t^2,\pi t\rangle\) from \((0,0)\) to \((1,\pi)\).

Predict, then check

Path 1 gives \(-1\). Now drag path 2's blue handles anywhere you like, even far off to the side. What will the work along path 2 do?

Now drag B. The work changes, because \(f(B)\) does, and the two paths still agree.

Predict, then check

The field \(\langle y^2,\ x^2\rangle\) is not a gradient. From \((0,0)\) to \((1,1)\), compare the straight line \(y=x\) with the path that runs along the \(x\)-axis to \((1,0)\) and then straight up. Which does more work?

Two more facts tie these ideas together.

Path independence \(\Longleftrightarrow\) every closed-path integral is zero.

  • (\(\Rightarrow\)) Split a closed path \(C\) at two points \(P\) and \(Q\) into \(C_1\) (from \(P\) to \(Q\)) and \(C_2\) (from \(Q\) back to \(P\)). Reversing a path flips the sign, so \(\oint_C=\int_{C_1}+\int_{C_2}=\int_{C_1}-\int_{-C_2}\), where \(-C_2\) also runs from \(P\) to \(Q\). Path independence says the two integrals on the right are equal, so \(\oint_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=0\).
  • (\(\Leftarrow\)) Let \(C_1\) and \(C_2\) be two paths from \(A\) to \(B\). Then \(C_1\) followed by \(-C_2\) is a closed path, so \(\int_{C_1}-\int_{C_2}=0\).

That is the lab's row out along 1, back along 2: it is the closed-path integral, and it is 0 exactly when the two paths agree.

Predict, then check

Back on the hill: hike up the switchbacks to the cabin, then come straight back down the direct trail to the trailhead. What is the total work done by gravity on the round trip?

Why path independence gives a potential (on an open, connected region)

Fix a starting point \(A\) and define \(f(B)=\int_A^B\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r\); path independence is exactly what makes this a well-defined function of \(B\). To find \(f_x\), go to \((x+\Delta x,y,z)\) by first reaching \((x,y,z)\) and then following the horizontal segment \(\langle t,y,z\rangle\). Then

\[\frac{f(x+\Delta x,y,z)-f(x,y,z)}{\Delta x}=\frac1{\Delta x}\int_x^{x+\Delta x}P(t,y,z)\,dt\longrightarrow P(x,y,z)\]

by the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus. The same argument gives \(f_y=Q\) and \(f_z=R\), so \(\nabla f=\mathbf F\). (On the hill, this \(f\) is \(-800\) N times your height above the trailhead.)

For a field on an open connected region, conservative, path independent, and zero around every closed path all mean the same thing.

The component test

If \(\mathbf F=\nabla f\) then mixed partials of \(f\) agree (Clairaut), so:

  • in the plane, \(\dfrac{\partial P}{\partial y}=\dfrac{\partial Q}{\partial x}\);
  • in space, \(\dfrac{\partial P}{\partial y}=\dfrac{\partial Q}{\partial x}\), \(\dfrac{\partial P}{\partial z}=\dfrac{\partial R}{\partial x}\), and \(\dfrac{\partial Q}{\partial z}=\dfrac{\partial R}{\partial y}\).

If the components have continuous first partials on a simply connected region (one where every closed loop can be shrunk to a point without leaving it; the plane is, the plane with the origin removed is not), then these equations are also sufficient. Where the region has a hole the test can pass and the field still fail to be conservative, so watch the domain.

Predict, then check: a hole in the domain

The vortex \(\mathbf F=\dfrac{\langle -y,\ x\rangle}{x^2+y^2}\) is defined everywhere except the origin, and there \(P_y=Q_x=\dfrac{y^2-x^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2}\). From \((1,0)\) to \((-1,0)\), path 1 passes above the origin and path 2 below it. Predict the two works.

Sort them

For each field, run the component test (in space, all three equations).

\(\langle ye^{xy},\ xe^{xy}\rangle\)
\(\langle yz,\ xz,\ x^2y\rangle\)
\(\langle 2xz,\ \sin z,\ x^2+y\cos z\rangle\)
\(\langle y^2,\ x^2\rangle\)
Worked example: is \(\mathbf F(x,y)=\langle y^2,\ x^2\rangle\) conservative?

\(P_y=2y\) but \(Q_x=2x\). These are not equal (for example at \((0,1)\) they are \(2\) and \(0\)), so \(\mathbf F\) is not conservative. And you can see path dependence directly. From \((0,0)\) to \((1,1)\), the straight line \(y=x\) gives \(\int_0^1 2x^2\,dx=\tfrac23\). Along the \(x\)-axis to \((1,0)\) and then straight up, the first leg contributes \(0\) and the second contributes \(\int_0^1 1\,dy=1\).

Worked example: is \(\mathbf F(x,y,z)=\langle yz,\ xz,\ x^2y\rangle\) conservative?

\(P_y=z=Q_x\), so far so good. But \(P_z=y\) while \(R_x=2xy\). One failed equation is enough: not conservative.

Finding a potential function

If the test passes, build \(f\) one variable at a time: integrate \(P\) in \(x\), which leaves an unknown "constant" \(g(y,z)\); match \(f_y\) with \(Q\) to find \(g\) up to a function of \(z\); match \(f_z\) with \(R\) to finish.

Worked example: \(\mathbf F(x,y,z)=\langle 2xz,\ \sin z,\ x^2+y\cos z\rangle\)

Show that \(\mathbf F\) is conservative and find a potential.

Test. \(P_y=0=Q_x\); \(P_z=2x=R_x\); \(Q_z=\cos z=R_y\). All three hold.

Step 1. \(f=\int 2xz\,dx=x^2z+g(y,z)\).

Step 2. \(f_y=g_y\) must equal \(Q=\sin z\), so \(g=y\sin z+h(z)\) and \(f=x^2z+y\sin z+h(z)\).

Step 3. \(f_z=x^2+y\cos z+h'(z)\) must equal \(R=x^2+y\cos z\), so \(h'=0\).

A potential is \(f(x,y,z)=x^2z+y\sin z\). For instance, the work along any path from \((0,0,0)\) to \((1,2,\pi/2)\) is \(f(1,2,\pi/2)-f(0,0,0)=\tfrac\pi2+2\).

Worked example: \(\mathbf F(x,y)=\langle 2x\cos y,\ -x^2\sin y\rangle\) along \(\mathbf r(t)=\langle t^2,\pi t\rangle\) (the lab's first field)

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r\), where \(C\) is \(\mathbf r(t)=\langle t^2,\pi t\rangle\), \(0\le t\le1\).

\(P_y=-2x\sin y=Q_x\), so the field passes the test. Integrating \(P\) in \(x\) gives \(f=x^2\cos y+g(y)\), and \(f_y=-x^2\sin y+g'(y)=Q\) forces \(g'=0\). Take \(f=x^2\cos y\). The curve starts at \(\mathbf r(0)=(0,0)\) and ends at \(\mathbf r(1)=(1,\pi)\), so

\[\begin{aligned}\int_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r&=f(1,\pi)-f(0,0)\\&=1\cdot(-1)-0=-1.\end{aligned}\]
Worked example, you drive

\(\mathbf F(x,y,z)=\langle e^z,\ 1,\ xe^z\rangle\) passes the test (\(P_y=Q_x=0\), \(P_z=R_x=e^z\), \(Q_z=R_y=0\)). Find a potential, then the work along the path from \((0,1,0)\) to \((1,1,0)\) to \((1,0,0)\) to \((1,0,\ln2)\) (three straight segments).

Practice & reflection

  1. Explain why the Fundamental Theorem for Line Integrals is the "same theorem" as the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus. What plays the role of the antiderivative? What plays the role of the interval?
    The potential \(f\) plays the antiderivative (its gradient is the field you integrate, just as an antiderivative's derivative is the integrand), and the path's two endpoints play the ends of the interval. The proof even uses the one-variable theorem: along the path, \(\nabla f\cdot\mathbf r'(t)\) is the derivative of \(f(\mathbf r(t))\).
  2. Someone hands you \(\int_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r\) along a complicated path and asks for the value. What is the first thing you should check, and why?
    Check whether \(\mathbf F\) is conservative (the component test, on a simply connected region). If it is, find a potential and subtract its values at the endpoints; the complicated path never has to be parametrized.
  3. If \(\mathbf F\) is conservative and \(C\) is a closed loop, what is \(\oint_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r\)? Which of the equivalent statements about conservative fields does that use?
    It is 0: the start and end are the same point, so \(f(\text{end})-f(\text{start})=0\). It uses "conservative \(\Rightarrow\) path independent \(\Leftrightarrow\) zero around every closed path" (the round trip on the hill).
  4. In a field that is not conservative, two particular paths from \(A\) to \(B\) might still give the same work by coincidence. Why does that not make the field path independent?
    Path independence is a claim about every pair of paths between every pair of points. One pair agreeing is one closed loop with integral 0; a single other loop with a nonzero integral breaks it. (In the lab, with \(\langle y^2,x^2\rangle\), you can bend path 2 until its work equals path 1's, and the field is still not conservative.)
  5. The plane is simply connected and the plane with the origin removed is not. Why might the second fact make you cautious about concluding "conservative" from the component test?
    On a region with a hole the test only checks the field point by point, and a loop around the hole can still collect a nonzero total. The vortex in the lab passes the test everywhere it is defined, yet going around the origin once gives \(2\pi\).
