Chapter 5 · A

Why chase beauty in math?

A. Chapter 5, “Beauty.” Su starts with an architecture class he took because it looked easy, and a line from Maryam Mirzakhani: “The beauty of mathematics only shows itself to more patient followers.” Then he tries to do what Paul Erdős said couldn’t be done: explain mathematical beauty to someone who hasn’t seen it.

Goal: Follow Su’s four kinds of mathematical beauty and his case for why they matter, then decide how far you agree. Chapter 4 was play; this chapter says beauty is what keeps you coming back to hard problems. Chapter 6 (permanence) picks up his chessboard puzzle and explains it with an invariant.

Chapter 5 · Puzzle

Ants on a Log

Puzzle. One hundred ants are dropped at random spots on a log 1 meter long, each facing left or right. Each walks toward the end it faces at 1 meter per minute. When two ants meet, they bounce off each other and both reverse direction at the same speed. An ant that reaches an end falls off. Over all possible starting setups, what is the longest you would have to wait to be sure the log is empty? (Su, Chapter 5.)

Goal: Find the longest possible wait, and see why no setup can make it longer. Su shares this puzzle as an example of insightful beauty: a simple way of looking at it makes a messy problem easy. In the language of Chapter 6, a bounce leaves the set of positions unchanged: an invariant.

Chapter 5 · Puzzle

Chessboard Problems

Puzzle. Remove two diagonally opposite corner squares from an \(8\times 8\) chessboard. Can you cover the remaining 62 squares with \(1\times 2\) dominoes, each covering two adjacent squares, with no overlaps and nothing hanging off the board? Show a tiling or show that none exists. Then try a couple of the follow-up questions Su poses. (Su, end of Chapter 5.)

Goal: Decide whether the tiling exists, with an argument that settles it for every possible arrangement. Christopher solves the first part in his letter, and Su returns to it in Chapter 6 as his example of an invariant. If you read the letter first, use this card to make the argument your own.
