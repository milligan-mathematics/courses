Paper first, then check in. Give yourself 15 minutes for the five problems on paper, no calculator, the way the real test runs. Then open each card. If you already have an answer, press Check my answer from paper: one try. Right, and you can skip to Look back for a faster route or a variation. Not right, or not sure, and the walk-through takes it one step at a time: Understand, Plan, Carry out, Look back.

Every wrong choice has a story. The test writers build the wrong answers out of the slips people actually make. When you pick one here, the page tells you which slip leads there, so you can spot it next time.

Your place is saved in this browser, so you can stop and come back. Bring the one card that bugged you most to our check-in.

Walk-through · Set B8, Problem 1

Volume under a paraboloid

1. What is the volume of the solid that lies under the paraboloid \(z=4-x^2-y^2\) and above the \(xy\)-plane?
(A) \(8\pi\)
(B) \(\frac{32\pi}{3}\)
(C) \(16\pi\)
(D) \(\frac{64\pi}{3}\)
(E) \(32\pi\)

Goal: Set up the volume as a double integral over the right base, in the coordinates that fit it. Round bases mean polar coordinates, and \(dA = r\,dr\,d\theta\) is the piece people drop. The MFT asks for volumes, masses and averages this way.

Walk-through · Set B8, Problem 2

When a product of matrices is zero

2. Let \(A\) and \(B\) be \(n\times n\) matrices with \(AB=0\), the zero matrix. Which of the following must be true?
  I. \(A=0\) or \(B=0\).
  II. \(BA=0\).
  III. If \(A\) is invertible, then \(B=0\).
(A) None
(B) I only
(C) II only
(D) III only
(E) II and III only

Goal: Decide each statement by proving it or breaking it with a small matrix example. Matrices break two rules that numbers keep: \(ab = 0\) forces a zero factor, and \(ab = ba\). The MFT tests both, and \(2\times2\) examples with one 1 in them settle most of these items.

Walk-through · Set B8, Problem 3

Undoing multiplication by 17

3. What is the smallest positive integer \(x\) such that \(17x\equiv 1\) (mod 60)?
(A) \(7\)
(B) \(13\)
(C) \(23\)
(D) \(47\)
(E) \(53\)

Goal: Find the number that undoes multiplication by 17 when you only keep remainders mod 60. Modular inverses come up in number theory, in solving congruences, and in \(\mathbb{Z}_n\) as a ring: an element is a unit exactly when it has one. The Euclidean algorithm run backward finds them.

Walk-through · Set B8, Problem 4

Two random numbers with a small sum

4. Let \(X\) and \(Y\) be independent random variables, each uniformly distributed on the interval \([0,2]\). What is \(P(X+Y<1)\)?
(A) \(\frac{1}{16}\)
(B) \(\frac{1}{8}\)
(C) \(\frac{1}{4}\)
(D) \(\frac{1}{3}\)
(E) \(\frac{1}{2}\)

Goal: Turn the probability into an area: the point \((X, Y)\) is spread evenly over a square. Two independent uniform variables give a point spread evenly over a rectangle, so any event about them is an area ratio. The MFT uses this for “meeting time” and “broken stick” problems too.

Walk-through · Set B8, Problem 5

Which set is in one piece?

5. Which of the following subsets of \(\mathbb{R}^2\), with the usual topology, is connected?
(A) \(\{(x,y): xy\ne 0\}\)
(B) \(\{(x,y): x^2-y^2=1\}\)
(C) \(\{(x,y): y\ge x^2\}\cup\{(x,y): y\le -x^2\}\)
(D) \(\{(x,y): x^2+y^2\ne 1\}\)
(E) \(\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{R}\)

Goal: Picture each set, then either split it into separated pieces or show how its parts are joined. Topology on the MFT is mostly pictures in \(\mathbb{R}^2\): connected, compact, open, closed. Two tools cover connectedness: a continuous function that changes sign without hitting zero splits a set, and connected pieces with a common point stay connected.
