Welcome to Unit 4. This page goes with the Day 31 handout, one card per Part. Do Part A on paper, then open the Part A card and tell it what you got. If something is off, the page tries to name the slip, and hints are there when you want them (each one a little more specific). Then on to Part B, and so on. The first Skeptic box is at the end of the Part C card, the second at the end of Part D.

The paper is what you turn in. You’ll see answers here as you check your work. That’s on purpose: they’re for checking, not copying. Your handout, with your lines drawn on the figure and your own reasoning, is what goes on Canvas, both Skeptic boxes included.

Typing tips: values of \(f\) can be decimals or fractions (-0.5 and -1/2 both work). Where a square root shows up, like a length \(\|\mathbf w\|\) or a margin, a rounded decimal is fine (0.447 or 0.45). Your place is saved in this browser, so you can stop and come back.

Walk-through · Part A

A line as the sign of w·x + b

Part A. Do Part A on paper first, then check in here.
MML writes a line (in more dimensions, a hyperplane) as the points where a function is zero: \(f(\mathbf x) = \mathbf w\cdot\mathbf x + b = w_1x_1 + w_2x_2 + b\), line: \(f(\mathbf x) = 0\), predicted label \(+1\) if \(f(\mathbf x) \ge 0\) and \(-1\) if \(f(\mathbf x) < 0\). Take \(\mathbf w = (1, 2)\) and \(b = -6\), so \(f(\mathbf x) = x_1 + 2x_2 - 6\).
1. Find two points on the line \(f(\mathbf x) = 0\) (try \(x_1 = 0\), then \(x_2 = 0\)). Subtract them to get a vector along the line, and dot it with \(\mathbf w\). What do you get, and what does it say about the direction of \(\mathbf w\) compared with the line? (MML Figure 12.2.)
2. Compute \(f\) at \((4, 4)\) and at \((1, 1)\). Which label does each get? Which side of the line does \(\mathbf w\) point toward, the \(+1\) side or the \(-1\) side?
3. The points where \(f(\mathbf x) = 1\) form a line too, and so do the points where \(f(\mathbf x) = -1\). How are those two lines related to \(f(\mathbf x) = 0\)? (Write each in the form \(x_1 + 2x_2 = \text{something}\).)

Goal: Read a line off \(\mathbf w\) and \(b\): \(\mathbf w\) is perpendicular to it and points to the \(+1\) side, and changing the value of \(f\) slides the line without turning it. Part B uses the perpendicular \(\mathbf w\) to measure how far a point is from the line, and the lines \(f = \pm1\) come back in Part D as the edges of the SVM’s street.

Walk-through · Part B

How far is a point from the line?

Part B. Do Part B on paper first, then check in here.
To get from a point \(\mathbf x\) to the closest point on the line, you walk straight toward the line, that is, along the unit vector \(\mathbf w/\|\mathbf w\|\) (Day 9: the shortest path to a line is perpendicular to it). Say the distance is \(r\), so the closest point is \(\mathbf x - r\,\frac{\mathbf w}{\|\mathbf w\|}\).
1. That closest point is on the line, so \(f\) of it is 0. Write that out, \(\mathbf w\cdot\bigl(\mathbf x - r\frac{\mathbf w}{\|\mathbf w\|}\bigr) + b = 0\), use \(\mathbf w\cdot\mathbf w = \|\mathbf w\|^2\), and solve for \(r\). You should get \(r = \dfrac{\mathbf w\cdot\mathbf x + b}{\|\mathbf w\|} = \dfrac{f(\mathbf x)}{\|\mathbf w\|}\).
2. \(r\) is negative for points on the \(-1\) side. Explain why the distance is \(|f(\mathbf x)|/\|\mathbf w\|\), and why \(y\,f(\mathbf x)/\|\mathbf w\|\) is the distance when the point is on its correct side (and negative when it is on the wrong side).
3. Sanity check on an easy line: the vertical line \(x_1 = 2.5\) is \(f(\mathbf x) = x_1 - 2.5\), with \(\mathbf w = (1, 0)\). Use the formula for the distance from \((4, 4)\) to this line, then check it by eye on the figure.

Goal: Turn \(f(\mathbf x)\) into a distance by dividing by \(\|\mathbf w\|\), and use the label \(y\) to tell the right side from the wrong side. Part A showed \(\mathbf w\) is perpendicular to the line; that’s why walking along \(\mathbf w\) is the shortest way there. Part C uses \(|f|/\|\mathbf w\|\) to compare three lines.

Walk-through · Part C

Comparing candidate lines

Part C. Do Part C on paper first, then check in here.
The figure shows eight labeled points: \(P_1, \dots, P_4\) have label \(+1\) and \(N_1, \dots, N_4\) have label \(-1\). (Their coordinates, from the figure: \(N_1 = (1, 2)\), \(N_2 = (2, 1)\), \(N_3 = (0, 1)\), \(N_4 = (1, 0)\); \(P_1 = (2, 3)\), \(P_2 = (3, 2)\), \(P_3 = (4, 4)\), \(P_4 = (3, 5)\).) Here are three candidate lines: \(\ell_1:\ x_1 - 2.5 = 0\), \(\ell_2:\ x_1 + 2x_2 - 6 = 0\), \(\ell_3:\ x_1 + x_2 - 4 = 0\).
1. Draw all three on the figure. Then fill in the table: the value of \(f\) at each point, for each line (columns \(N_1, \dots, N_4, P_1, \dots, P_4\), \(\|\mathbf w\|\), margin).
2. Which lines classify all eight points correctly? (Check the signs.) For a line that does, the margin is the distance from the line to the closest point. Fill in the margin column for those lines.
3. Which line has the biggest margin? Which points are closest to it? Those closest points are its support vectors: the line is propped up by them.
4. Slide \(\ell_3\) to \(x_1 + x_2 - 4.5 = 0\) (same direction, shifted). What is its margin now? Where, between the two classes, does the best line sit?
5. Imagine moving \(P_3\) from \((4, 4)\) to \((5, 5)\). Does the best line change? What about moving \(P_1\) from \((2, 3)\) to \((2, 2.5)\)?
Skeptic. “Multiply \(\ell_3\) by 10: \(10x_1 + 10x_2 - 40 = 0\). Now \(f\) at the closest points is \(\pm10\) instead of \(\pm1\), so this line has ten times the margin.” Is it a different line? Use the distance formula to settle it.

Goal: Score each candidate line by its margin, the distance to its closest point, and find the line with the widest street and the points that hold it up. This is the SVM’s question, answered by hand for three lines. Part D turns “biggest margin” into a problem a computer can solve, and on Wednesday the computer finds the best line among all lines.

Walk-through · Part D

Scaling, and when no line works

Part D. Do Part D on paper first, then check in here.
The SVM’s scale. The Skeptic shows that \((\mathbf w, b)\) and \((10\mathbf w, 10b)\) are the same line. The SVM uses that freedom to pick one scale: it scales so that \(f(\mathbf x) = \pm1\) at the closest points (MML §12.2.3). Then every point satisfies \(y_n f(\mathbf x_n) \ge 1\).
1. With that scaling, explain why the margin is \(1/\|\mathbf w\|\). Check that \(\ell_2\) and \(\ell_3\) are already scaled this way, and that \(1/\|\mathbf w\|\) gives your margins from Part C.
2. So “make the margin as big as possible” becomes: make \(\|\mathbf w\|\) as small as possible, while keeping \(y_n(\mathbf w\cdot\mathbf x_n + b) \ge 1\) for every point. In your own words, why does that pair of goals rule out a line that squeezes between the classes off-center?
3. Now a ninth point arrives: \(N_5 = (3, 3)\) with label \(-1\). Plot it. Can any line put all nine points on their correct sides? Explain.
4. When no line works, the soft-margin SVM lets some points break the rule, and charges each one a slack \(\xi_n = \max\{0,\ 1 - y_nf(\mathbf x_n)\}\) (MML §12.2.4–12.2.5). For \(\ell_3\), compute \(\xi_n\) for \(N_5\), for \(P_1\), and for \(N_3\). Which points pay nothing? What does a slack between 0 and 1 mean? A slack bigger than 1?
5. The soft-margin SVM picks the line that makes \(\tfrac12\|\mathbf w\|^2 + C\sum_n\xi_n\) as small as possible, where you choose the number \(C > 0\). Compute this for \(\ell_3\) with \(C = 1\). Then describe in words what the SVM cares about most when \(C\) is tiny, and when \(C\) is huge.
Skeptic. “A huge \(C\) is always better: it punishes every mistake, so the classifier makes the fewest mistakes on the training points.” Think back to Day 16’s wiggly polynomials. What is the risk, and how would you choose \(C\) honestly?

Goal: Turn “widest margin” into “smallest \(\|\mathbf w\|\) with every \(y_nf(\mathbf x_n) \ge 1\),” then soften the rule with slacks and a price \(C\) for when no line can separate the classes. This is the problem scikit-learn’s SVC(kernel="linear", C=...) solves. On Wednesday you hand it these nine points and watch the line move as \(C\) changes; Monday’s kernel trick bends the line.
