Paper first, then check in. Give yourself 15 minutes for the five problems on paper, no calculator, the way the real test runs. Then open each card. If you already have an answer, press Check my answer from paper: one try. Right, and you can skip to Look back for a faster route or a variation. Not right, or not sure, and the walk-through takes it one step at a time: Understand, Plan, Carry out, Look back.

Every wrong choice has a story. The test writers build the wrong answers out of the slips people actually make. When you pick one here, the page tells you which slip leads there, so you can spot it next time.

Your place is saved in this browser, so you can stop and come back. Bring the one card that bugged you most to our check-in.

Walk-through · Set B10, Problem 1

A series inside a series

1. What is the coefficient of \(x^4\) in the Maclaurin series for \(\cos(x^2)\)?
(A) \(-\frac{1}{2}\)
(B) \(-\frac{1}{24}\)
(C) \(0\)
(D) \(\frac{1}{24}\)
(E) \(\frac{1}{2}\)

Goal: Get the series for \(\cos(x^2)\) from the one you know for \(\cos u\), without taking any derivatives. Substituting into a known series (\(e^u\), \(\sin u\), \(\cos u\), \(\frac{1}{1-u}\)) is the fastest route to most MFT series questions, and to high derivatives at 0.

Walk-through · Set B10, Problem 2

Two paths to s

2. Let \(z=x^2y\), where \(x=s+t\) and \(y=st\). What is \(\frac{\partial z}{\partial s}\) when \(s=1\) and \(t=2\)?
(A) \(12\)
(B) \(18\)
(C) \(21\)
(D) \(30\)
(E) \(36\)

Goal: Follow every path from \(z\) down to \(s\), and add up what each one contributes. The multivariable chain rule is a sum over paths in a dependency tree. The MFT checks that you find all the paths and evaluate each piece at the right point.

Walk-through · Set B10, Problem 3

What a projection can and can’t undo

3. Let \(P\) be the \(3\times3\) matrix of the orthogonal projection of \(\mathbb{R}^3\) onto a plane through the origin. Which of the following must be true?
  I. \(P^2=P\)
  II. \(\det(P)=0\)
  III. \(P\) is invertible.
(A) I only
(B) II only
(C) I and II only
(D) I and III only
(E) I, II, and III

Goal: Decide each statement from what \(P\) does geometrically: to vectors in the plane, and to the normal vector. Projections are the MFT’s favorite matrices to ask about without numbers: \(P^2 = P\), eigenvalues 0 and 1, rank equal to the dimension of the target. Least squares is built on them.

Walk-through · Set B10, Problem 4

How rare is 69 inches?

4. Heights of adult women in a large population are approximately normally distributed with mean \(64\) inches and standard deviation \(2.5\) inches. About what percent of these women are taller than \(69\) inches?
(A) \(0.15\%\)
(B) \(2.5\%\)
(C) \(5\%\)
(D) \(16\%\)
(E) \(32\%\)

Goal: Measure 69 inches in standard deviations from the mean, then use the 68–95–99.7 rule. The MFT gives no table, so normal-distribution questions are built for the 68–95–99.7 rule and a z-score you can do in your head.

Walk-through · Set B10, Problem 5

How far is the plane from the origin?

5. What is the distance from the origin to the plane that passes through the points \((1,0,0)\), \((0,2,0)\), and \((0,0,3)\)?
(A) \(\frac{1}{7}\)
(B) \(\frac{3}{7}\)
(C) \(\frac{1}{2}\)
(D) \(\frac{6}{11}\)
(E) \(\frac{6}{7}\)

Goal: Write the plane as \(ax + by + cz = d\), then measure along its normal vector. Point-to-plane distance, \(\frac{|ax_0 + by_0 + cz_0 - d|}{\sqrt{a^2+b^2+c^2}}\), is a standard MFT item, and the intercept form of a plane gets you the equation in one line.
