Chapter 10 · A

Who gets the secret menu?

A. Chapter 10, “Justice.” Su notices that at his favorite Chinese restaurant, some diners get a secret menu and red bean soup while others get crunchy noodles and Jell-O. He was told, “You won’t like the stuff on that menu.” Then he asks: “Whom do we allow a peek at the secret mathematical menu?”

Goal: Follow Su’s case that justice is a desire mathematics should serve, and decide what it asks of you. This chapter returns to Chapter 1’s Simone Weil line and to Chapter 8’s “progress, not promise.” It is the most direct link in the book to love of neighbor, which your Math & Faith paper can use.

Chapter 10 · Puzzle

Rental Harmony

Puzzle. You and some friends rent a house together. The rooms differ in size and features, and you each have different preferences. Can you always split the rent and price the rooms so that each person wants a different room? Su proved the answer is yes, under three mild conditions (good house, closed preferences, miserly tenants). Try it on small houses. (Su, end of Chapter 10.) To make the houses concrete, this card uses a simple preference model of its own, not Su’s: each friend puts a dollar worth on each room and prefers the room with the largest worth minus rent.

Goal: Understand what the theorem promises, then find and check harmonious rent splits in two concrete houses. Fair division is mathematics in the service of justice, the chapter’s desire. Su’s proof uses geometry and combinatorics, and the New York Times built a rent calculator from it.
