# §13.8: Lagrange Multipliers

Most optimization problems come with a catch. You can't pick any point you like; you have to stay on a curve or a surface. The fence has a fixed length, the box has a fixed surface area, or the point has to sit on a given ellipse. In §13.7 you handled some of these by substitution: solve the constraint for one variable and plug it in. That works well when the constraint is easy to solve, and it gets messy fast when it isn't.

The method of Lagrange multipliers, named after Joseph-Louis Lagrange, takes a different route. You never solve the constraint for anything. You look for the places where the level curves of $$f$$ just touch the constraint curve, and that touching turns into a short algebra condition: the two gradient vectors point along the same line. That is the whole idea, and the demo below lets you watch it happen before you trust the algebra.

By the end of this section you should be able to:

1. Explain geometrically why an extreme value of $$f$$ on a constraint has parallel gradients.
2. Set up the Lagrange system $$\nabla f = \lambda \nabla g$$ together with the constraint $$g = c$$.
3. Solve the system, evaluate $$f$$ at every candidate, and identify the absolute maximum and minimum on a closed, bounded constraint.
4. Extend the method to two constraints, using two multipliers.

Each subsection below has a worked example from the lecture notes. Check each one by hand before you move on.

### Level curves that just touch

The level curves of $$f$$ are the curves where $$f$$ has a fixed value, and moving to a higher level curve makes $$f$$ bigger. Now draw the constraint curve $$g(x,y) = c$$ over the same picture. If the constraint crosses a level curve at some point, you can slide a little way along the constraint and reach a higher or lower level. So a crossing point can't be a maximum or a minimum. An extreme value can only happen where the constraint just touches a level curve without crossing it. That is tangency. The demo works in the plane, where the picture is easiest to see, but the same idea holds in space with level surfaces.

Think of walking a trail along a hillside, with the contour lines drawn on a map. If the trail crosses a contour, you can climb or descend by following the trail in one direction. At the highest point on the trail, the trail runs along a contour for an instant, and that is the picture to keep in mind.

Why is a gradient perpendicular to a level curve in the first place? Walk along the level curve. The value of $$f$$ never changes, so its rate of change in that direction is zero. That rate is the dot product of $$\nabla f$$ with the direction of travel, so $$\nabla f$$ must be perpendicular to every direction along the level curve.

Tangency has a gradient description. The gradient $$\nabla f$$ is perpendicular to the level curve through a point, and $$\nabla g$$ is perpendicular to the constraint. If the two curves are tangent, their normal vectors are parallel, so

$$\nabla f = \lambda \nabla g$$

for some number $$\lambda$$, the Lagrange multiplier. Here is the short argument in the plane. Let $$\mathbf{r}(t)$$ trace the constraint curve. At an extreme value, the derivative of $$f(\mathbf{r}(t))$$ is zero, so $$\nabla f$$ is perpendicular to $$\mathbf{r}'$$. The constraint never changes along the curve, so $$\nabla g$$ is also perpendicular to $$\mathbf{r}'$$. In the plane, a nonzero vector has only one perpendicular direction, so $$\nabla f$$ and $$\nabla g$$ are parallel, as long as $$\nabla g \neq \mathbf{0}$$. In space, run the same argument on every curve lying on the constraint surface. Their tangent vectors fill out a plane, and both gradients are perpendicular to that whole plane, so they are parallel too.

> **Worked example.** Find the point on the plane $$-2x - 6y + 4z = 8$$ closest to $$(1,-2,3)$$. Minimize the squared distance $$f = (x-1)^2 + (y+2)^2 + (z-3)^2$$. Then $$\nabla f = \langle 2(x-1), 2(y+2), 2(z-3) \rangle$$ and $$\nabla g = \langle -2, -6, 4 \rangle$$. At the closest point $$\left(\tfrac32, -\tfrac12, 2\right)$$, $$\nabla f = \langle 1, 3, -2 \rangle = -\tfrac12 \nabla g$$. The two gradients lie along one line, so the sphere centered at $$(1,-2,3)$$ through that point just touches the plane.

Minimizing the squared distance gives the same point as minimizing the distance itself. It is easier, because the square root disappears and the gradient is a simple polynomial.

### The method, step by step

Notice what the method does not do: it never solves the constraint for one variable. You keep all the variables and let the multiplier handle the bookkeeping. The procedure has six steps, in the order of the notes:

1. Name the objective $$f$$ and the constraint $$g = c$$.
2. Compute $$\nabla f$$ and $$\nabla g$$.
3. Solve $$\nabla f = \lambda \nabla g$$ together with $$g = c$$. With three variables that is four equations in $$x$$, $$y$$, $$z$$, and $$\lambda$$.
4. Find every solution, including the special cases: $$\lambda$$ can be zero, and a variable can be zero.
5. Evaluate $$f$$ at each candidate.
6. Compare the values. On a closed, bounded constraint, the largest is the absolute maximum and the smallest is the absolute minimum.

Step 3 is also a count check. With $$n$$ variables you have $$n+1$$ unknowns (the variables and $$\lambda$$), and the $$n$$ gradient equations plus the constraint give $$n+1$$ equations. That is why the system usually has finitely many solutions.

The common mistake is dividing by something that might be zero. If you divide an equation by $$x$$, you have quietly assumed $$x \neq 0$$. Check that case by itself, or solutions disappear without any warning.

Step 6 is where candidates become answers. A solution of the system can be a maximum, a minimum, or neither. To sort out the absolute extremes, compare the values of $$f$$, as the example below does. If the constraint set is not closed and bounded, a candidate may be only a local extreme, and you have to check what happens far out along the constraint.

> **Worked example.** Same plane and point as before. The gradient equations are $$2(x-1) = -2\lambda$$, $$2(y+2) = -6\lambda$$, and $$2(z-3) = 4\lambda$$, so $$x = 1 - \lambda$$, $$y = -2 - 3\lambda$$, and $$z = 3 + 2\lambda$$. Substituting into $$-2x - 6y + 4z = 8$$ gives $$22 + 28\lambda = 8$$, so $$\lambda = -\tfrac12$$. Back-substituting gives the one candidate $$\left(\tfrac32, -\tfrac12, 2\right)$$. Its squared distance is $$\tfrac14 + \tfrac94 + 1 = \tfrac72$$, so the distance is $$\sqrt{7/2} = \tfrac{\sqrt{14}}{2}$$. Since $$f$$ grows without bound as you move away from the point, that single candidate must be the minimum.

### Two constraints

Two constraints $$g = c$$ and $$h = k$$ in space meet in a curve, and an extreme value of $$f$$ on that curve must have $$\nabla f$$ lying in the plane spanned by $$\nabla g$$ and $$\nabla h$$. So

$$\nabla f = \lambda \nabla g + \mu \nabla h$$

with two multipliers. Together with the two constraints, that is five equations in five unknowns: $$x$$, $$y$$, $$z$$, $$\lambda$$, and $$\mu$$.

Why must $$\nabla f$$ lie in that plane? Both constraints stay constant along the curve where they meet, so $$\nabla g$$ and $$\nabla h$$ are both perpendicular to its tangent direction. At an extreme value, $$\nabla f$$ is perpendicular to that tangent too. All three gradients therefore lie in the plane perpendicular to one direction. When $$\nabla g$$ and $$\nabla h$$ are not parallel, they span that plane, and $$\nabla f$$ is a combination of them.

> **Worked example.** Find the absolute extreme values of $$f = x + y + z$$ subject to $$x^2 + 2y^2 = 6$$ and $$3x + 2y + 4z = 5$$. Here $$\nabla f = \langle 1,1,1 \rangle$$, $$\nabla g = \langle 2x, 4y, 0 \rangle$$, and $$\nabla h = \langle 3, 2, 4 \rangle$$. The $$z$$-equation gives $$1 = 4\mu$$, so $$\mu = \tfrac14$$. The $$x$$- and $$y$$-equations then give $$\lambda x = \tfrac18$$ and $$\lambda y = \tfrac18$$. Because $$\tfrac18 \neq 0$$, we know $$\lambda \neq 0$$, so $$x = y$$. Then $$3x^2 = 6$$ gives $$x = \pm\sqrt2$$, and the second constraint gives the candidates $$\left(\sqrt2, \sqrt2, \tfrac{5-5\sqrt2}{4}\right)$$ and $$\left(-\sqrt2, -\sqrt2, \tfrac{5+5\sqrt2}{4}\right)$$. Their values are $$\tfrac{5+3\sqrt2}{4}$$ (the maximum) and $$\tfrac{5-3\sqrt2}{4}$$ (the minimum). The constraint set is a closed, bounded curve, so both are absolute.

Notice that $$\mu = \tfrac14$$ at both candidates, while $$\lambda = \tfrac{\sqrt2}{16}$$ at the maximum and $$\lambda = -\tfrac{\sqrt2}{16}$$ at the minimum. So $$\lambda$$ changes sign between the two points, while $$\mu$$ does not.

### Practice & reflection

These questions are about the ideas, not the arithmetic. Answer each in a sentence or two. The demo can test your answers to the first two.

1. Suppose the level curve of $$f$$ through a point $$P$$ on the constraint crosses the constraint instead of touching it. Explain why you can move along the constraint in one direction to get a larger value of $$f$$ than at $$P$$, and what that tells you about $$P$$.
2. In the first worked example, $$\lambda = -\tfrac12$$. Does a negative multiplier mean the point is a minimum? Say what the sign tells you about how $$\nabla f$$ and $$\nabla g$$ point, and what it leaves undecided.
3. What happens if $$\nabla g = \mathbf{0}$$ at a point of the constraint? Could an extreme value occur there, and would the system $$\nabla f = \lambda \nabla g$$ be able to find it?
4. In the two-constraint example, $$\mu = \tfrac14$$ came out of a single equation right away. Why did that make the rest of the system easier, and how would you choose which equation to solve first in a harder problem?
5. The method produces candidates, not answers. Name two things you still have to check before you can call a candidate the absolute maximum on a constraint curve.

Play with the demo below before the homework. Pick a preset, predict where the extremes are from the contour map, and then slide the point along the green constraint. Watch for the moment the orange and blue arrows line up: the readout shows $$\lambda$$ there, and the small plot shows that the highs and lows of $$f$$ happen at exactly those moments. Then check your $$\lambda$$ against the algebra, the way the examples above did.
