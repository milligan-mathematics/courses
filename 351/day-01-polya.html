Walk-through · Part A

Sort it out

Part A. Do Part A on paper first, then check in here.
The four families: Regression (R), predict a number; Dimensionality reduction (DR), summarize many measurements with a few; Density estimation (DE), describe where the data lives: its groups and its oddballs; Classification (C), predict a category.
For each question, write the family it belongs to (R, DR, DE, or C). Then pick four of them and, for each, say what goes in (the data) and what comes out (the answer).
1. Predict a used car’s price from its age and mileage. 2. Decide whether an X-ray of a weld shows a crack. 3. Boil 50 sensor readings from a jet engine down to 2 or 3 numbers an engineer can watch. 4. Flag a credit-card purchase that doesn’t look like your usual spending. 5. Predict tomorrow’s high temperature. 6. Sort email into spam and not spam. 7. Find natural groups of shoppers from their purchases, with no labels given. 8. Store face photos as a few numbers each, so they can be compared quickly. 9. Predict a building’s yearly energy use from its size and insulation. 10. Notice a vibration pattern that means a bearing is about to fail. 11. Identify which of 7 bean varieties a bean is from measurements of its shape. 12. Say how rare a 5-inch rainstorm is in your town.
Skeptic. Pick one question that could belong to two families and argue for both. What would you need to know to decide?

Goal: Check your twelve labels, see why the borderline ones are borderline, and practice naming what goes in and what comes out. These four families are the four units of the semester, one MML chapter each (9 to 12). Part B opens the first one, regression, with four cars and a line.

Walk-through · Part B

Anatomy of a learning problem

Part B. Do Part B on paper first, then check in here.
Four cars (weight in thousands of pounds) and their fuel economy: car A, \(w = 2.0\), 33 mpg; car B, \(w = 2.5\), 27 mpg; car C, \(w = 3.5\), 21 mpg; car D, \(w = 4.0\), 15 mpg. A model is a rule that turns inputs into a prediction. We’ll use a line: \(\text{predicted mpg} = a - b\,w\). The numbers \(a\) and \(b\) are the model’s parameters. Different parameters give different models.
1. Fill in the table for two candidate models, Model A: \(a = 45,\ b = 7.5\) and Model B: \(a = 50,\ b = 9\): predicted, error, error\(^2\) for each car, and the total squared error. The error is (actual \(-\) predicted).
2. Which model fits better? Model A gets one car exactly right and Model B gets none right. Why can Model B still win?
3. Why square the errors before adding them? What would go wrong if you just added the errors?
4. A computer searched every possible \((a, b)\) and reported the smallest total squared error at \(a = 49.2\), \(b = 8.4\). Check its total yourself. Did it beat both of your models?
5. In everyday words, what does \(b = 8.4\) say about cars?
The vocabulary. The four cars are the data. The line \(a - bw\) is the model. Total squared error is the loss. Learning means searching for the parameters that make the loss small.

Goal: Check your two tables and totals, say why squared error picks Model B, and confirm the computer’s line beats both. Part A named four families; this is the first, regression, in miniature: data, a model with two parameters, a loss, and a search. Unit 1 builds the search (least squares) from the linear algebra you know.

Walk-through · Part C

Push back

Part C. Do Part C on paper first, then check in here.
1. A wiggly curve could pass through all four points exactly, giving a total squared error of 0. Is it a better model than the line? What would you want to test it on?
2. Use the computer’s line to predict the mpg of a 6,000-pound pickup truck. What happened, and what does it warn you about?
Skeptic. “The best model is the one with the smallest error.” Smallest error on which cars? Write one sentence you’d add to make this statement true.
From the reading. In one sentence, in your own words: what does MML mean by a “model”?

Goal: See why a perfect fit isn’t automatically a good model, and why a line is only trustworthy near its data. Part B found the best line for four cars. Here you ask what “best” is good for: predicting cars you haven’t seen. That question (training data and test data) comes back on Day 8 and runs all semester.
