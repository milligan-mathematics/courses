Walk-through · Part A

No single cut

Part A. Do Part A on paper first, then check in here.
On a number line, a “line” that separates is a single cut: a number \(t\), with one label on each side. The Part A points: class \(+1\) (\(\bullet\)) at \(x = -3, -2, 2, 3\) and class \(-1\) (\(\circ\)) at \(x = -1, 0, 1\).
1. Explain why no single cut \(t\) puts all the \(\bullet\)’s on one side and all the \(\circ\)’s on the other.
2. Lift each point: send \(x\) to the point \((x,\ x^2)\) in the plane. Plot the seven lifted points on the Part A axes, keeping \(\bullet\) and \(\circ\).
3. Draw a straight line in the plane that separates the lifted \(\bullet\)’s from the lifted \(\circ\)’s. Which line has the biggest margin? Write it as an equation in the coordinates \((x, x^2)\).
4. Your line is straight in the lifted plane. What does it say back on the number line? Translate “above the line” into a statement about \(x\) alone. How many cuts is that?
Skeptic. “Adding \(x^2\) makes this a nonlinear classifier, so last week’s linear SVM can’t be used.” Is the rule \(w_1x + w_2x^2 + b \ge 0\) linear in the weights \((w_1, w_2, b)\)? Compare with Day 17, where adding horsepower\(^2\) still used LinearRegression.

Goal: See a pattern no single cut can split, lift it to the plane where one straight line does, and read that line back as a rule about \(x\). Day 31’s straight-line classifier, fed a new coordinate. Part B tilts the line, Part C lifts the plane into 3-D, and Part D finds a way to skip writing the new coordinates down at all.

Walk-through · Part B

A tilted cut

Part B. Do Part B on paper first, then check in here.
Part A was symmetric, so a flat line worked. Now the Part B points: class \(-1\) (\(\circ\)) at \(x = 1, 2, 3\) and class \(+1\) (\(\bullet\)) at \(x = -1, 0, 4, 5\).
1. Lift them to \((x, x^2)\) and plot them on the Part B axes. Can a flat line \(x^2 = c\) separate them? Draw a tilted line that does.
2. A line in the lifted plane is \(x^2 = m\,x + c\). Where does it meet the parabola of lifted points? Explain why that gives two cuts on the number line, at the roots of \(x^2 - mx - c = 0\).
3. The natural cuts are halfway between neighbors: at \(x = 0.5\) and \(x = 3.5\). Find \(m\) and \(c\) so the roots are exactly 0.5 and 3.5. (Hint: \((x - 0.5)(x - 3.5) = 0\).)
4. Write your classifier as \(f(x) = w_1x + w_2x^2 + b\) with label \(+1\) when \(f(x) \ge 0\), and check it on all seven points (the table on the handout).

Goal: Tilt the line in the lifted plane to put the two cuts wherever you want them, and turn the line into a classifier \(f\). Part A’s flat line gave two cuts placed symmetrically around 0. A tilted line moves them. Part C does the same thing one dimension up, where lines become planes and cuts become circles.

Walk-through · Part C

Two rings in the plane

Part C. Do Part C on paper first, then check in here.
Now the plane itself: an inner ring (\(\bullet\), label \(+1\)) and an outer ring (\(\circ\), label \(-1\)). Inner: \(Q_1 (0.5, 0)\), \(Q_2 (0, 0.6)\), \(Q_3 (-0.4, -0.3)\), \(Q_4 (0.3, -0.4)\). Outer: \(R_1 (2, 0)\), \(R_2 (0, -2)\), \(R_3 (-1.6, 1.2)\), \(R_4 (1.2, 1.6)\).
1. Can any straight line separate the two rings? Explain in a sentence.
2. Lift each point \((x, y)\) to three coordinates, \((x,\ y,\ z)\) with \(z = x^2 + y^2\). Compute \(z\) for all eight points. What does \(z\) measure?
3. Sketch the lifted points in 3-D: the inner points sit low, the outer points sit high, on a bowl. Find a flat plane \(z = c\) that separates them, with the biggest margin in the \(z\) direction.
4. What does your plane \(z = c\) look like back in the original \((x, y)\) plane? Draw it on the figure.
5. A tilted plane, \(z = ax + by + c\), would also be a “straight” cut in 3-D. Show that back in the \((x, y)\) plane it is still a circle, just with a different center. (Complete the square.) So lines in the lifted space give circles in the plane.

Goal: Lift the plane onto a bowl, cut the bowl with a flat plane, and see the cut come back as a circle. Part A’s move, one dimension up: \(x^2\) became \(x^2 + y^2\), and a flat line became a flat plane. Wednesday’s lab runs an SVM on rings like these, and Part D shows how it can skip the lifting.

Walk-through · Part D

The kernel trick

Part D. Do Part D on paper first, then check in here.
The fact that saves us. Lifting can get expensive: with 5 car features, all the squares and products already give 20 coordinates, and the cubes too give 55. The fact from MML §12.3: the SVM never needs the lifted coordinates themselves, only the dot product \(\phi(\mathbf a)\cdot\phi(\mathbf b)\) of the lifted versions of every pair of points. A kernel is a shortcut that computes that dot product from the original points.
1. The 1-D lift from Parts A and B is \(\phi(x) = (x,\,x^2)\). Show that \(\phi(a)\cdot\phi(b) = ab + a^2b^2\). Check with \(a = 2\), \(b = 3\) both ways.
2. A 2-D lift: \(\phi(\mathbf a) = \bigl(a_1^2,\ \sqrt2\,a_1a_2,\ a_2^2\bigr)\) for \(\mathbf a = (a_1, a_2)\). Take \(\mathbf a = (1, 2)\) and \(\mathbf b = (3, 1)\). Compute \(\phi(\mathbf a)\), \(\phi(\mathbf b)\), and their dot product. Then compute \((\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b)^2\). What do you notice?
3. Show that \(\phi(\mathbf a)\cdot\phi(\mathbf b) = (\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b)^2\) for every \(\mathbf a, \mathbf b\) (expand \((a_1b_1 + a_2b_2)^2\)). Why is \((\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b)^2\) a cheaper way to get the same number? This is the polynomial kernel \(k(\mathbf a, \mathbf b) = (\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b)^2\), and using \(k\) instead of \(\phi\) is the kernel trick.
4. The RBF (Gaussian) kernel, with a dial \(\gamma > 0\): \(k(\mathbf a, \mathbf b) = e^{-\gamma\|\mathbf a - \mathbf b\|^2}\). Its lift has infinitely many coordinates. With \(\gamma = 1\), compute \(k\) for \(\mathbf a = \mathbf b\), for two points at distance 1, and for two points at distance 3. In words, what does \(k\) measure? Now redo distance 1 with \(\gamma = 10\). What does a big \(\gamma\) do to “nearby”?
5. An RBF SVM labels a new point by a weighted vote of the support vectors, each vote weighted by \(k(\text{support vector}, \text{new point})\). With a huge \(\gamma\), only the very nearest support vector gets a real vote. Sketch what the boundary around the two rings might look like for a small \(\gamma\) and for a huge one.
Skeptic. “The RBF kernel lifts to infinitely many dimensions, where any two classes can be separated. So the RBF SVM with a big \(\gamma\) and a big \(C\) is always the best classifier.” What would it do with a training point that has the wrong label, or that sits deep in the other class? Day 16 again: what goes wrong, and how will Wednesday’s lab choose \(C\) and \(\gamma\)?

Goal: Check that a kernel computes a lifted dot product without lifting, and read the RBF kernel as a measure of “nearby” with a dial. Parts A–C lifted by hand. Wednesday’s lab hands scikit-learn’s SVC a kernel instead, runs it on 300 points of rings, and tunes \(C\) and \(\gamma\) by cross-validation.
