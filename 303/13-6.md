# §13.6: Tangent Planes and Differentials

If you zoom in close enough on a smooth surface, it stops looking curved. It starts to look like a flat sheet that touches the surface at one point and matches its slope in every direction. That sheet is the **tangent plane**, and most of this section is about what you can do with it.

The payoff is practical. A function can be awkward to evaluate exactly, and measurements always carry small errors. A plane is easy to work with, so we replace the function near a point with its linearization. The total differential then turns that replacement into a rule for how small changes in the inputs move the output. It is the same idea behind the error estimates engineers make with formulas.

By the end of this section you should be able to:

1. Find the tangent plane and normal line to a level surface $$F(x,y,z)=k$$ using the gradient.
2. Write the tangent plane to $$z=f(x,y)$$ and recognize it as the linearization $$L(x,y)$$.
3. Find parametric equations for the tangent line to a curve where two surfaces meet.
4. Use the total differential $$dz$$ to estimate a change $$\Delta z$$, and bound the error that a measurement error can cause.
5. Explain why the tangent plane is the best linear approximation near a point, and say when that approximation breaks down.

### The tangent plane to a level surface

Write the surface as a level set $$F(x,y,z)=k$$. At a point $$P_0$$ on it, the gradient $$\nabla F(P_0)$$ is perpendicular to the surface, and that is what makes it the normal vector of the tangent plane. You can see why by differentiating. If $$\mathbf{r}(t)$$ is any curve lying on the surface through $$P_0$$, then $$F(\mathbf{r}(t))=k$$ for all $$t$$, and the chain rule gives $$\nabla F\cdot\mathbf{r}'=0$$ at $$P_0$$. Every direction you can walk on the surface is perpendicular to the gradient.

So the tangent plane through $$P_0$$ is

$$F_x(P_0)(x-x_0)+F_y(P_0)(y-y_0)+F_z(P_0)(z-z_0)=0$$

and the normal line runs along the gradient: $$x=x_0+F_x(P_0)t$$, $$y=y_0+F_y(P_0)t$$, $$z=z_0+F_z(P_0)t$$. The normal line is the line through $$P_0$$ that pierces the surface perpendicularly.

> **Worked example.** Find the tangent plane and normal line to $$x^2+3y^2+z=15$$ at $$(2,1,8)$$.
>
> Let $$F(x,y,z)=x^2+3y^2+z$$, so the surface is $$F=15$$. The point is on it, since $$4+3+8=15$$.
>
> Then $$\nabla F=\langle 2x,\,6y,\,1\rangle$$, and at $$(2,1,8)$$ this is $$\langle 4,6,1\rangle$$. That vector is the normal.
>
> The tangent plane is $$4(x-2)+6(y-1)+(z-8)=0$$, which simplifies to $$4x+6y+z=22$$. The normal line is $$x=2+4t$$, $$y=1+6t$$, $$z=8+t$$.

### Tangent planes to $$z=f(x,y)$$

If the surface is a graph, $$z=f(x,y)$$, rewrite it as the level surface $$F(x,y,z)=f(x,y)-z=0$$. Its gradient is $$\langle f_x,\,f_y,\,-1\rangle$$. The $$-1$$ in the last slot is never zero, so the tangent plane is never vertical and can be written as a graph over the $$xy$$-plane. Solving the tangent plane equation for $$z$$ gives

$$z=f(x_0,y_0)+f_x(x_0,y_0)(x-x_0)+f_y(x_0,y_0)(y-y_0)$$

The right-hand side is the **linearization** $$L(x,y)$$ of $$f$$ at $$(x_0,y_0)$$. The tangent plane and the linearization are the same object, and the demo below draws exactly that plane.

It has the same pattern as the single-variable tangent line $$y=f(a)+f'(a)(x-a)$$, with one term for each variable. Notice that the plane passes through $$(x_0,y_0,f(x_0,y_0))$$, because setting $$x=x_0$$ and $$y=y_0$$ leaves $$z=f(x_0,y_0)$$.

> **Worked example.** Find the tangent plane to $$z=\cos(xy)$$ at $$\left(\tfrac{\pi}{4},1,\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)$$.
>
> First check that the point is on the surface: $$\cos\left(\tfrac{\pi}{4}\right)=\tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$$. The partials are $$f_x=-y\sin(xy)$$ and $$f_y=-x\sin(xy)$$.
>
> At $$\left(\tfrac{\pi}{4},1\right)$$ they equal $$-\tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$$ and $$-\tfrac{\pi\sqrt{2}}{8}$$. So the tangent plane is $$z-\tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}=-\tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\left(x-\tfrac{\pi}{4}\right)-\tfrac{\pi\sqrt{2}}{8}(y-1)$$.

### Tangent lines to curves of intersection

Now suppose a curve is the intersection of two surfaces, $$F=c_1$$ and $$G=c_2$$. Its tangent vector must be perpendicular to both gradients at the point, so it is parallel to $$\nabla F\times\nabla G$$. That works because a vector perpendicular to two non-parallel vectors must be parallel to their cross product. Once you have a direction vector $$\langle a,b,c\rangle$$, the tangent line is $$x=x_0+at$$, $$y=y_0+bt$$, $$z=z_0+ct$$.

> **Worked example.** Find the tangent line at $$(1,1,1)$$ to the curve where $$z=\tfrac{x^2+y^2}{2}$$ meets $$z=4-x^2-2y^2$$.
>
> Use $$F=x^2+y^2-2z$$ and $$G=x^2+2y^2+z$$, so the curve lies on $$F=0$$ and $$G=4$$. The point is on both: $$F(1,1,1)=1+1-2=0$$ and $$G(1,1,1)=1+2+1=4$$.
>
> At $$(1,1,1)$$, $$\nabla F=\langle 2,2,-2\rangle$$ and $$\nabla G=\langle 2,4,1\rangle$$. Their cross product is $$\langle 10,-6,4\rangle$$, and $$\langle 5,-3,2\rangle$$ is a simpler direction. The tangent line is $$x=1+5t$$, $$y=1-3t$$, $$z=1+2t$$. Notice that this line lies in both tangent planes, since its direction is perpendicular to both gradients.

### Linear approximation

The linearization gives an estimate: for $$(x,y)$$ near $$(x_0,y_0)$$, $$f(x,y)\approx L(x,y)$$. The estimate is exact at the base point and gets worse as you move away from it. Close to the base point, the surface and the plane are nearly the same sheet, which is what the zoom panel in the demo shows. That is what lets you estimate function values without complicated calculations.

The idea extends to more variables. For $$f(x_1,\dots,x_n)$$ the linearization is $$L(\mathbf{x})=f(\mathbf{x}_0)+\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_0)\cdot(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_0)$$, so the gradient plays the role that the derivative plays in one variable. The estimate is only as good as the step is small, and the demo lets you watch how quickly it degrades.

> **Worked example.** Linearize $$f(x,y)=(x-1)^2-y+(y-1)^4$$ at $$(2,1)$$.
>
> First, $$f(2,1)=(1)^2-1+0=0$$. The partials are $$f_x=2(x-1)$$ and $$f_y=-1+4(y-1)^3$$, so $$f_x(2,1)=2$$ and $$f_y(2,1)=-1$$.
>
> Substitute into the linearization formula: $$L(x,y)=0+2(x-2)-(y-1)=2x-y-3$$. For example, at $$(2.1,0.9)$$ the estimate is $$L=0.3$$, while the true value is $$f(2.1,0.9)=0.3101$$.

### Differentials and error estimates

In one variable, if $$y=g(x)$$ then $$dy=g'(x)\,dx$$ is the change along the tangent line. For $$z=f(x,y)$$, the total differential is $$dz=f_x(x,y)\,dx+f_y(x,y)\,dy$$. Here $$dx$$ and $$dy$$ are actual nudges, $$\Delta x$$ and $$\Delta y$$, and $$dz$$ estimates the true change $$\Delta z=f(x+\Delta x,y+\Delta y)-f(x,y)$$. For the function in the last example, $$df=2(x-1)\,dx+\bigl(-1+4(y-1)^3\bigr)\,dy$$. In that example the error is $$\Delta x^2+\Delta y^4$$, which shrinks much faster than the step itself. That is why $$dz$$ is a good estimate for small steps and a poor one for large steps.

Panel 2 of the demo draws exactly this. The orange curve is the true height along a straight step from $$(a,b)$$, and the blue line is the plane's height along the same step.

The common mistake is treating $$dz$$ as a height. It is a change, so the plane's height at the new point is $$f(x_0,y_0)+dz$$, and the error of the estimate is $$\Delta z-dz$$.

The same tool bounds errors in measured quantities. If $$Q=f(x_1,\dots,x_n)$$ and each measured input carries a small error, then the error in $$Q$$ is about $$dQ$$, and the worst case adds up the sizes of the individual contributions. The relative error is $$dQ/Q$$, and the percentage error is $$100\,dQ/Q$$. Engineers use this to see which input a formula is most sensitive to.

> **Worked example.** The radius of an oil drum is known to within 4%, and its height to within 1%. Bound the relative error in the volume $$V=\pi r^2h$$.
>
> Differentiate: $$dV=2\pi rh\,dr+\pi r^2\,dh$$. Dividing by $$V=\pi r^2h$$ gives $$\dfrac{dV}{V}=2\dfrac{dr}{r}+\dfrac{dh}{h}$$.
>
> The radius appears squared, so its relative error is multiplied by $$2$$. In the worst case the errors add: $$\left\lvert\dfrac{dV}{V}\right\rvert\le 2(4\%)+1\%=9\%$$. This is an upper bound: if the two errors have opposite signs, the true error is smaller.

### Practice & reflection

1. What information about $$f$$ does the linearization use at $$(x_0,y_0)$$, and what does it ignore? Why does the estimate get worse as you move away from $$(x_0,y_0)$$?
2. In the example with $$f(x,y)=(x-1)^2-y+(y-1)^4$$, the error of the estimate from $$(2,1)$$ is $$\Delta x^2+\Delta y^4$$ for any step. Why can the plane never over-estimate that function there? What would the surface have to do for the plane to over-estimate?
3. The graph of $$z=\lvert x\rvert$$ is a sharp fold along the $$y$$-axis. Picture zooming in on a point on that fold: what happens to the picture, and why can no single plane serve as its linear approximation?
4. In the oil drum example, why does an error in the radius count twice as much as an error in the height? How would that change the way you measure a cylinder?
5. In the linearization example, the curvature in the $$y$$-direction comes from $$(y-1)^4$$, and the curvature in the $$x$$-direction comes from $$(x-1)^2$$. Zoom in on the demo's first panel. Why does the surface flatten in the $$y$$-direction much faster than in the $$x$$-direction?
