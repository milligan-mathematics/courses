Walk-through · Part A

One point, two directions

Part A. Do Part A on paper first, then check in here.
The data. The four centered points from Day 18: \(\mathbf x_1=(2,1)\), \(\mathbf x_2=(-2,-1)\), \(\mathbf x_3=(1,2)\), \(\mathbf x_4=(-1,-2)\), with covariance matrix \(S=\begin{pmatrix}2.5&2\\2&2.5\end{pmatrix}\), eigenvalues 4.5 and 0.5, and top eigenvector \(\mathbf b_1=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,1)\).
Words for today. Squash a centered point \(\mathbf x\) onto the line through a unit vector \(\mathbf u\): its code is one number, \(z=\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf x\); its reconstruction is the point \(\tilde{\mathbf x}=z\,\mathbf u\) on the line; what’s lost is the leftover \(\mathbf x-\tilde{\mathbf x}\).
1. Take \(\mathbf x_1=(2,1)\) and \(\mathbf u=(1,0)\) (the \(0^\circ\) line). Find the code \(z\), the reconstruction \(\tilde{\mathbf x}\), and the leftover. Draw them on the figure.
2. Repeat with \(\mathbf u=\mathbf b_1=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,1)\) (the \(45^\circ\) line). Keep \(\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\) exact as long as you can.
3. In both cases compute \(z^2\), \(\|\mathbf x_1-\tilde{\mathbf x}\|^2\), and their sum. Compare the sum with \(\|\mathbf x_1\|^2\).
4. Why does that happen? (Day 9: the leftover is perpendicular to the line. What theorem about right triangles applies?)

Goal: Squash one point onto two lines, check its code, reconstruction and leftover, and see why kept plus lost is always the point’s squared length. This is Day 9’s projection onto a line, with new names: code, reconstruction, leftover. Part B runs it on all four points and four lines, and Part A’s sum becomes the shortcut that fills the table.

Walk-through · Part B

Kept and lost, for all four points

Part B. Do Part B on paper first, then check in here.
For each direction, fill in the codes of all four points. Then the variance kept is the average of \(z_n^2\) (the codes average to 0, so this really is their variance), and the average loss is the average of \(\|\mathbf x_n-\tilde{\mathbf x}_n\|^2\). Use Part A’s discovery to get the losses quickly: notice that \(\|\mathbf x_n\|^2\) is the same for all four points.
The directions: \(0^\circ\), \(\mathbf u=(1,0)\); \(45^\circ\), \(\mathbf u=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,1)\); \(90^\circ\), \(\mathbf u=(0,1)\); \(135^\circ\), \(\mathbf u=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(-1,1)\). The table’s columns: \(z_1, z_2, z_3, z_4\), variance kept, average loss, kept + loss.
1. Which direction keeps the most variance? Which direction has the smallest average loss?
2. The last column is the same in every row. Which number from \(S\) is it? (MML calls it the total variance.)
3. Finish the argument in one sentence: “Since kept \(+\) lost is the same for every direction, making the variance kept as big as possible is the same as ________.”
4. Day 18’s formula says the variance kept along \(\mathbf u=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\) is \(2.5+2\sin2\theta\). Write a formula for the average loss, and check it against your \(135^\circ\) row.
Skeptic. “That only worked because all four points happen to have \(\|\mathbf x_n\|^2=5\). With messy data, the direction of most variance and the direction of least loss could be different.” Is that right? (Part A’s Pythagoras holds for every point, whatever its length. Average it over the points. What does kept \(+\) lost equal for any centered data?)

Goal: Fill in the kept-and-lost table for four directions, and see why the direction that keeps the most variance must be the one that loses the least. Part A split one point’s \(\|\mathbf x\|^2\) into kept and lost. Averaged over the data, the same split says MML’s two stories of PCA (§10.2, most variance; §10.3, least loss) pick the same line. Part C finds that line inside the SVD.

Walk-through · Part C

Meet the SVD

Part C. Do Part C on paper first, then check in here.
Stack the four centered points as the rows of a \(4\times2\) data matrix \(X\). Every matrix, of any shape, can be written as \(X=U\Sigma V^{T}\): a turn, a stretch, and another turn (MML Theorem 4.22). For a matrix with two columns, that is the same as a sum of two simple pieces:\[X=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\-2&-1\\1&2\\-1&-2\end{pmatrix}=\sigma_1\,\mathbf u_1\mathbf v_1^{T}+\sigma_2\,\mathbf u_2\mathbf v_2^{T},\]\(\sigma_1=3\sqrt2\approx4.24\), \(\mathbf v_1=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,1)\), \(\mathbf u_1=\tfrac12(1,-1,1,-1)\); \(\sigma_2=\sqrt2\approx1.41\), \(\mathbf v_2=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,-1)\), \(\mathbf u_2=\tfrac12(1,-1,-1,1)\).
The numbers \(\sigma_1\ge\sigma_2\) are the singular values; the \(\mathbf v\)’s (in \(\mathbb R^2\)) and the \(\mathbf u\)’s (in \(\mathbb R^4\)) are the right and left singular vectors. (These \(\mathbf u\)’s are a new use of the letter: they are not Part B’s directions.)
1. Check that \(\mathbf v_1,\mathbf v_2\) are unit vectors and perpendicular, and that \(\mathbf u_1,\mathbf u_2\) are too.
2. Compute \(X\mathbf v_1\) (one dot product per row). Compare it with \(\sigma_1\mathbf u_1\), and with the \(45^\circ\) codes in your Part B table. In words: what does multiplying the data matrix by \(\mathbf v_1\) compute?
3. Compare \(\mathbf v_1\), \(\mathbf v_2\) with the eigenvectors of \(S\). Compare \(\sigma_1^2/4\) and \(\sigma_2^2/4\) with the eigenvalues of \(S\). (Here \(N=4\).)
4. Compute the first piece, the \(4\times2\) matrix \(\sigma_1\mathbf u_1\mathbf v_1^{T}\) (an outer product: entry \((i,j)\) is \(\sigma_1\cdot(\mathbf u_1)_i\cdot(\mathbf v_1)_j\)). Compare its rows with the reconstructions \(\tilde{\mathbf x}_n\) on the \(45^\circ\) line. Then add the second piece to row 1 and check that you get \(\mathbf x_1=(2,1)\) back.
So the first piece of the SVD is PCA with one component: keep it, and every point is squashed onto the best line. The second piece is exactly what was lost.

Goal: Check the SVD of the data matrix piece by piece, and recognize PCA inside it: the codes, the eigenvalues, and the reconstructions. Parts A and B squashed the points onto the \(45^\circ\) line by hand. The SVD does all of it in one factorization, for any matrix, which is how the computer does PCA. Part D uses it to compress pictures.

Walk-through · Part D

Why this matters on Wednesday

Part D. Do Part D on paper first, then check in here.
A grayscale photo is a matrix of brightness numbers: a \(512\times512\) photo has 262,144 of them. Its SVD has 512 pieces \(\sigma_i\mathbf u_i\mathbf v_i^{T}\), with the \(\sigma_i\) in decreasing order. Keeping only the first \(k\) pieces gives a picture built from \(k\) columns \(\mathbf u_i\), \(k\) rows \(\mathbf v_i\), and \(k\) numbers \(\sigma_i\). The Eckart–Young theorem (MML Theorem 4.25) says no matrix of rank \(k\) is closer to the original.
1. How many numbers do you need to store for \(k\) pieces of a \(512\times512\) photo? What fraction of 262,144 is that for \(k=20\)? For \(k=100\)?
2. For our \(X\): keeping one piece out of two throws away \(\sigma_2\mathbf u_2\mathbf v_2^{T}\). Find the sum of the squares of its eight entries, and compare it with \(\sigma_2^2\) and with \(4\times\)(your smallest average loss in Part B).
Skeptic. “The SVD is just eigenvectors with a new name.” Give one way that’s wrong. (What shape is \(X\)? Can a \(4\times2\) matrix have an eigenvector?) And one way it’s right: compute \(X^{T}X\) and compare it with \(S\).

Goal: Count what a compressed photo costs, check Eckart–Young on our tiny \(X\), and sort out what the SVD shares with eigenvectors and what it adds. Part C found PCA inside the SVD. Wednesday’s lab (Day 22) takes the SVD of a real \(512\times512\) photo and keeps \(k\) pieces; today’s count says what that saves, and Eckart–Young says no rank-\(k\) picture does better.
