Instant Replay

What we found with two goals, and the idea that carries forward.

The teams are evenly matched, so every goal is 50–50. With exactly two goals there are four match stories, the order in which the goals were scored:

2–0
BB
1 of 4 stories = ¼
1–1
BWWB
2 of 4 stories = ½
0–2
WW
1 of 4 stories = ¼
The big idea When every goal is 50–50, every match story is equally likely. So the probability of a final score is
(number of stories that end with that score) ÷ (number of possible stories)
For the rest of today, the job is counting stories.

The Scoreboard Triangle

Stack the story counts. A pattern shows up.

Each row lists how many stories end with each score, from an all-Buffaloes blowout on the left to an all-Wildebeest blowout on the right. You've already found rows 2, 3, and 4. Row 0 is the match where nobody scores: one story, 0–0.

Fill in rows 5 and 6. Hint: look at how each number in row 4 relates to the two numbers just above it. Click any finished number to see why it works.

Why does it work? Click a number in the triangle (row 1 or below).

Mathematicians in India, Persia, and China worked with this triangle centuries before Blaise Pascal wrote about it in 1654. Today it's usually called Pascal's triangle.

Choosing the Goals

Counting stories without listing them: combinations.

A six-goal thriller ends 4–2 for the Buffaloes. A story is set once you know which four of the six goals were Buffaloes goals. Click goals to choose four, then record the story.

Counting without listing

Pick the Buffaloes' four goals one at a time, as if you were filling a security code with no repeats:

But that counts the same story many times. Picking goal 5, then 1, then 6, then 2 gives the same story as picking 1, 2, 5, 6. In how many different orders could you pick the same four goals?

So the number of 4–2 stories is
Combinations The number of ways to choose k goals out of n, when order doesn't matter, is written C(n, k) or “n choose k”:
C(6, 4) = (6·5·4·3) ÷ (4·3·2·1) = 360 ÷ 24 = 15
Ordered picks on top, the number of orders of the same picks on the bottom. Find 15 in row 6 of your triangle.

Teacher's lens, the Chapter 16 connections, and your match report.

Teacher's lens

Grade 7 standard 7.SP.8b asks students to represent sample spaces for compound events with organized lists, tables, and tree diagrams. Combinations and Pascal's triangle aren't part of the grade 7 standards, but the reasoning behind them is.

A 7th grader listing four-goal stories keeps finding 13 or 14 and missing the rest. What organizing strategy would you suggest, and what question would you ask instead of giving them the list?
The handout's tree diagram has 4 leaves for two goals. How many would it have for ten goals? When should a student stop drawing and start reasoning? What should they reason with?
