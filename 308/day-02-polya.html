How these work. Each walk-through takes one exercise through Pólya’s four phases. You answer one step at a time and get feedback right away; when something isn’t right, read why and try again. Hints are there when you ask for them. Your place is saved in this browser, so you can stop and come back.

The walk-throughs end in full solutions, so the work you present in class should still be your own, in your own words.

Pólya walk-through · Exercise 2.4.10

Add one term, then fold

Exercise 2.4.10. Prove that \(\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{6} + \cdots + \frac{1}{n(n + 1)} = \frac{n}{n + 1}\) for \(n \in \mathbb N\).

Goal: Set up an induction proof, and carry out its inductive step, so the hypothesis does the work and the algebra stays short. It uses the First Principle of Mathematical Induction (Principle 2.1.1). Every induction proof in the course has this two-part shape: a base case, then a step from \(k\) to \(k + 1\).

Pólya walk-through · Exercise 2.4.15 (a, f)

The Euclidean algorithm, run backward

Exercise 2.4.15 (a, f). For each of the following pairs of numbers \(a\) and \(b\), calculate \(\gcd(a,b)\) and find integers \(r\) and \(s\) such that \(\gcd(a,b) = ra + sb\).
(a) \(14\) and \(39\)
(f) \(-4357\) and \(3754\)

Goal: Compute a gcd with the Euclidean algorithm, then work the steps backward to write it as a combination of the two numbers. It uses Theorem 2.2.2 and the method of Example 2.2.4, which also notes that \(r\) and \(s\) are not unique.

Pólya walk-through · Exercise 2.4.29

Primes of the form 6n + 5

Exercise 2.4.29. Prove that there are an infinite number of primes of the form \(6n + 5\).

Goal: Adapt Euclid’s argument to one family of primes, and use remainders on division by \(6\) to make sure the prime you find belongs to that family. It adapts Euclid’s proof (Theorem 2.2.6), and it uses the division algorithm (Theorem 2.2.1) to sort primes by their remainder on division by \(6\).
