Paper first, then check in. This page goes with the Day 9 handout, one card per Part. Do Part A on paper, then open the Part A card and tell it what you got: numbers, vectors, matrices. If something is off, the page tries to name the slip, and hints are there when you want them (each one a little more specific). Then on to Part B, and so on.

The paper is what you turn in. You’ll see answers here as you check your work. That’s on purpose: they’re for checking, not copying. Your handout, with your own reasoning, is what goes on Canvas, along with the two Skeptic answers and the reading sentence.

Typing tips: a vector is a column of boxes, one entry per box. Decimals like 6.25, fractions like 25/4 and roots like sqrt(2) all work. Your place is saved in this browser, so you can stop and come back.

Walk-through · Part A

Lengths, angles, right angles

Part A. Do Part A on paper first, then check in here.
For vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^n\): the dot product is \(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=u_1v_1+\cdots+u_nv_n\), the length is \(\|\mathbf{u}\|=\sqrt{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{u}}\), and the angle \(\theta\) between \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\) satisfies \(\cos\theta = \dfrac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{u}\|\,\|\mathbf{v}\|}\).
1. Let \(\mathbf{u}=(3,4)\), \(\mathbf{v}=(4,-3)\), \(\mathbf{w}=(7,1)\). Find \(\|\mathbf{u}\|\), \(\|\mathbf{v}\|\), and \(\|\mathbf{w}\|\).
2. Find the angle between \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\), and between \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{w}\).
3. Two vectors are orthogonal (perpendicular) when their dot product is 0. Which pair above is orthogonal? Notice that \(\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}\). Check that \(\|\mathbf{w}\|^2=\|\mathbf{u}\|^2+\|\mathbf{v}\|^2\). What famous theorem is this?
4. Expand \(\|\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}\|^2=(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v})\cdot(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v})\) to explain why that theorem works for orthogonal vectors in any number of dimensions, and fails when \(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}\neq0\).

Goal: Check your lengths and angles, and see the Pythagorean theorem as a fact about dot products, in any dimension. Everything today rests on one move: a right angle turns a squared length into a sum of squared lengths. Part B uses it to find the closest point on a line, and Part D to prove least squares is best.

Walk-through · Part B

Project onto a line

Part B. Do Part B on paper first, then check in here.
Take the line through the origin in the direction \(\mathbf{u}=(2,1)\), and the point \(\mathbf{v}=(1,3)\) off the line. Which point of the line is closest to \(\mathbf{v}\)?
1. Points on the line are \(t\,\mathbf{u}=(2t,\,t)\). Fill in the table of squared distances \(\|\mathbf{v}-t\mathbf{u}\|^2\) from \(\mathbf{v}\) for \(t = 0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2\). Which \(t\) wins? What pattern do you see?
2. Let \(\mathbf{p}\) be the winning point and \(\mathbf{e}=\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{p}\) the leftover. Draw \(\mathbf{u}\), \(\mathbf{v}\), \(\mathbf{p}\), and \(\mathbf{e}\) on the grid. Compute \(\mathbf{e}\cdot\mathbf{u}\). What is the angle between the leftover and the line?
3. The rule. The closest point is the one where the leftover is perpendicular to the line. Write \(\mathbf{p}=\lambda\mathbf{u}\) and set \((\mathbf{v}-\lambda\mathbf{u})\cdot\mathbf{u}=0\). Solve for \(\lambda\) to get \(\lambda=\dfrac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{u}}\). Check that it gives your winning \(t\). (MML writes this as equation (3.40), projecting \(\mathbf{x}\) onto \(\mathbf{b}\).)
4. Use Part A’s theorem to explain the pattern in your table: why is \(\|\mathbf{v}-t\mathbf{u}\|^2=\|\mathbf{e}\|^2+\|\mathbf{p}-t\mathbf{u}\|^2\) for every \(t\)?
Skeptic. “The closest point is just the point of the line straight below \(\mathbf{v}\), at \((1,\,0.5)\).” Find that point’s squared distance. What is wrong with “straight below”?

Goal: Find the point of a line nearest a given point, three ways: a table, a right angle, and a formula, and see why they agree. Part A said a right angle splits a squared length into two. Here that turns “closest point” into one equation, \(\mathbf e\cdot\mathbf u = 0\). Part C does the same with a plane, and solves Day 6.

Walk-through · Part C

Project onto a plane: Day 6 solved

Part C. Do Part C on paper first, then check in here.
Back to Day 6. The columns of \(A\) are \(\mathbf{a}_1=(1,1,1)\) and \(\mathbf{a}_2=(0,1,2)\), they span a plane in \(\mathbb{R}^3\), and the data is \(\mathbf{b}=(6,0,0)\). Same rule as Part B: the closest point \(\mathbf{p}=c\,\mathbf{a}_1+m\,\mathbf{a}_2\) is the one whose leftover \(\mathbf{e}=\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{p}\) is perpendicular to the plane, which means perpendicular to both columns.
1. Write the two conditions \(\mathbf{a}_1\cdot(\mathbf{b}-c\,\mathbf{a}_1-m\,\mathbf{a}_2)=0\) and \(\mathbf{a}_2\cdot(\mathbf{b}-c\,\mathbf{a}_1-m\,\mathbf{a}_2)=0\) as two equations in \(c\) and \(m\). (You need \(\mathbf{a}_1\cdot\mathbf{a}_1\), \(\mathbf{a}_1\cdot\mathbf{a}_2\), \(\mathbf{a}_2\cdot\mathbf{a}_2\), \(\mathbf{a}_1\cdot\mathbf{b}\), \(\mathbf{a}_2\cdot\mathbf{b}\).)
2. Solve them. Which of Day 6’s five lines did you get?
3. Compute \(\mathbf{p}\) and \(\mathbf{e}\). Compare \(\mathbf{e}\) with Day 6’s normal vector \(\mathbf{n}=(1,-2,1)\). Why had it to come out a multiple of \(\mathbf{n}\)?
4. The matrix version. Compute \(A^{\mathsf T}A\) and \(A^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{b}\). Check that your two equations are exactly \(A^{\mathsf T}A\,\mathbf{x}=A^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{b}\), the normal equations (MML (3.56)). Explain in words why: what is each entry of \(A^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{e}\)?
5. The entries of \(\mathbf{e}\) add up to 0. Explain why that is guaranteed for any least-squares line with an intercept. (Hint: which column is \((1,1,1)\)?)

Goal: Find Day 6’s best line exactly, with no guessing, from two perpendicularity conditions, and recognize them as the normal equations. Part B projected onto a line with one dot product condition. A plane needs two, one per column, and they land on Day 6’s line (iv). Part D proves it beats every line there is.

Walk-through · Part D

Why perpendicular means best

Part D. Do Part D on paper first, then check in here.
Let \(\mathbf{q}\) be any other point of the plane (the predictions of some other line). Fill in the reasons:
1. \(\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{q}=(\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{p})+(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q})=\mathbf{e}+(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q})\). The vector \(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}\) lies in the plane because ________.
2. So \(\mathbf{e}\perp(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q})\), and by ________, \(\|\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{q}\|^2=\|\mathbf{e}\|^2+\|\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}\|^2\).
3. Therefore \(\|\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{q}\|^2\ \geq\) ____, with equality only when ____.
4. Check it with Day 6’s line (ii), \(y=6-3t\), whose predictions are \(\mathbf{q}=(6,3,0)\). Compute \(\|\mathbf{e}\|^2\) and \(\|\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}\|^2\), add them, and compare with line (ii)’s score in your Day 6 table.
The big picture. Nothing here used 3 points or 2 parameters. For 392 cars and 6 parameters, \(\mathbf{b}\) lives in \(\mathbb{R}^{392}\), the predictions \(A\mathbf{x}\) fill a 6-dimensional “plane” inside it, and the best fit is still the foot of the perpendicular, found by solving \(A^{\mathsf T}A\,\mathbf{x}=A^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{b}\). On Wednesday the computer does exactly that.
Skeptic. The normal equations need \(A^{\mathsf T}A\) to be invertible. Suppose an engineer gives you a data table with weight in pounds and weight in kilograms as two columns. What goes wrong with the plane, and with the answer? (Try \(\mathbf{a}_2=(0,1,2)\) and \(\mathbf{a}_3=(0,2,4)\).)
From the reading. MML §9.4 says the least-squares fit is the orthogonal projection of \(\mathbf{y}\) onto a certain subspace. In one sentence: which subspace, in Day 6’s language?

Goal: Prove that the foot of the perpendicular beats every other line, check it with numbers, and see what goes wrong when two columns say the same thing. Part C found line (iv) from two equations. Part A’s theorem now proves no line can do better, in any dimension. Wednesday’s lab (Day 10) hands the same normal equations to the computer.
