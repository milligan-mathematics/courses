Slice and Stack

§14.4 — cut a solid with a plane, read the area of the cross-section, and stack the slices to see why adding up the areas gives the volume.

Pick a solid and a direction. The solid is cut into n thin slabs, each as thick as Δt, perpendicular to the axis you chose. The orange sheet is the slice at position t0, and its area is A(t0). A slab has volume about A(t) Δt, so the stack has volume Σ A(tk) Δt. The graph underneath shows the same bars. That sum is a Riemann sum for ∫ A(t) dt, and that is the triple integral of 1 over the solid. Drag inside the scene to orbit it.

Each slab is drawn using the slice at its middle. The orange slice is drawn on top of the stack so you can always see it. Slices very near the ends of the solid are too thin to draw, but they are counted in the readouts.

Things to try

  1. Pick the wedge, slice perpendicular to the x-axis. Before you move the slider, predict where along x the slice area is largest. Then check. The area is (2 − x)(x + 1), which is what you get from the first two integrals in the section page’s dz dy dx order.
  2. Same wedge, now slice perpendicular to the z-axis. Predict what the graph of A(t) looks like: one smooth curve, or something with a corner? Then slide t0 across 1. Why does the corner sit exactly where the dx dz dy setup on the page needed two pieces?
  3. Pick the tetrahedron and slice perpendicular to any axis. Set n = 1, then 2, 4, 8. Predict whether the sum will be too big or too small for each, then compare with the exact volume 4.
  4. Pick the bowl. Predict the shape of the graph of A(t) for slices perpendicular to the z-axis (what is the area of a disk of radius √t?) and for slices perpendicular to the x-axis. Then check, and notice that the exact volume does not depend on the direction.
  5. Pick the half-ball. Predict which two of the three directions give slices of the same shape and the same area, and which one is different. Check all three.
