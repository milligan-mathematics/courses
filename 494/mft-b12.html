Paper first, then check in. Give yourself 15 minutes for the five problems on paper, no calculator, the way the real test runs. Then open each card. If you already have an answer, press Check my answer from paper: one try. Right, and you can skip to Look back for a faster route or a variation. Not right, or not sure, and the walk-through takes it one step at a time: Understand, Plan, Carry out, Look back.

Every wrong choice has a story. The test writers build the wrong answers out of the slips people actually make. When you pick one here, the page tells you which slip leads there, so you can spot it next time.

Walk-through · Set B12, Problem 1

An improper integral that telescopes

1. \(\displaystyle\int_1^{\infty}\frac{dx}{x(x+1)}=\)
(A) \(0\)
(B) \(\ln\frac{3}{2}\)
(C) \(\frac{1}{2}\)
(D) \(\ln 2\)
(E) The integral diverges.

Goal: Evaluate the improper integral as a limit, keeping the two logarithms together. Partial fractions plus a limit at infinity is a standard MFT integral. The trap of splitting into two divergent pieces shows up in series too (telescoping sums).

Walk-through · Set B12, Problem 2

When three vectors collapse

2. For which values of \(k\) is the set \(\{(1,2,k),\ (1,k,2),\ (1,1,1)\}\) linearly dependent in \(\mathbb{R}^3\)?
(A) \(k=0\) only
(B) \(k=2\) only
(C) \(k=1\) only
(D) \(k=-1\) and \(k=2\)
(E) \(k=0\) and \(k=2\)

Goal: Find every \(k\) that makes the determinant zero. “For which values of \(k\)” questions on the MFT are usually a determinant set to zero. Watch for the value that is easy to spot and the one that isn’t.

Walk-through · Set B12, Problem 3

Which combinations of ideals are ideals?

3. Let \(R\) be a commutative ring with identity, and let \(I\) and \(J\) be ideals of \(R\). Which of the following must also be ideals of \(R\)?
  I. \(I\cap J\)
  II. \(I+J=\{a+b: a\in I,\ b\in J\}\)
  III. \(I\cup J\)
(A) I only
(B) II only
(C) I and II only
(D) I and III only
(E) I, II, and III

Goal: Decide each statement by checking the ideal test or breaking it with ideals of \(\mathbb{Z}\). Ideals of \(\mathbb{Z}\) are the sets \(n\mathbb{Z}\), so \(\mathbb{Z}\) is the first place to look for counterexamples in ring theory. The same pattern (intersections and sums yes, unions no) holds for subgroups and subspaces.

Walk-through · Set B12, Problem 4

The best line through three points

4. An engineer fits a line \(y=mx+b\) by least squares to the three data points \((0,1)\), \((1,3)\), and \((2,4)\). What is the least-squares line?
(A) \(y=\frac{3}{2}x+1\)
(B) \(y=\frac{3}{2}x+\frac{7}{6}\)
(C) \(y=2x+1\)
(D) \(y=\frac{3}{2}x+\frac{4}{3}\)
(E) \(y=x+\frac{5}{3}\)

Goal: Find the slope and intercept that make the sum of squared vertical errors as small as possible. Least squares is where linear algebra meets data: the normal equations \(A^TA\mathbf{x} = A^T\mathbf{b}\) and the statistics formulas give the same line. The MFT asks it either way.

Walk-through · Set B12, Problem 5

Is it a topology?

5. Let \(X=\{a,b,c\}\). Which of the following collections of subsets of \(X\) is a topology on \(X\)?
(A) \(\{\varnothing,\ X,\ \{a\},\ \{b\}\}\)
(B) \(\{\varnothing,\ X,\ \{a,b\},\ \{b,c\}\}\)
(C) \(\{\varnothing,\ \{a\},\ \{b,c\}\}\)
(D) \(\{X,\ \{a\},\ \{a,b\}\}\)
(E) \(\{\varnothing,\ X,\ \{a\},\ \{a,b\}\}\)

Goal: Check each collection against the axioms for a topology and find the first one that fails. Point-set topology on the MFT is mostly definitions: topologies, open and closed sets, bases. Small finite spaces make the definitions concrete and quick to check.
