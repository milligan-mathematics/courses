# §11.2: Vectors and Vector Algebra

Every quantity you've measured so far in this course — a distance, a temperature, a radius — has been a single number. But plenty of quantities in the real world need more than a number to describe them. A gust of wind isn't just "15 mph"; it's 15 mph *out of the northwest*. A force isn't just "10 pounds"; it's 10 pounds pulling in a particular direction. To capture both the size of something and the direction it's pointing, we need a new kind of object: the **vector**. It's one of the simplest ideas in the course, and also one of the most useful — nearly everything else in multivariable calculus is built on top of it.

By the end of this section you should be able to:

1. Write a vector in component form given its initial and terminal points, and compute its magnitude.
2. Add, subtract, and scale vectors — both algebraically (with components) and geometrically (with pictures).
3. Express a vector using the standard basis vectors $$\mathbf{i}, \mathbf{j}, \mathbf{k}$$, and find the unit vector pointing in a given direction.

### What exactly is a vector?

Geometrically, a vector is just an arrow: it has an **initial point** (the tail) and a **terminal point** (the head, marked with the arrowhead), and it carries two pieces of information — how long it is (its **magnitude**) and which way it points (its **direction**). We write vectors in bold, like $$\mathbf{v}$$, or with a little arrow, $$\vec{v}$$. If the tail is at $$P$$ and the head is at $$Q$$, the vector is $$\overrightarrow{PQ}$$.

Here's the idea that trips people up at first: **a vector doesn't care where it's drawn.** Two arrows with the same length and the same direction are the *same vector*, even if they're sitting in completely different parts of the plane. Slide an arrow around without rotating or stretching it, and it's still the same vector. This is why we can always translate a vector to have its tail at the origin — doing so is called putting it in **standard position**, and it's usually the most convenient place for it to live.

That leads us to the algebraic side, which is what we'll actually compute with. If a vector runs from $$P = (x_1, y_1, z_1)$$ to $$Q = (x_2, y_2, z_2)$$, its **component form** is found by subtracting tail from head — always "terminal minus initial":

$$\mathbf{v} = \langle x_2 - x_1,\ y_2 - y_1,\ z_2 - z_1 \rangle$$

(Drop the third coordinate for the $$\mathbb{R}^2$$ version.) When a vector is in standard position, its components are simply the coordinates of its terminal point — that's the whole appeal of standard position.

The **magnitude** of $$\mathbf{v} = \langle a, b, c \rangle$$, written $$\lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert$$, is just the distance from tail to head, so it's the same Pythagorean formula from §11.1:

$$\lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}$$

Two special vectors round out the vocabulary: the **zero vector** $$\mathbf{0} = \langle 0,0,0 \rangle$$ has no length and no meaningful direction, and a **unit vector** is any vector with magnitude exactly $$1$$ — a "pure direction" with the size stripped away.

> **Example 1.** Find the component form and length of $$\mathbf{u}$$, given initial point $$P = (-1, 3, -2)$$ and terminal point $$Q = (0, 1, -5)$$.
>
> Subtract tail from head, coordinate by coordinate:
> $$\mathbf{u} = \langle 0-(-1),\ 1-3,\ -5-(-2) \rangle = \langle 1, -2, -3 \rangle$$
> Then the magnitude is just the square root of the sum of squares:
> $$\lVert \mathbf{u} \rVert = \sqrt{1^2 + (-2)^2 + (-3)^2} = \sqrt{1+4+9} = \sqrt{14}$$

### Adding, subtracting, and scaling vectors

Once vectors are in component form, arithmetic with them is refreshingly boring — you just do it coordinate by coordinate. The interesting part is what these operations *mean* geometrically.

**Scalar multiplication** ($$c\mathbf{v}$$) stretches or shrinks a vector without changing the line it lies on: multiply each component by $$c$$. If $$c>0$$ the direction is unchanged and the length scales by $$c$$; if $$c<0$$ the vector flips to point the opposite way; if $$c=0$$ you get the zero vector.

**Vector addition** ($$\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}$$) has a nice physical story: think of $$\mathbf{u}$$ as "walk this far in this direction," then $$\mathbf{v}$$ as "now walk *this* far in *that* direction." Your net displacement — from where you started to where you ended up — is $$\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}$$. Draw it by placing the tail of $$\mathbf{v}$$ at the head of $$\mathbf{u}$$; this is the **triangle rule**. Equivalently, if you instead place $$\mathbf{u}$$ and $$\mathbf{v}$$ tail-to-tail, their sum is the diagonal of the parallelogram they form — the **parallelogram rule**. Both pictures give exactly the same vector; they're just two ways of drawing the same addition.

**Vector subtraction** ($$\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}$$) is addition of a flipped vector, $$\mathbf{u} + (-\mathbf{v})$$, but it's worth knowing the direct picture too: place $$\mathbf{u}$$ and $$\mathbf{v}$$ tail-to-tail, and $$\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}$$ is the arrow running from the head of $$\mathbf{v}$$ to the head of $$\mathbf{u}$$.

| Operation | Component form | Picture |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Scalar multiple $$c\mathbf{v}$$ | $$\langle cv_1, cv_2, cv_3 \rangle$$ | scales the length by $$\lvert c \rvert$$; flips direction if $$c<0$$ |
| Sum $$\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}$$ | $$\langle u_1{+}v_1,\, u_2{+}v_2,\, u_3{+}v_3 \rangle$$ | tail of $$\mathbf{v}$$ to head of $$\mathbf{u}$$ (triangle), or the diagonal of the parallelogram |
| Difference $$\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}$$ | $$\langle u_1{-}v_1,\, u_2{-}v_2,\, u_3{-}v_3 \rangle$$ | arrow from the head of $$\mathbf{v}$$ to the head of $$\mathbf{u}$$ |

All of this behaves exactly the way your intuition hopes it does — addition commutes, scalars distribute over sums, and so on — so you can manipulate vector expressions with the same algebra habits you already have for numbers.

> **Example 2.** Let $$\mathbf{u} = \langle -2, 2, 4 \rangle$$ and $$\mathbf{v} = \langle 3, -5, 1 \rangle$$. Find **(a)** $$2\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}$$, and **(b)** the unit vector pointing in the same direction as $$\mathbf{u}$$.
>
> **(a)** Scale first, then add component-wise:
> $$2\mathbf{u} = \langle -4, 4, 8 \rangle \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad 2\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v} = \langle -4+3,\ 4-5,\ 8+1 \rangle = \langle -1, -1, 9 \rangle$$
>
> **(b)** First find the length of $$\mathbf{u}$$:
> $$\lVert \mathbf{u} \rVert = \sqrt{(-2)^2+2^2+4^2} = \sqrt{24} = 2\sqrt{6}$$
> Dividing $$\mathbf{u}$$ by its own length rescales it to have length exactly $$1$$ while leaving its direction alone:
> $$\hat{\mathbf{u}} = \frac{\mathbf{u}}{\lVert \mathbf{u} \rVert} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{6}}\langle -2, 2, 4 \rangle = \left\langle -\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}},\ \frac{1}{\sqrt{6}},\ \frac{2}{\sqrt{6}} \right\rangle$$

### Basis vectors, and going the other direction

There's one more piece of notation worth having in your toolkit. In $$\mathbb{R}^3$$, the three **standard basis vectors**

$$\mathbf{i} = \langle 1,0,0 \rangle,\qquad \mathbf{j} = \langle 0,1,0 \rangle,\qquad \mathbf{k} = \langle 0,0,1 \rangle$$

point one unit along each axis, and *every* vector is just a combination of them: $$\langle a,b,c \rangle = a\mathbf{i} + b\mathbf{j} + c\mathbf{k}$$. So $$\langle 3,-2,4 \rangle$$ and $$3\mathbf{i}-2\mathbf{j}+4\mathbf{k}$$ are two spellings of the exact same vector — pick whichever notation is more convenient for the problem in front of you.

The normalizing trick from Example 2 works for any nonzero vector: dividing $$\mathbf{v}$$ by its own magnitude, $$\hat{\mathbf{u}} = \mathbf{v}/\lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert$$, always produces a unit vector pointing the same way as $$\mathbf{v}$$. This is enormously useful whenever you care about a *direction* but want to factor the *size* out separately — which is exactly what happens constantly in physics: a velocity is a speed times a direction, a force is a strength times a direction, and vectors let you write that as literally one multiplication, $$\mathbf{v} = \lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert\, \hat{\mathbf{u}}$$.
