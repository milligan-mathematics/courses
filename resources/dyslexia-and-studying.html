Dyslexia and Studying in Higher Education

A Milligan Mathematics resource · open to anyone

A guide to understanding dyslexia and its relationship with studying in higher education. Dyslexia is not one thing — it comes in patterns, and which pattern you have changes which study techniques actually help.

How to use this guide

Reading isn't one skill. It's several, running at once, and dyslexia can hit any one of them while leaving the others fine. That's why two people with the same label can have completely different problems.

This page gives you eight patterns to try on. Each one takes about thirty seconds: read a few words, notice what your brain does, and mark whether it feels familiar. At the bottom, the strategies that fit what you marked get flagged.

Use the bar above to change the text size, spacing, typeface, and paper colour until this page is comfortable. That's not a gimmick — noticing which settings help is itself information about how you read. Your marks and settings are saved in this browser, so you can come back to them.

Start here: reading uses two roads

Almost everything in the research on dyslexia hangs off one idea. When you meet a written word, your brain can get to its sound and meaning by two different routes, running in parallel.

Road 1 is sounding out. You chop the word into letter-sounds and blend them. It's the only road that can handle a word you've never seen — including every technical term in your textbook the first time you meet it.

Road 2 is recognition. You've seen the word so many times that the whole shape maps straight to the sound. It's the only road that handles yacht, colonel, and Wednesday, which do not obey the rules.

Before those two roads even start, there's a stage that just works out which letters are there and in what order. Several of the patterns below live in that early stage rather than in either road — and those are the ones that cost the most in technical subjects, because a transposed digit or a dropped minus sign is a wrong answer, not a slow one.

What this page is not

These patterns come from research models of reading, not from a diagnostic manual. No clinician is going to hand you a report that says “letter position dyslexia.” What a real evaluation gives you is scores on the underlying skills — decoding, fluency, spelling, rapid naming, working memory, processing speed.

So use this page for what it's actually good for: getting precise language for your own experience, and picking study techniques you can start using this week. If you do pursue an evaluation, what you write down here will make you a much better historian of your own reading.

Eight patterns — try each one on

Read the sample out loud if you can, or at least sub-vocally. Speed doesn't matter. What matters is whether the description of the error feels like something that happens to you. Mark each one honestly — “sometimes” is a real and useful answer.

Pattern 1 · Road 1 is weak

New words don't assemble

Read these out loud. They're all fake, so there's nothing to recognise — Road 2 is no help.

blick   sare   tov   flig   glorp   chirn   plimth   strandle
The tell

It's slow and effortful in a way real words aren't. Or you find yourself producing a real word that looks similar — saying care or sore for sare, black for blick. That's Road 2 stepping in because Road 1 couldn't deliver. It's the single most common pattern in dyslexia.

Where this shows up in coursework

Every new technical term is a fake word the first time you see it. Eigenvector. Holonomic. Enthalpy. Nyquist. Bernoulli. If Road 1 is weak, you're not slowly learning the vocabulary of your field — you're re-decoding it from scratch every single time, which is why reading the chapter is exhausting even when you understand the physics.

Pattern 2 · Road 2 is weak

Words that break the rules trip you

Read these out loud. Every one of them lies about how it's pronounced.

yacht   colonel   choir   island   Wednesday   subtle   gauge   buoy
The tell

You sound them out and get yatch-ett, co-lo-nel, eye-land, Wed-nes-day. That's Road 1 doing its job correctly on words that don't play fair, because Road 2 didn't recognise them fast enough. It usually shows up more in spelling than in reading.

Where this shows up in coursework

Your field is full of surnames that don't obey English spelling — Fourier, Laplace, Euler, Poisson, Reynolds, Navier — plus words like gauge and torque. If you can't recall a term's spelling reliably, you can't look it up, and spellcheck won't save you because none of it is in the dictionary.

Does this happen to you?
Pattern 3 · Before either road

The middle letters swap places

Read each pair. They differ only in the order of two middle letters.

diary / dairy   trial / trail   slime / smile
casual / causal   angel / angle   form / from
The tell

First and last letters are always right; the middle migrates. You read one word of the pair and get the other, or you have to stop and deliberately check. Researchers found the same error in deaf readers of fingerspelled words — so it isn't your eyes, it's the stage that encodes letter order.

Where this shows up in coursework

This is the expensive one. Angle / angel. Causal / casual. Form / from. And it doesn't stop at words: transposing digits when you copy a number (2.718 → 2.178), flipping subscripts (x₁₂ vs x₂₁), reversing matrix indices, swapping the order of a limit. You get the physics right and the arithmetic wrong, then can't find the error on re-reading — because you re-read it the same wrong way.

Does this happen to you?
Pattern 4 · Before either road

Neighbouring words trade letters

Read these two-word phrases at normal speed, without slowing down.

mild wind   cane love   fig tree   bent tank
The tell

You read wild mind, lane cove, tig free. The letters keep their position but jump to the wrong word. The giveaway: single words in isolation are fine, and it only goes wrong in running text. If you read better with a finger under the line or a card covering the rest of the page, this is probably why.

Where this shows up in coursework

Textbook pages are dense and equations are crowded — exactly the conditions that cause it. In a long expression you grab a symbol from the term next door, or pull a subscript off the neighbouring variable. Dense two-column PDFs are the worst case.

Does this happen to you?
Pattern 5 · Before either road

The front of the word goes missing

Read the left word of each pair. Then check what you actually said.

blend → lend   price → rice   strain → train
plate → late   shown → own   charm → harm
The tell

Letters drop off one end — nearly always the left — and what's left is still a real word. That's what makes this pattern invisible: nothing feels wrong, the sentence still parses, and you sail past it.

Where this shows up in coursework

A dropped left-hand character is a different quantity: −x read as x, sinh as sin, 10⁻³ as 10³, ≤ as <, mV as V. Same failure mode as the words: the result still looks like a legitimate expression, so nothing flags it.

Does this happen to you?
Pattern 6 · Before either road

The consonants hold, the vowels slide

Read down each group. The consonant frame never changes.

bit · bat · but · bet · boat
flux · flex   vector · victor   mass · moss · miss
The tell

You lock onto the consonant skeleton and the vowel becomes a guess filled in from context. Often the first thing people notice is in their own spelling — consonants right, vowels wrong.

Where this shows up in coursework

Mostly it costs you in written work and in variable naming. If you have flux and flex in the same derivation, or vel and val in the same function, you've built a trap for yourself.

Does this happen to you?
Pattern 7 · Speed, not accuracy

You get it right, it just costs too much

Time yourself. Say this row out loud as fast as you can, twice through, without errors.

7   3   9   2   5   8   1   4   6   3   9   7
2   8   5   1   6   4   9   3   7   2   8   5
The tell

You knew every digit instantly, but naming them in sequence at speed felt weirdly effortful. Researchers call this rapid naming, and it's an independent problem from sounding out — you can have one, the other, or both. Having both is the hardest combination, and it's the one most likely to have been missed at school, because your accuracy was never the problem.

Where this shows up in coursework

This is the accommodation case, full stop. If you are accurate but slow, then a timed exam measures your reading speed, not your grasp of the subject. It's also why you can be genuinely wiped out after forty minutes of reading that a classmate does in fifteen — you did the same work with a bigger engine.

Does this happen to you?
Pattern 8 · The one that hides

You understand it faster than you can read it

No sample for this one. Just read the list and count how many land.

  • You've worked out where a paragraph is going before you've finished decoding it.
  • Reading comprehension tests never flagged you — but reading aloud in class is something you'd pay to avoid.
  • Your spelling and handwriting don't match how you actually think.
  • You're flattened after forty minutes of reading in a way your friends aren't.
  • You quietly route around reading-heavy tasks and couldn't fully explain why.
  • You can explain a concept beautifully out loud and then write three laboured sentences about it.
  • Some part of you suspects you're getting away with something.
The tell

This is what dyslexia looks like when strong reasoning is covering for it — sometimes called stealth dyslexia. You're not reading the words; you're using vocabulary, context and inference to reconstruct the meaning, and it works. It works so well that standardised tests come back fine and nobody ever looked further.

The cost is that it takes enormous effort, all the time, invisibly. The mask slips in exactly two places: reading out loud, and spelling — because neither one lets you use context. If patterns 1–7 felt mild but this list felt pointed, this is your pattern.

Where this shows up in coursework

Technical and quantitative subjects reward the reasoning that's carrying you, so you can get a long way before the bill comes due — usually when reading volume jumps in upper-level courses, or when exams get tight enough that speed matters. And the mismatch between how well you think and how hard reading is has a way of turning into “I must be a fraud,” which is a much worse problem than the reading.

Does this sound like you?

Two things that often ride along

Working memory. If you lose the beginning of a long word by the time you reach the end, or lose the thread of a derivation between line three and line seven, that's the same limited buffer being spent on decoding instead of on the maths.

Attention. Somewhere between a third and a half of people with dyslexia also meet criteria for ADHD. If you avoid reading, it's worth knowing which kind of avoidance it is: dyslexia-avoidance is exhaustion — you'd keep going if you could. ADHD-avoidance is boredom — you can't stay on the page even when it's easy. If both descriptions fit, both are worth raising, because untreated attention problems will sabotage any reading strategy you try.

Strategies

Everything here is usable. The ones matching what you marked are flagged in the margin — start with those, and pick two. Not eight. Two that you'll actually do for a month.

Start with these, whatever you marked

Everyone
  • Stop re-reading. Start blank-paging. After a section, shut the book and write what you remember on blank paper. Then check. Re-reading is the most expensive thing you can do and one of the least effective for anyone — for you it's doubly wasteful. Recall costs almost no reading and is the best-evidenced study technique there is.
  • Use worked examples as your main diet. Read a fully solved problem. Then redo it with the solution covered. Then do a new one. Going straight to unsolved problems burns the working memory you need for the concept.
  • Draw it. Free-body diagram, block diagram, concept map, circuit sketch. Spatial reasoning is usually a strength in this profile and a diagram holds information that would otherwise sit in your working memory.
  • Explain it out loud. To a friend, a rubber duck, or your phone's voice recorder. Talking is almost certainly your strongest channel — use it for the part of studying that's actually about understanding.
  • Read before lecture, not after. Even a bad ten-minute skim means lecture is your second exposure. That single change does more for a slow reader than any note-taking system.
  • Get the textbook in a format you control. An EPUB or accessible PDF lets you change the font, size and spacing and turn on read-aloud. Your disability services office can request accessible formats from publishers, and Bookshare has a large academic catalogue. Ask — this is routine.

Build a pronunciation sheet before the chapter

Matches your answers
  • Before you read a chapter, pull the new terms out of the summary or index — usually ten to fifteen. Get each one said out loud (text-to-speech, YouTube, or ask your professor) and say it back three times.
  • Write them on one sheet with the pronunciation spelled out your way: Euler (OY-ler), Bernoulli (ber-NOO-lee), Navier (nav-YAY), enthalpy (EN-thal-pee). Never meet a technical word for the first time in print.
  • Learn the parts, not the words. iso- same, -tropic direction, homo- same, poly- many, -morphic shape, co- together, -variant changing with. Then isotropic is two known chunks instead of nine unknown letters. This is the adult version of structured phonics, and it's the highest-leverage thing on this page for technical vocabulary.
  • Add your terms to your phone's or Word's autocorrect list so the spelling stops being your problem.

Let the machine do the decoding

Matches your answers
  • Use text-to-speech with your eyes on the same text — not audio alone. Hearing and seeing together is far better than either one, because the audio carries the decoding while your eyes learn the word shapes. Microsoft Edge's Read Aloud and Word's Read Aloud are free and already on your machine; Speechify and NaturalReader do more.
  • Push the speed up over a few weeks. Most people end up comfortable well above normal speaking pace, and that's when it stops feeling like a compromise and starts feeling like an advantage.
  • Record lectures (ask first) and get a transcript — Otter, Word's transcribe, or your phone. Then you can search the lecture instead of re-reading your notes.
  • For maths in a PDF, screenshot into Mathpix to get clean LaTeX you can enlarge and re-space. Fighting a fixed, tiny, two-column PDF is a losing game.

Make copying errors impossible to hide

Matches your answers
  • Check numbers right-to-left. Read a copied number backwards, digit by digit. Reading forwards lets whole-word recognition smooth over a transposition; backwards it can't, so the error has nowhere to hide. Same for a copied expression: check it term by term from the right.
  • Say subscripts and indices out loud every time. Pick one phrasing and never vary it — always “x sub one two, row one column two.” Speech is sequential, so it catches order errors that your eye slides past.
  • Copy a number once. If a value appears three times in a solution, define it as a symbol on line one and use the symbol. Every re-copy is a fresh chance to transpose.
  • Carry units on every line. Units are a free error-checker: a dropped prefix or exponent breaks the dimensions and shows up immediately. This is the cheapest habit here and it catches the pattern-5 errors that otherwise look fine.
  • Run a sign-and-exponent pass. Before you box an answer, do one deliberate scan for only minus signs, exponents, prefixes and inequality directions. A separate pass, not a feeling — because these errors don't feel like anything.
  • Read your final answer aloud in words, including the sign and the units. “Negative three point two times ten to the minus four newtons.” You'll hear what you didn't see.

Give the page less to crowd with

Matches your answers
  • Mask the page. An index card under the line, or the line guide at the top of this page. If it helps here, it'll help in your textbook — that's a real finding about you, not a placebo.
  • Open up the spacing. More space between letters and lines measurably reduces crowding errors. In anything you control — your own notes, a Word doc, an EPUB — go to 1.5 or double spacing and bump the letter spacing slightly.
  • Work equations one term at a time. Cover the rest of the line with your hand or a card. Long expressions are exactly the crowded condition that causes letters and symbols to migrate.
  • Re-copy dense equations onto your own paper, big and spread out, before you work with them. The copy is worth the two minutes.
  • Avoid reading dense two-column PDFs at 100%. Zoom until a line is short, or reflow it.

Get the accommodations you're entitled to

Matches your answers
  • Go to disability services. Not because you can't cope — because a timed exam is currently measuring the wrong thing. Extended time (usually 1.5x), a low-distraction room, and permission to record lectures are standard, unremarkable, and not visible to anyone.
  • They'll want documentation. If you were ever tested in school, ask your parents or your old district for the report — it may still be on file. If not, ask disability services what they accept and who evaluates locally; some universities have funds for this.
  • If you need a new evaluation, the things worth asking to be tested are: phonological awareness, rapid naming, pseudoword decoding, reading fluency, spelling, working memory, and processing speed. Ask specifically that decoding be tested without context — that's where compensation stops working and the real picture shows up.
  • Timebox, don't marathon. Forty or fifty minutes then a real break. Your fatigue is a genuine load limit, and pushing through it produces reading you won't retain.
  • Do your first read of new material when you're fresh, and your problem sets when you're not. Never spend your best hours on your most expensive activity.

About the fraud feeling

Matches your answers
  • The gap between how fast you think and how slowly you read is not evidence that the thinking is fake. It's the actual, documented shape of this profile: strong reasoning, weak decoding, and the two are genuinely independent.
  • Reading slowly is not the same as understanding slowly. You already know this about other people; the work is believing it about yourself.
  • Using text-to-speech, extra time, or a recorded lecture isn't a crutch any more than wearing glasses is. You're removing an obstacle that has nothing to do with the subject so you can spend your effort on the subject itself.
  • Say it to one person — a professor, an advisor, a friend in the major. The isolation costs more than the reading does.

Two honest caveats

Coloured paper. This page lets you tint the background because glare and comfort are real, and if a tint makes you want to keep reading, that's a genuine benefit. But be clear-eyed: the evidence that coloured overlays actually improve reading in dyslexia is weak, and the “letters wobble” visual theory behind it is contested. Larger text and wider spacing have better support than colour does.

The subtype labels. Much of the subtype research comes from studying adults who lost reading ability after brain injury, then extended to developmental dyslexia. That extension is real research, but it's less settled than most summaries of it suggest. Trust the descriptions of the errors — those are things you can check against your own experience. Hold the labels loosely.

If you only take one thing

The thing that has been costing you the most is almost certainly not the reading itself — it's that you've been using reading as your study method. Reading and re-reading is the most expensive tool you own and one of the weakest for actually retaining anything.

Swap it. Read once, with whatever supports make that survivable. Then close the book and work: recall it, draw it, say it, solve it. That's a better way to study for anyone. For you it also happens to cut the price in half.
