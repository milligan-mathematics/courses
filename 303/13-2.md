# §13.2: Limits and Continuity of Multivariable Functions

In single-variable calculus, a limit asks what happens as $$x$$ approaches a number. There are only two ways to come in, from the left or from the right. In two variables there are infinitely many ways to approach a point: straight along a line, around a curve, or in any direction you like. A limit exists only when every one of those routes heads to the same value.

That extra freedom is why multivariable limits feel harder, and why they matter. Partial derivatives (§13.3), the chain rule (§13.4), and the search for maxima and minima (§13.7) all depend on a function behaving predictably near a point. Continuity is the name for that predictable behavior, and this section gives you the tools to check it.

By the end of this section you should be able to:

1. State the $$\varepsilon$$-$$\delta$$ definition of a limit of a function of two variables, and explain what it says about approaching from every direction.
2. Use the two-path test to show that a limit does not exist.
3. Evaluate limits algebraically with the Extended Limit Laws, and explain why cancelling a common factor is allowed.
4. Explain why checking paths can disprove a limit but can never prove one.
5. Decide where a polynomial, rational, or composed function is continuous.

### Limits in two variables and the two-path test

We write $$\lim_{(x,y)\to(a,b)} f(x,y) = L$$ when, for every $$\varepsilon > 0$$, there is a $$\delta > 0$$ such that $$\lvert f(x,y) - L \rvert < \varepsilon$$ whenever $$0 < \lVert (x,y) - (a,b) \rVert < \delta$$. In words: you can keep the outputs within $$\varepsilon$$ of $$L$$ by keeping the inputs within $$\delta$$ of $$(a,b)$$, no matter which direction they come from. The point $$(a,b)$$ itself is excluded, so $$f$$ does not have to be defined there.

The definition also gives you a fast test for failure. If two paths into $$(a,b)$$ make $$f$$ approach different values, no single $$L$$ can work for both, and the limit does not exist. Lines and axes are the usual paths to try first.

> **Worked example.** Show that $$\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)} \dfrac{x^2-y^2}{x^2+y^2}$$ does not exist.
>
> Along the $$x$$-axis, $$y = 0$$, so $$f(x,0) = \dfrac{x^2}{x^2} = 1$$ for $$x \neq 0$$. Along the $$y$$-axis, $$x = 0$$, so $$f(0,y) = \dfrac{-y^2}{y^2} = -1$$ for $$y \neq 0$$. The two paths approach $$1$$ and $$-1$$. Those are different, so the limit does not exist.

### Why paths can suggest a limit but never prove one

Suppose every path you try gives the same answer. That is evidence for a limit, but it is not proof. The most common mistake in this section is to check a few paths, see the same number each time, and declare the limit to exist.

> **Worked example.** Examine $$\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)} \dfrac{5x^2y}{x^2+y^2}$$ along three kinds of paths.
>
> Along the $$x$$-axis, $$f(x,0) = 0$$, so the limit along this path is $$0$$. Along the $$y$$-axis, $$f(0,y) = 0$$, so the limit along this path is $$0$$.
>
> Along $$y = mx$$, $$f(x,mx) = \dfrac{5mx^3}{x^2(1+m^2)} = \dfrac{5mx}{1+m^2} \to 0$$ as $$x \to 0$$, for every $$m$$.
>
> Every path gives $$0$$. That suggests the limit is $$0$$, but it does not prove it. A proof has to control every direction at once, and that is the job of the $$\varepsilon$$-$$\delta$$ definition.

Straight lines are not enough, either. A function can give the same value along every line through the origin and still have no limit, because a curved path such as a parabola can give a different answer. You will meet a function like that in the demo.

### Algebra first: the Extended Limit Laws

If $$f$$ is a polynomial, or a rational function whose denominator is not zero at the point, direct substitution finishes the job. When substitution gives $$\dfrac{0}{0}$$, algebra comes first: factor, rationalize, or cancel a common factor. The Extended Limit Laws are what make this work. If $$\lim f$$ and $$\lim g$$ both exist, then:

- the limit of a sum, difference, or constant multiple is the same combination of the limits;
- the limit of a product is the product of the limits;
- the limit of a quotient is the quotient of the limits, provided the limit of $$g$$ is not zero;
- the limit of $$f^{a/b}$$ is $$\left(\lim f\right)^{a/b}$$, as long as $$\lim f \ge 0$$ when $$b$$ is even.

Cancelling a common factor is legal because the limit only looks at points where that factor is not zero. The line where the factor vanishes does not matter.

> **Worked example.** Find $$\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)} \dfrac{xy-y^2}{\sqrt{x}-\sqrt{y}}$$.
>
> Direct substitution gives $$\dfrac{0}{0}$$. Factor the numerator as $$y(x-y)$$ and multiply top and bottom by the conjugate $$\sqrt{x}+\sqrt{y}$$:
>
> $$\dfrac{y(x-y)}{\sqrt{x}-\sqrt{y}} \cdot \dfrac{\sqrt{x}+\sqrt{y}}{\sqrt{x}+\sqrt{y}} = \dfrac{y(x-y)(\sqrt{x}+\sqrt{y})}{x-y} = y\left(\sqrt{x}+\sqrt{y}\right)$$
>
> The original quotient is undefined on the line $$x = y$$, and the limit never needs that line, so the cancellation is fine. Now substitute: $$0\left(\sqrt{0}+\sqrt{0}\right) = 0$$. The limit is $$0$$.

> **Worked example.** Find $$\lim_{(x,y)\to(1,4)} \dfrac{\sqrt{x+3}-\sqrt{y}}{x-y+3}$$.
>
> Direct substitution gives $$\dfrac{2-2}{0}$$, which is $$\dfrac{0}{0}$$. This time rationalize the numerator with $$\sqrt{x+3}+\sqrt{y}$$. The numerator becomes $$x+3-y$$, which is exactly the denominator $$x-y+3$$. Cancel, then substitute:
>
> $$\dfrac{\sqrt{x+3}-\sqrt{y}}{x-y+3} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{x+3}+\sqrt{y}} \quad\text{so the limit is}\quad \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{4}+\sqrt{4}} = \dfrac{1}{4}$$

### Continuity

A function $$f$$ is **continuous at** a point $$c$$ of its domain when $$\lim_{x\to c} f(x) = f(c)$$. That single equation packs in three requirements: $$f(c)$$ is defined, the limit exists, and the two values agree. Miss any one of them and $$f$$ is not continuous at $$c$$.

A few facts make checking much easier. Polynomials are continuous everywhere. Rational functions are continuous everywhere they are defined. Sums, differences, and products of continuous functions are continuous, and quotients are continuous wherever the denominator is nonzero. A composition of a continuous outer function with a continuous inner function is continuous.

> **Worked example.** Find where $$f(x,y,z) = \dfrac{1}{x^2+y^2+z^2-9}$$ is continuous.
>
> This is a rational function, so it is continuous wherever its denominator is nonzero. The denominator vanishes exactly on the sphere $$x^2+y^2+z^2 = 9$$ of radius $$3$$ centered at the origin. So $$f$$ is continuous everywhere except on that sphere. The origin is not on the sphere, so $$f$$ is continuous there, with $$f(0,0,0) = -\tfrac{1}{9}$$.

> **Worked example.** Examine the continuity of $$f(x,y,z) = e^{-3xy-4x^2}$$.
>
> Write $$f = g \circ h$$, where $$h(x,y,z) = -3xy-4x^2$$ is a polynomial and $$g(u) = e^u$$ is continuous for every real $$u$$. Both pieces are continuous everywhere, so their composition $$f$$ is continuous on all of $$\mathbb{R}^3$$.

### Practice & reflection

1. Explain why two paths with different limits are enough to show that a limit does not exist, while agreement along infinitely many paths is still not enough to show that it exists.
2. A classmate tests a function along the $$x$$-axis, the $$y$$-axis, and five lines through the origin. Every answer is $$0$$, so the classmate says the limit is $$0$$. Describe a family of paths the classmate should test next, and explain why lines alone can miss the problem.
3. A rational function can have a limit at a point where its denominator is zero. Explain why the function is still not continuous there, and what you would have to define to make it continuous.
4. Explain why a function that is continuous at $$(a,b)$$ must approach $$f(a,b)$$ along every path into $$(a,b)$$. What does that tell you about a quick way to show a function is not continuous?
