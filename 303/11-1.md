# §11.1: Three-Dimensional Cartesian Space

For centuries, mathematicians modeled the world with two dimensions: length and width. That's enough for graphing $$y = x^2$$ or finding the area of a region, but the real world has depth. This section extends the familiar $$(x, y)$$ plane to three-dimensional space, $$\mathbb{R}^3$$, where we'll spend the rest of the course.

By the end of this section you should be able to:

1. Locate points using an ordered triple $$(x, y, z)$$.
2. Visualize and graph basic equations and inequalities in 3D space.
3. Calculate the distance between two points in space.
4. Derive and use the equation of a sphere.

### Cartesian coordinates in three dimensions

Just as an ordered pair $$(x,y)$$ locates a point in the plane, an ordered triple $$(x, y, z)$$ locates a point in space. A third axis — the $$z$$-axis — is added perpendicular to both the $$x$$- and $$y$$-axes at the origin $$O = (0,0,0)$$.

- **Right-hand rule.** Curl the fingers of your right hand from the positive $$x$$-axis toward the positive $$y$$-axis; your thumb points along the positive $$z$$-axis. This fixes the orientation of the system.
- **Coordinate planes.** The axes define three planes: the $$xy$$-plane ($$z=0$$), the $$xz$$-plane ($$y=0$$), and the $$yz$$-plane ($$x=0$$).
- **Octants.** The three coordinate planes divide space into eight **octants**, analogous to how the axes divide the plane into four quadrants. The *first octant* is where $$x \ge 0$$, $$y \ge 0$$, and $$z \ge 0$$.

A variable that's **missing** from an equation is the key to reading its graph in 3D:

| Equation | Missing variable | Graph |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $$z = k$$ | none free beyond $$x,y$$ | a plane parallel to the $$xy$$-plane |
| $$x^2 + y^2 = 1$$ | $$z$$ | a circular **cylinder** along the $$z$$-axis |
| $$y + z = 0$$ | $$x$$ | the line $$z=-y$$ in the $$yz$$-plane, swept along the entire $$x$$-axis — a **plane** |

The rule of thumb: whatever variable doesn't appear in the equation is free to take *any* value, and the graph extends infinitely in that direction.

### Distance in three dimensions

**Distance formula.** The distance between $$P_1(x_1,y_1,z_1)$$ and $$P_2(x_2,y_2,z_2)$$ is

$$d = \sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2 + (y_2-y_1)^2 + (z_2-z_1)^2}$$

This is just the Pythagorean theorem applied twice: first within a plane parallel to the $$xy$$-plane to get the "flat" distance $$d_{xy} = \sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2+(y_2-y_1)^2}$$, then again using $$d_{xy}$$ and the vertical leg $$\lvert z_2 - z_1 \rvert$$ as the two legs of a right triangle whose hypotenuse is $$d$$.

> **Example.** The distance between $$(-2,5,1)$$ and $$(3,0,-1)$$ is
> $$d = \sqrt{(3-(-2))^2 + (0-5)^2 + (-1-1)^2} = \sqrt{25+25+4} = \sqrt{54} = 3\sqrt{6}$$

### The equation of a sphere

A **sphere** is the set of all points a fixed distance (the **radius** $$r$$) from a fixed point (the **center** $$C=(a,b,c)$$). Setting the distance from a general point $$(x,y,z)$$ to $$C$$ equal to $$r$$ and squaring both sides gives the standard equation:

$$(x-a)^2 + (y-b)^2 + (z-c)^2 = r^2$$

> **Example.** The sphere of radius $$2$$ centered at $$(2,3,-1)$$ has equation
> $$(x-2)^2 + (y-3)^2 + (z+1)^2 = 4$$

### Practice & reflection

1. What are the equations of the $$x$$-, $$y$$-, and $$z$$-axes? (Think about which coordinates must be zero and which are free.)
2. Describe the region defined by $$x = 0$$ and $$y \ge 0$$. Is it a line, a plane, or something else?
3. A sphere is centered at $$(-1, 4, -3)$$ and is tangent to the $$yz$$-plane. What is its radius? *(Hint: the shortest distance from the center to a plane it's tangent to equals the radius.)*
4. Find the equation of the line through $$(0,-1,2)$$ that is parallel to the $$x$$-axis.
5. Explain why $$x^2 + z^2 = 9$$ is a cylinder in $$\mathbb{R}^3$$ but a circle if plotted only in the $$xz$$-plane.
