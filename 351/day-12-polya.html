Paper first, then check in. This page goes with the Day 12 handout, one card per Part. Do Part A on paper, then open the Part A card and tell it what you got: gradients, misses, a step, a line. If something is off, the page tries to name the slip, and hints are there when you want them (each one a little more specific). Then on to Part B, and so on.

The paper is what you turn in. You’ll see answers here as you check your work. That’s on purpose: they’re for checking, not copying. Your handout, with your own reasoning and both Skeptic answers, is what goes on Canvas.

Typing tips: a vector is a column of boxes, one entry per box, with \(m\) on top and \(c\) below whenever the question is about a line. Decimals like -17.5 and fractions like -35/2 both work. A formula is typed the way you’d write it: 2x, -2(y - m x - c). Your place is saved in this browser, so you can stop and come back.

Walk-through · Part A

A bowl you can see

Part A. Do Part A on paper first, then check in here.
Take the bowl \(f(x,y)=x^2+4y^2\). Its level curves are on the handout; the bottom is at \((0,0)\).
1. Find \(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}\) and \(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial y}\). The gradient is the vector of both: \(\nabla f=\left(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x},\,\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right)\).
2. Compute \(\nabla f\) at \(P=(2,1)\) and at \(Q=(0,-1.5)\).
3. The gradient points uphill, the way \(f\) grows fastest. So \(-\nabla f\) points downhill. Draw a short arrow in the direction of \(-\nabla f\) at \(P\) and at \(Q\).
4. Look at your arrows. Does each one cross its level curve at a right angle? Does each one point straight at the bottom \((0,0)\)?
Skeptic. “Downhill” and “toward the bottom” sound like the same thing. Using point \(P\), explain why they aren’t. What shape would the bowl need for them to always agree?

Goal: Check your gradient of \(f = x^2 + 4y^2\) at \(P\) and \(Q\), and see when downhill does and doesn’t point at the bottom. It’s the gradient from multivariable calculus, on a bowl small enough to see whole. Part B does the same thing on the loss bowl for a line, where you can’t see the whole bowl and the gradient is all you have.

Walk-through · Part B

The loss bowl for a line

Part B. Do Part B on paper first, then check in here.
Back to the four cars from Day 1 (weight \(x\) in thousands of pounds): A \((2.0, 33)\), B \((2.5, 27)\), C \((3.5, 21)\), D \((4.0, 15)\), as (weight \(x\), mpg \(y\)). A line \(\hat y = m x + c\) has two parameters, slope \(m\) and intercept \(c\). The miss on car \(i\) is \(r_i = y_i - (m x_i + c)\), and the loss is the mean squared error \(L(m,c) = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl(y_i - m x_i - c\bigr)^2\) \((n = 4)\). Point \(S = (0, 24)\) is the flat line \(\hat y = 24\); point \(T = (-8, 45)\) is the line \(\hat y = 45 - 8x\).
1. For the line at \(T\), fill in the table (predicted \(45 - 8x\), miss \(r\), \(r^2\)) and compute \(L(-8, 45)\).
2. One term of the sum is \((y_i - m x_i - c)^2\). Use the chain rule to find its partial derivative with respect to \(c\), and then with respect to \(m\). Adding up the terms gives \(\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial m} = -\dfrac2n\sum_i x_i r_i\), \(\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial c} = -\dfrac2n\sum_i r_i\). Check that your term-by-term answers match these.
3. Compute \(\nabla L\) at \(T\). (Your table already has the \(r_i\).)
4. At \(S\) every prediction is 24, so the misses are \(9, 3, -3, -9\). Compute \(\nabla L\) at \(S\).
5. On the figure, draw the downhill arrow \(-\nabla L\) at \(S\) and at \(T\). In words: to improve the line at \(S\), should the slope go up or down? Should the intercept change? What about at \(T\)? (The two axes use different scales, so draw the arrows short and don’t worry if they don’t look perpendicular to the curves: it’s the direction that matters.)

Goal: Check your table and loss at \(T\), your gradient at \(T\) and at \(S\), and what each one says to do to the line. Part A found downhill on a bowl you could see. Here the bowl is the loss of a line, and the gradient is computed from the misses alone, without drawing the bowl. Part C takes a step.

Walk-through · Part C

Take one step

Part C. Do Part C on paper first, then check in here.
Walking downhill means repeating one move: from where you stand, step a little way along \(-\nabla L\): \((m,c)_{\text{new}} = (m,c) - \eta\,\nabla L(m,c)\). The step size \(\eta\) (the Greek letter “eta”) is called the learning rate.
1. Take one step from \(T\) with \(\eta = 0.05\). Where do you land? Compute \(L\) there. Did the loss go down from your answer in Part B?
2. With \(\eta = 0.1\) instead, the step lands at \((-6.25,\ 45.6)\) and the loss there is about \(11.9\). Is that better or worse than where you started? How can stepping downhill make things worse? (Look at the shape of the bowl near \(T\).)

Goal: Check your one step from \(T\) and the loss where it lands, and see how a step that’s too big can make things worse. Part B found the downhill direction at \(T\). Here you move along it once, by hand. Wednesday’s lab lets the computer repeat the move a thousand times.

Walk-through · Part D

Where the ground is flat

Part D. Do Part D on paper first, then check in here.
At the bottom of the bowl there’s no downhill: \(\nabla L = (0,0)\).
1. Setting both partial derivatives to zero says \(\sum r_i = 0\) and \(\sum x_i r_i = 0\). Write these out as two equations in \(m\) and \(c\). (Helpful sums: \(\sum x_i = 12\), \(\sum y_i = 96\), \(\sum x_i^2 = 38.5\), \(\sum x_i y_i = 267\).)
2. Solve them. Have you seen this line before? (Day 1, and what the least-squares method of Days 10–11 gives for these cars.)
3. In Day 9’s language: \(\sum r_i = 0\) says the leftover vector \(\mathbf r\) is perpendicular to \((1,1,1,1)\), and \(\sum x_i r_i = 0\) says it’s perpendicular to \(\mathbf x\). Those are the two columns of the design matrix. So “flat ground” and “leftover perpendicular” are ________.
Skeptic. Part D found the bottom exactly, with no walking. So why would anyone bother taking small steps downhill? Give at least one reason. (Think about a model with thousands of parameters, or a loss that isn’t a nice bowl.)

Goal: Solve \(\nabla L = 0\) for the four cars, recognize the line, and connect flat ground to Day 9’s perpendicular leftover. Parts B and C walked toward the bottom. Here you jump straight to it, and it’s an old friend: the least-squares line. Wednesday’s lab walks all the way down and checks that it lands in the same place.
