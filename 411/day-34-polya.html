How these work. Each walk-through takes one problem through Pólya’s four phases. You answer one step at a time and get feedback right away; when something isn’t right, read why and try again. Hints are there when you ask for them. Your place is saved in this browser, so you can stop and come back.

Typing \(N\), with a twist. Where a step asks for \(N\) as a formula, type eps for \(\varepsilon\) and b for \(b\). The page plays the adversary from the Day 17 game against your formula. Today one \(N\) has to serve every \(x\) in the interval at once. In Problem 10.1.4 the largest error over \([0,b]\) is a single term, \(b^n\), so the page checks your \(N\) against that term. Where a choice depends on \(x\), the steps ask you to choose rather than type.

The walk-throughs end in full proofs, so the proof you bring to class should still be your own, in your own words.

Pólya walk-through · Problem 10.1.1

Cauchy’s argument, and the shrinking δ

Problem 10.1.1. Find the flaw in the following “proof” that \(f\) is also continuous at \(a\).
Suppose \(f_1, f_2, f_3, f_4 \ldots\) are all continuous at \(a\) and that \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty f_n = f\). Let \(\varepsilon > 0\). Since \(f_n\) is continuous at \(a\), we can choose \(\delta_n > 0\) such that if \(|x - a| < \delta_n\), then \(|f_n(x) - f_n(a)| < \frac{\varepsilon}{2^n}\). Let \(\delta = \inf(\delta_1, \delta_2, \delta_3, \ldots)\). If \(|x - a| < \delta\) then
\[\begin{align*} |f(x) - f(a)| &= \Bigl|\sum_{n=1}^\infty f_n(x) - \sum_{n=1}^\infty f_n(a)\Bigr| \\ &= \Bigl|\sum_{n=1}^\infty \bigl(f_n(x) - f_n(a)\bigr)\Bigr| \\ &\le \sum_{n=1}^\infty |f_n(x) - f_n(a)| \\ &\le \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{\varepsilon}{2^n} \\ &\le \varepsilon \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{1}{2^n} \\ &= \varepsilon \end{align*}\]Thus \(f\) is continuous at \(a\).

Goal: Find the claim in this argument that doesn’t hold, say why, and test it on one concrete choice of \(\delta_n\). It is Cauchy’s 1821 argument from the reading. The rest of the day asks the same question of each problem: which quantity has to be fixed, and what is it allowed to depend on?

Pólya walk-through · Problem 10.1.4

One N for every x

Problem 10.1.4. Let \(0 < b < 1\) and consider the sequence of functions \((f_n)\) defined on \([0,b]\) by \(f_n(x) = x^n\). Use the definition to show that \(f_n \rightrightarrows 0\) on \([0,b]\).

Goal: Find one \(N\) that works for every \(x\) in \([0,b]\) at once, and show that it does. Compare it with the pointwise \(N\) you could find for each \(x\) separately. It uses the \(N\) from Problem 6.1.7 (Day 17) for \(|b|^n\). The new question is which quantity the \(N\) is allowed to depend on.

Pólya walk-through · Problem 10.1.6

Three pieces, and n before δ

Problem 10.1.6. Provide a formal proof of Theorem 10.1.5 based on the above ideas.
Theorem 10.1.5. Consider a sequence of functions \((f_n)\) which are all continuous on an interval \(I\). Suppose \(f_n \rightrightarrows f\) on \(I\). Then \(f\) must be continuous on \(I\).

Goal: Write a formal \(\varepsilon\)–\(\delta\) proof that a uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous, with every choice made in the right order. It is the proof the day page scaffolds. It also explains the Fourier example that opens the reading (Day 16’s square wave): a discontinuous limit cannot be a uniform one.

Pólya walk-through · Problem 10.1.7

Pointwise, but not uniformly

Problem 10.1.7. Consider the sequence of functions \((f_n)\) defined on \([0,1]\) by \(f_n(x) = x^n\). Show that the sequence converges to the function
\[f(x) = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{ if } x \in [0,1) \\ 1 & \text{ if } x = 1 \end{cases}\]pointwise on \([0,1]\), but not uniformly on \([0,1]\).

Goal: Prove a pointwise limit in two cases, then negate the definition of uniform convergence carefully enough to show that it fails. The sequence \(x^n\) on \([0,1]\) is where the day’s jump comes from. Problem 6.1.7 supplies most of the case \(0 \le x < 1\), and Theorem 10.1.5 gives a second route to the same conclusion.
