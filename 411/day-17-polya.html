How these work. Each walk-through takes one problem through Pólya’s four phases. You answer one step at a time and get feedback right away; when something isn’t right, read why and try again. Hints are there when you ask for them. Your place is saved in this browser, so you can stop and come back.

Something new: you name the \(N\). Some steps ask you to type \(N\) as a formula in \(\varepsilon\) (type eps). The page then plays the adversary from the Day 17 game: it throws challenges \(\varepsilon\) at your formula, big and small, and checks every term past your \(N\). Any \(N\) that works is right, not only the one in the model proof. And if yours fails, you’ll see the exact challenge it lost.

The walk-throughs end in a full proof, so the proof you bring to class should still be your own, in your own words.

Pólya walk-through · Problem 6.1.2

Two ways to say “within \(b\) of \(0\)”

Problem 6.1.2. Let \(a\) and \(b\) be real numbers with \(b > 0\). Prove \(|a| < b\) if and only if \(-b < a < b\). Notice that this can be extended to \(|a| \le b\) if and only if \(-b \le a \le b\).

Goal: Prove an “if and only if” by proving both directions, splitting into cases where the absolute value forces you to. Note 3 under Definition 6.1.1 uses this to trade \(|s_n| < \varepsilon\) for \(-\varepsilon < s_n < \varepsilon\), and every limit proof from here on leans on it.

Pólya walk-through · Problem 6.1.4

Solve for \(n\), then turn it around

Problem 6.1.4. Use the definition of convergence to zero to prove the following.
(a) \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n^2} = 0\)
(b) \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}} = 0\)

Goal: Find an \(N\) that answers any \(\varepsilon\), then write the proof forward: given \(\varepsilon\), here is \(N\), and here is why it works. It builds on the reading’s proof that \(\frac1n \to 0\) with \(N = \frac{1}{\varepsilon}\), and leads to 6.1.6, where solving for \(n\) exactly gets hard and you bound instead.

Pólya walk-through · Problem 6.1.6

Bound it, don’t solve it

Problem 6.1.6. Use the definition of convergence to zero to prove \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n^2+4n+1}{n^3} = 0\).

Goal: Bound a messy expression above by a simple one that still goes to \(0\), and find \(N\) for the simple one. It builds on Example 6.1.5, which bounded \(\frac{n+4}{n^2+1}\) by \(\frac2n\). The habit of making it bigger and simpler carries almost every limit proof from here through Chapter 8.

Pólya walk-through · Problem 6.1.7

Powers of \(b\), and an inequality that flips

Problem 6.1.7. Let \(b\) be a nonzero real number with \(|b| < 1\), and let \(\varepsilon > 0\).
(a) Solve the inequality \(|b|^n < \varepsilon\) for \(n\).
(b) Use part (a) to prove \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty} b^n = 0\).

Goal: Solve an inequality with logarithms, keeping track of the sign that flips it, and turn the answer into an \(N\) that works for every \(\varepsilon\). It builds on 6.1.4 (solve for \(n\), then write the proof forward) and leads to Day 18, where \(|b| = 1\) gives sequences that don’t converge to \(0\), and later to geometric series.
