Chapter 4 · A

The mind as a playground

A. Chapter 4, “Play.” Su starts with a baby cooing and kicking, “driven not by need but by curiosity,” and claims that play “is a deep human desire, and it is a mark of human flourishing.” Then he makes his case: “Mathematics makes the mind its playground.”

Goal: Follow Su’s case that doing mathematics properly is a kind of play, then decide how far you agree. Play gathers up the earlier chapters: exploration supplies the questions, meaning the connections. Its virtues (hopefulness, patience, perseverance) set up the later chapter on struggle.

Chapter 4 · Puzzle

The Game of Cycles

Puzzle. Draw a triangle with a dot at each corner and one in the middle, joined to the corners: three triangular cells. Players take turns marking an arrow on one unmarked edge. No dot may become a sink (every edge at it marked, all pointing in) or a source (all pointing out), so some edges become unmarkable. Whoever completes a cycle cell (a cell whose arrows all run the same way around) or makes the last possible move wins. Does the first or the second player have a winning strategy? (Su, the Game of Cycles box in Chapter 4.)

Goal: Learn the game’s first lessons, find who wins on the starting diagram, and see what the reason looks like. This is a directed graph on a planar map, and “no sinks or sources” is a condition on in-degree and out-degree: a small taste of graph theory you can play at a kitchen table.

Chapter 4 · Puzzle

Stubborn Endings

Puzzle. Squares of numbers ending in 5 follow a pattern: \(15^2 = 225\), \(25^2 = 625\), \(35^2 = 1225\), \(45^2 = 2025\). Find the shortcut and prove it. Then call the last few digits of a number its ending, and call an ending stubborn if the product of any two numbers with that ending has that ending too. The two-digit stubborn endings are …00, …01, …25 and …76; the three-digit ones are …000, …001, …625 and …376. Which four-digit endings are stubborn? (Su’s own example, worked through in the text of Chapter 4; it is not one of his end-of-chapter puzzles.)

Goal: Prove the squaring shortcut, find a stubborn four-digit ending, and see one reason the endings come in pairs. A stubborn ending is a solution of \(e^2 \equiv e \pmod{10^k}\): an idempotent. Su says the pattern he saw gave up its secret when he met the Chinese remainder theorem in a number theory course.
