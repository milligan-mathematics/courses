  <h2>Orders inside \(D_6\)</h2>

  <p>Every element \(g\) of a group generates a cyclic subgroup \(\langle g \rangle\), the set of powers of \(g\). The
    <strong>order</strong> \(|g|\) is the number of distinct powers, which is also the number of elements in
    \(\langle g \rangle\). The widget shows the twelve elements of \(D_6\), with one tint per order.</p>

  <p>The powers of \(g\) form a subgroup, so \(|g|\) is the size of a subgroup. That link is what lets Lagrange's
    Theorem say something about element orders.</p>

  <p>Predict first: which orders occur? Then click a few elements and check their powers against your prediction.</p>

  <div id="d13-orders"></div>

  <p>Every order you see divides 12. The table alone doesn't prove that, but the scaffold below proves it for every
    finite group. A useful habit meanwhile: when you see an order, ask which subgroup it comes from. The answer is
    always the cyclic subgroup generated by that element.</p>

<div class="act scaffold">
  <div class="act-type">Proof Scaffold</div>
  <h2>Counting the pieces</h2>

  <p>Judson's proof of Lagrange's Theorem is short. It uses two facts you already have: the left cosets of \(H\)
    partition \(G\) (Section 6.1), and every left coset has \(|H|\) elements (last class's scaffold). Let \(G\) be a
    finite group and \(H\) a subgroup.</p>

  <p>Three words carry the proof. A coset is a set \(gH\). The index \([G : H]\) is the number of left cosets of
    \(H\) in \(G\). And \(|g|\) is the order of \(g\), which is the size of \(\langle g \rangle\).</p>

  <div class="proof-scaffold">
    <ol class="scaffold-steps">
      <li class="sstep">
        <div class="sstep-prompt">Suppose there are \(k\) left cosets of \(H\). How many elements of \(G\) do they account for?</div>
        <div class="sstep-body"><p>All of them. Each element lies in exactly one coset, and the cosets don't overlap, so
          \(|G|\) is the sum of the sizes of the \(k\) cosets.</p></div>
      </li>
      <li class="sstep">
        <div class="sstep-prompt">Each coset has the same size. What is it?</div>
        <div class="sstep-body"><p>\(|H|\), by the equal-size scaffold. So the sum has \(k\) terms, and each term is \(|H|\).</p></div>
      </li>
      <li class="sstep">
        <div class="sstep-prompt">Put the two steps together. What is \(|G|\) in terms of \(k\) and \(|H|\), and what does that say about \(|H|\)?</div>
        <div class="sstep-body"><p>\(|G| = k\,|H|\). So \(|H|\) divides \(|G|\). That's Lagrange's Theorem: the order of a
          subgroup divides the order of the group.</p></div>
      </li>
      <li class="sstep">
        <div class="sstep-prompt">What is \(k\)? Give it a name.</div>
        <div class="sstep-body"><p>\(k = |G|/|H|\), the number of left cosets. The reading calls this the index \([G : H]\), so
          \(|G| = [G : H]\,|H|\).</p></div>
      </li>
    </ol>
    <button class="btn411 ghost scaffold-all" type="button">Reveal the whole proof</button>
  </div>
</div>

  <h2>Which orders actually occur?</h2>

  <p>Lagrange's Theorem says a subgroup's order must divide the order of the group. It only counts, so it can rule
    orders out, but it can't by itself produce a subgroup of a given order. Two groups of order 12 test that. The first
    is \(A_4\), the group of even permutations of four objects. The second is \(D_6\), from the first explore. Lagrange
    allows a subgroup of order 6 in both.</p>

  <p>Each lattice shows the subgroups of one group. Each box is one subgroup, and the boxes on one row share an order.
    Predict before you click: for each group, is there a row labeled order 6?</p>

  <div id="d13-lattice"></div>
  <div id="d13-lattice-d6"></div>

  <p>Click any box to see its elements. The reading proves that \(A_4\) has no subgroup of order 6, even though 6
    divides 12. \(D_6\) does have one. The rule can't tell the two groups apart, so the flaw below is about exactly
    this gap.</p>

  <p>Lines join a subgroup to the larger subgroups that contain it directly. Start at the smallest box and follow the
    lines upward: each step lands on a bigger subgroup that contains the one below it.</p>

  <h2>Prime orders and indices</h2>

  <p>Each question uses one consequence of Lagrange's Theorem. Read the feedback even when you get the answer right,
    because the wrong options are the ones people actually pick.</p>

  <div class="mc" data-answer="c">
    <p class="mc-q">A group has 13 elements. How many of them generate the whole group?</p>
    <button class="mc-opt" data-key="a">One.</button>
    <button class="mc-opt" data-key="b">Six.</button>
    <button class="mc-opt" data-key="c">Twelve.</button>
    <button class="mc-opt" data-key="d">Thirteen.</button>

    <div class="mc-fb" data-key="a"><p>One is too few. The identity generates only itself, but every other element works.</p></div>
    <div class="mc-fb" data-key="b"><p>Six would be half of the non-identity elements. There's no halving here: the prime-order corollary covers all of them.</p></div>
    <div class="mc-fb" data-key="c"><p>Right. A non-identity element has order greater than 1 that divides 13, so its order is 13, and it generates the
      whole group.</p></div>
    <div class="mc-fb" data-key="d"><p>The identity doesn't generate the group. It generates \(\{e\}\), which has one element, not thirteen.</p></div>
  </div>

  <div class="mc" data-answer="d">
    <p class="mc-q">A group \(G\) has 24 elements. It has a subgroup \(H\) of order 6, and \(H\) has a subgroup \(K\) of order 2.
      What is the index \([G : K]\)?</p>
    <button class="mc-opt" data-key="a">3</button>
    <button class="mc-opt" data-key="b">4</button>
    <button class="mc-opt" data-key="c">6</button>
    <button class="mc-opt" data-key="d">12</button>

    <div class="mc-fb" data-key="a"><p>3 is \([H : K] = 6/2\), the index of \(K\) inside \(H\). The question asks about \(G\).</p></div>
    <div class="mc-fb" data-key="b"><p>4 is \([G : H] = 24/6\), the index of \(H\). That's a different index.</p></div>
    <div class="mc-fb" data-key="c"><p>6 is \(|H|\), the order of \(H\), not an index.</p></div>
    <div class="mc-fb" data-key="d"><p>Right. \([G : K] = |G|/|K| = 24/2 = 12\). Indices multiply along a chain:
      \([G : K] = [G : H][H : K] = 4 \cdot 3\).</p></div>
  </div>
</div>

<div class="act flaw">
  <div class="act-type">Spot the Flaw</div>
  <h2>If it divides, is it there?</h2>

  <p>The common misconception is the converse of Lagrange's Theorem: if \(m\) divides \(|G|\), then \(G\) has a subgroup
    of order \(m\). A classmate argues for it below. Each line reads like a theorem you know, so read them one at a
    time and ask what each line actually licenses. Click the line that goes wrong.</p>

  <p><strong>Click the step that isn't justified.</strong></p>

  <div class="flaw-widget" data-flaw="3">
    <div class="flawlist">
      <button class="fline" type="button">Let \(G\) be any group with 12 elements.</button>
      <button class="fline" type="button">By Lagrange's Theorem, the order of every subgroup of \(G\) divides 12.</button>
      <button class="fline" type="button">Since 6 divides 12, \(G\) has a subgroup of order 6.</button>
      <button class="fline" type="button">So every divisor of 12 is the order of some subgroup of \(G\).</button>
    </div>

    <div class="flaw-verdict" data-key="1"><p><strong>Fine.</strong> That's the hypothesis.</p></div>
    <div class="flaw-verdict" data-key="2"><p><strong>Fine.</strong> This is the theorem, and it holds in every finite group.</p></div>
    <div class="flaw-verdict" data-key="3"><p><strong>This is the flaw.</strong> Lagrange's Theorem runs one way: a
      subgroup's order divides \(|G|\). It doesn't say that a given divisor is realized. \(A_4\) has order 12, and the reading
      shows it has no subgroup of order 6. The lattice above is the counterexample.</p></div>
    <div class="flaw-verdict" data-key="4"><p><strong>Fine as a deduction.</strong> It repeats line 3 for each divisor, so it
      stands or falls with line 3.</p></div>
  </div>
</div>
