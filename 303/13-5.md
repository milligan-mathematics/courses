# §13.5: Directional Derivatives and Gradient Vectors

Partial derivatives tell you how fast a function changes when you move due east or due north. But you rarely walk along a coordinate axis. If you want to know how steeply a hillside rises when you head northeast, or how fast a room cools as you walk away from a heater at an odd angle, the partial derivatives alone can't answer the question. You need the rate of change in *any* direction you choose.

That rate is the **directional derivative**, and it has a very good helper. The **gradient** packages all of the partial derivatives into one vector. Once you have it at a point, the rate of change in every direction is a single dot product. The gradient also tells you which way is uphill, which is the question people actually ask when they look at a contour map.

By the end of this section you should be able to:

1. Explain what $$D_{\mathbf{u}} f(a,b)$$ measures, and why the direction $$\mathbf{u}$$ must be a unit vector.
2. Compute $$\nabla f$$ and use $$D_{\mathbf{u}} f = \nabla f \cdot \mathbf{u}$$ to find a directional derivative.
3. State the direction and size of the greatest increase and greatest decrease at a point, and find the directions of no change.
4. Use the gradient to find an equation for the tangent line to a level curve.
5. Apply the gradient rules for sums, products, and quotients, and interpret the gradient of a function of three variables.

### Directional derivatives and the gradient

The directional derivative of $$f(x,y)$$ at $$(a,b)$$ in the direction of a unit vector $$\mathbf{u} = \langle u_1, u_2 \rangle$$ is

$$D_{\mathbf{u}} f(a,b) = \lim_{t \to 0} \frac{f(a + t u_1,\ b + t u_2) - f(a,b)}{t}$$

when the limit exists. You walk a distance $$t$$ along the line in direction $$\mathbf{u}$$ and measure how fast $$f$$ changes per unit of distance walked. That's why $$\mathbf{u}$$ has to be a **unit vector**: only then is $$t$$ the actual distance you traveled.

Here is the common mistake to watch for: forgetting to normalize. If you are handed $$\mathbf{v} = \langle 3, 4 \rangle$$, divide by $$\lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert = 5$$ first. Skip that step and every answer comes out too big by the factor $$\lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert$$.

Evaluating the limit by hand is tedious, so we almost never do. The **gradient** of $$f(x,y)$$ is

$$\nabla f(x,y) = \langle f_x(x,y),\ f_y(x,y) \rangle = f_x \mathbf{i} + f_y \mathbf{j},$$

a vector field that assigns a vector to every point of the domain. For a function of $$n$$ variables, the same pattern gives $$\nabla f = \langle \partial f/\partial x_1, \ldots, \partial f/\partial x_n \rangle$$.

The shortcut is the key theorem. If $$f$$ is differentiable at $$(a,b)$$ and $$\mathbf{u}$$ is a unit vector, then

$$D_{\mathbf{u}} f(a,b) = \nabla f(a,b) \cdot \mathbf{u}.$$

A directional derivative is a dot product. Since $$\lVert \mathbf{u} \rVert = 1$$, that dot product is $$\lVert \nabla f \rVert \cos\varphi$$, where $$\varphi$$ is the angle between $$\nabla f$$ and $$\mathbf{u}$$. Assume $$\nabla f(a,b) \neq \mathbf{0}$$. Then the cosine tells you everything about direction:

- **Greatest increase.** When $$\varphi = 0$$, $$\mathbf{u}$$ points the same way as $$\nabla f$$, and the rate is $$\lVert \nabla f \rVert$$. The gradient points in the direction of steepest ascent, and its length is that maximum rate.
- **Greatest decrease.** When $$\varphi = \pi$$, $$\mathbf{u}$$ points opposite the gradient, and the rate is $$-\lVert \nabla f \rVert$$. That is the steepest way downhill.
- **No change.** When $$\varphi = \pi/2$$, $$\mathbf{u}$$ is perpendicular to $$\nabla f$$, and the rate is $$0$$. Those directions run along the level curve through the point. So the gradient is normal to the level curves, and on a contour map the steepest arrow crosses the contour lines at right angles.

> **Worked example.** For $$f(x,y) = \sin(x + y^2)$$ at $$(2,1)$$, find the direction and size of the greatest increase and greatest decrease, and the directions of no change.
>
> The gradient is $$\nabla f = \langle \cos(x+y^2),\ 2y\cos(x+y^2) \rangle$$, so $$\nabla f(2,1) = \cos(3)\,\langle 1, 2 \rangle$$. Since $$\cos 3 \approx -0.990$$, the gradient points opposite to $$\langle 1,2 \rangle$$, and its length is $$\lVert \nabla f(2,1) \rVert = \sqrt{5}\,\lvert \cos 3 \rvert \approx 2.21$$.
>
> - **Greatest increase:** the gradient divided by its length, $$-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{5}}\langle 1,2 \rangle \approx \langle -0.447,\ -0.894 \rangle$$, with rate $$2.21$$.
> - **Greatest decrease:** the opposite direction, $$\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{5}}\langle 1,2 \rangle \approx \langle 0.447,\ 0.894 \rangle$$, with rate $$-2.21$$.
> - **No change:** any direction perpendicular to $$\langle 1,2 \rangle$$, such as $$\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{5}}\langle 2,-1 \rangle \approx \langle 0.894,\ -0.447 \rangle$$ or its opposite. These directions are tangent to the level curve through $$(2,1)$$.

### Gradients and level curves

A level curve $$F(x,y) = k$$ has $$\nabla F$$ as a normal vector. That means the gradient gives you tangent lines without any implicit differentiation. A line through $$(x_0,y_0)$$ with normal vector $$\langle A, B \rangle$$ has equation $$A(x-x_0) + B(y-y_0) = 0$$.

> **Worked example.** Find an equation for the line tangent to $$(x-2)^2 + 4(y+1)^2 = 8$$ at $$(4,0)$$.
>
> This curve is the level curve $$F(x,y) = 8$$ of $$F(x,y) = (x-2)^2 + 4(y+1)^2$$. Then $$\nabla F = \langle 2(x-2),\ 8(y+1) \rangle$$, so $$\nabla F(4,0) = \langle 4, 8 \rangle$$. That vector is normal to the curve at $$(4,0)$$, so the tangent line is $$4(x-4) + 8(y-0) = 0$$, which simplifies to $$x + 2y = 4$$.

### Properties of the gradient

Just as with ordinary derivatives, the gradient obeys the familiar rules. If $$f$$ and $$g$$ are differentiable and $$k$$ is a constant, then

- $$\nabla(f \pm g) = \nabla f \pm \nabla g$$ and $$\nabla(kf) = k\nabla f$$,
- $$\nabla(fg) = f\nabla g + g\nabla f$$,
- $$\nabla\!\left(\dfrac{f}{g}\right) = \dfrac{g\nabla f - f\nabla g}{g^2}$$, provided $$g \neq 0$$.

> **Worked example.** Find $$\nabla f$$ for $$f(x,y) = \dfrac{x^2 - xy^3}{x^2 + y^2}$$.
>
> Let $$N = x^2 - xy^3$$ and $$D = x^2 + y^2$$. Since $$\nabla N = \langle 2x - y^3,\ -3xy^2 \rangle$$ and $$\nabla D = \langle 2x,\ 2y \rangle$$, the quotient law gives components $$\dfrac{(x^2+y^2)(2x-y^3) - (x^2-xy^3)(2x)}{(x^2+y^2)^2}$$ and $$\dfrac{(x^2+y^2)(-3xy^2) - (x^2-xy^3)(2y)}{(x^2+y^2)^2}$$. Expanding and simplifying the numerators gives
>
> $$\nabla f(x,y) = \left\langle \dfrac{y^2(x^2y + 2x - y^3)}{(x^2+y^2)^2},\ \dfrac{-xy(3x^2y + y^3 + 2x)}{(x^2+y^2)^2} \right\rangle.$$

### Three dimensions: a temperature field

The same ideas hold for three variables. The gradient is $$\nabla f = \langle f_x, f_y, f_z \rangle$$, it points in the direction of steepest ascent, and its length is the maximum rate of increase. Level sets are now surfaces instead of curves.

> **Worked example.** Near a heat source, the temperature in degrees Celsius is modeled by $$T(x,y,z) = \dfrac{100}{1 + 2x^2 + y^2 + 0.5z^2}$$, where $$x$$ and $$y$$ are horizontal displacements and $$z$$ is the vertical displacement from the source, all in meters. From $$(1,3,2)$$, find the direction of greatest decrease in temperature and the rate of change in that direction.
>
> The gradient is $$\nabla T = -\dfrac{100}{(1 + 2x^2 + y^2 + 0.5z^2)^2}\,\langle 4x,\ 2y,\ z \rangle$$. At $$(1,3,2)$$ the denominator is $$1 + 2 + 9 + 2 = 14$$, so $$\nabla T(1,3,2) = -\dfrac{100}{196}\langle 4,6,2 \rangle = -\dfrac{25}{49}\langle 4,6,2 \rangle$$.
>
> The greatest decrease points opposite the gradient: $$\dfrac{-\nabla T}{\lVert \nabla T \rVert} = \dfrac{\langle 2,3,1 \rangle}{\sqrt{14}} \approx \langle 0.535,\ 0.802,\ 0.267 \rangle$$. The rate in that direction is $$-\lVert \nabla T(1,3,2) \rVert = -\tfrac{50\sqrt{14}}{49}$$, about $$-3.82\ ^\circ\text{C/m}$$.

### Practice & reflection

1. Why does $$D_{\mathbf{u}} f$$ need a unit vector to mean "rate of change per unit distance"? What would change if you used $$\mathbf{v} = \langle 3, 4 \rangle$$ directly?
2. Explain, in your own words, why $$\nabla f$$ is perpendicular to the level curves of $$f$$. Why does that make sense when you think about walking along a level curve?
3. If $$\nabla f(\mathbf{c}) = \mathbf{0}$$, what is the rate of change at $$\mathbf{c}$$ in every direction? Name a familiar point from single-variable calculus that behaves the same way.
4. You are sailing on water whose depth is $$D(x,y)$$. Describe how you would use the gradient to steer toward shallower water as fast as possible, and then how you would steer to keep the depth constant.
