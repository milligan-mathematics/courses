# §11.6: Cylinders and Quadric Surfaces

So far we've described space with points, distances, and spheres. Now it's time for the fun part: naming the shapes that show up over and over in three dimensions. Almost every "new" 3D surface you'll meet in this course turns out to be one of a small, predictable family. Learn to recognize the family, and sketching becomes far less mysterious.

By the end of this section you should be able to:

1. Recognize a **cylinder** from a missing variable and describe its generating curve and rulings.
2. Name each of the six standard **quadric surfaces** from its equation.
3. Use **traces** (cross-sections) to understand and sketch a surface — and know when a "surface" turns out to have no points at all.

### Cylinders: it's not just soup cans

In everyday language a cylinder is a tube. In $$\mathbb{R}^3$$, the definition is more generous: a **cylinder** is the surface swept out by a family of parallel lines, called **rulings**, that all pass through a fixed plane curve called the **generating curve**. The generating curve can be a circle — but it can just as easily be a parabola, an ellipse, or two crossing lines. Whatever shape it is, that shape is dragged straight through space, unchanged, forever.

Here's the shortcut that makes cylinders easy to spot: **if an equation in $$x,y,z$$ is missing one variable, its graph is a cylinder.** The two variables that *do* appear sketch the generating curve in their coordinate plane, and the rulings run parallel to the axis of the *missing* variable.

> **Example.** Consider $$x^2 = 4y$$. There's no $$z$$ anywhere, so this is a cylinder with rulings parallel to the $$z$$-axis. In the $$xy$$-plane, $$x^2 = 4y$$ is a parabola opening in the positive $$y$$ direction with vertex at the origin. So the surface is a **parabolic cylinder**: take that parabola and stretch it infinitely up and down the $$z$$-axis, like a curved sheet of paper standing on its edge.

The same idea works no matter which conic shows up. A missing variable plus a circle or ellipse gives a circular or elliptic cylinder (a tube); a missing variable plus a hyperbola gives a hyperbolic cylinder; a missing variable plus two crossing lines gives, well, two crossing planes.

One quick warning worth internalizing now, because it will save you on an exam: when you see a chain of equalities like $$A = B = C$$, it secretly means *two* separate equations, $$A=C$$ and $$B=C$$, both of which must hold. Occasionally that combination is impossible — for instance $$z^2+2=3$$ forces $$z=\pm 1$$, while $$-4x^2 = 3$$ has no real solution at all. When one of the pieces has no solution, the "surface" they jointly describe is the **empty set**. Not every equation graphs to something!

### The big six: quadric surfaces

A **quadric surface** is the graph of a second-degree equation in $$x, y, z$$. After rotating and shifting axes to clear out cross terms like $$xy$$, every quadric surface reduces to one of six standard shapes. You don't need to memorize six separate equations from scratch — just learn to read the pattern of squared terms, signs, and the one leftover linear term.

| Surface | Standard equation | Shape |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Ellipsoid | $$\dfrac{x^2}{a^2}+\dfrac{y^2}{b^2}+\dfrac{z^2}{c^2}=1$$ | A football or (if $$a=b=c$$) a sphere. Every trace is an ellipse. |
| Elliptic paraboloid | $$\dfrac{x^2}{a^2}+\dfrac{y^2}{b^2}=\dfrac{z}{c}$$ | A bowl. Traces perpendicular to the axis are ellipses; traces containing the axis are parabolas. |
| Hyperbolic paraboloid | $$\dfrac{x^2}{a^2}-\dfrac{y^2}{b^2}=\dfrac{z}{c}$$ | A saddle (think Pringle chip). Traces are parabolas one way, hyperbolas the other — except right at the saddle point, where you get two crossing lines. |
| Elliptic cone | $$\dfrac{x^2}{a^2}+\dfrac{y^2}{b^2}=\dfrac{z^2}{c^2}$$ | Two nappes joined at a single apex. |
| Hyperboloid of one sheet | $$\dfrac{x^2}{a^2}+\dfrac{y^2}{b^2}-\dfrac{z^2}{c^2}=1$$ | An hourglass — one connected surface with a "waist." |
| Hyperboloid of two sheets | $$\dfrac{x^2}{a^2}-\dfrac{y^2}{b^2}-\dfrac{z^2}{c^2}=1$$ | Two separate bowls opening away from each other. |

Here's the pattern-matching trick, which is really all you need on a test:

- **All three variables squared, equal to 1?** It's an ellipsoid (all $$+$$) or a hyperboloid (one or two $$-$$ signs: one sheet, two sheets).
- **Two variables squared, one variable linear?** It's a paraboloid — elliptic if the squared terms share a sign, hyperbolic (saddle) if they don't.
- **All three variables squared, equal to 0** (after moving everything to one side)? It's a cone.

The traces are your best sketching tool, and they follow directly from the shape of the equation: setting one variable equal to a constant $$k$$ and simplifying tells you exactly what curve lives in that slice, and watching how that curve changes as $$k$$ moves is what turns a flat equation into a 3D picture in your head.

### Worked example: naming a surface from its equation

**Identify the surface $$x^2+4y^2+4z=0$$.**

First, isolate the linear term: $$z = -\frac{x^2}{4}-y^2$$. Two variables are squared ($$x$$ and $$y$$), one is linear ($$z$$), and the squared terms have the *same* sign — so this is an **elliptic paraboloid**, and since that shared sign is negative, it opens downward.

Now check the traces to confirm the picture:

- At $$z=0$$: $$x^2+4y^2=0$$, which forces $$x=y=0$$ — just the vertex, at the origin.
- At $$z=k<0$$: $$x^2+4y^2=-4k>0$$, an ellipse that grows as $$k$$ becomes more negative.
- At $$z=k>0$$: $$x^2+4y^2=-4k<0$$, which is impossible — confirming the bowl never rises above $$z=0$$.

Put together: a downward-opening bowl with its vertex at the origin, widening as you go down. That's exactly what "elliptic paraboloid, negative sign" promised.

### Worked example: completing the square to find the type

Equations rarely arrive in standard form. If a quadric equation has *linear* terms mixed in with the squared ones (like $$9x^2-9y^2-4z^2+18y+16z=61$$), the surface is just shifted off-center — you find its true type and its center by completing the square, exactly as you would in two dimensions.

Grouping and completing the square in $$y$$ and $$z$$ turns that equation into

$$\frac{x^2}{4}-\frac{(y-1)^2}{4}-\frac{(z-2)^2}{9}=1.$$

One positive squared term and two negative ones, set equal to $$1$$: that's a **hyperboloid of two sheets**, centered at $$(0,1,2)$$ instead of the origin, opening along the direction of the $$x$$-term (the lone positive one). The moral: the *signs and the number of squared terms* tell you the family; completing the square just tells you where it's sitting.

### Practice & reflection

1. What is the missing variable in $$y^2-4y+z^2=8$$, and what does that tell you about the surface's rulings?
2. Classify $$6y^2+4z^2-3x^2=12$$ by rewriting it in standard form. Which axis does it open along?
3. The equation $$z^2+2=-4x^2=3$$ looks like it should describe a surface. Work through both halves of the chained equality — does it?
4. For the elliptic paraboloid $$\dfrac{x^2}{9}+\dfrac{z^2}{4}=\dfrac{y}{25}$$, describe the trace in the plane $$y=25$$ and the trace in the plane $$x=0$$.
5. Why does a plane trace of a hyperboloid of *one* sheet always produce either a hyperbola or an ellipse, but a trace of a hyperboloid of *two* sheets can also come up completely empty?
