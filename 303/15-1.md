# §15.1: Vector Fields

Until now a function of several variables handed you a *number* at each point: a temperature, a height, a density. Many things in engineering are not numbers. Wind has a speed and a direction; gravity pulls with a strength and a direction; water in a pipe moves at some velocity at every point. To describe these we need a rule that hands you a *vector* at each point. That rule is a **vector field**.

Vector fields are the setting for the rest of the course. Next we will add up a field along a curve (§15.2) and ask when the answer depends only on the endpoints (§15.3). First we need to be able to read a vector field, sketch it, and recognize the special ones that come from a single scalar function.

By the end of this section you should be able to:

1. Evaluate a vector field at a point and tell a vector field from a scalar field.
2. Describe a field from its formula (direction pattern and how the length changes) and sketch it with representative arrows.
3. Write the gradient field $$\nabla f$$ of a scalar function, and say what it means for a field to be conservative.
4. Apply the component test $$\partial P/\partial y = \partial Q/\partial x$$ to rule out conservative fields.

### Vector fields in the plane and in space

A **vector field** in the plane assigns to each point $$(x,y)$$ of a region a vector

$$\mathbf{F}(x,y) = \langle P(x,y),\,Q(x,y)\rangle = P(x,y)\,\mathbf{i} + Q(x,y)\,\mathbf{j},$$

where $$P$$ and $$Q$$ are ordinary scalar functions. In space, $$\mathbf{F}(x,y,z) = \langle P, Q, R\rangle$$. To sketch a field, choose a grid of points, compute $$\mathbf{F}$$ at each, and draw the vector with its **tail at that point**. Wind maps, water flow, gravity, and electric fields are all vector fields; air temperature and pressure are *scalar* fields.

> **Worked example.** Describe $$\mathbf{F}(x,y) = \langle -y,\ x\rangle$$.
>
> At $$(1,0)$$ the vector is $$\langle 0,1\rangle$$ (up); at $$(0,1)$$ it is $$\langle -1,0\rangle$$ (left); at $$(-1,0)$$ it is $$\langle 0,-1\rangle$$ (down); at $$(0,-1)$$ it is $$\langle 1,0\rangle$$ (right). At the diagonal points, $$\mathbf{F}(1,1) = \langle -1,1\rangle$$, $$\mathbf{F}(-1,1) = \langle -1,-1\rangle$$, $$\mathbf{F}(-1,-1) = \langle 1,-1\rangle$$, and $$\mathbf{F}(1,-1) = \langle 1,1\rangle$$.
>
> The length is $$\lVert\mathbf{F}\rVert = \sqrt{y^2 + x^2}$$, the distance from the origin, so arrows grow longer as you move out. The direction is always perpendicular to the position vector, because $$\mathbf{F}\cdot\langle x,y\rangle = -yx + xy = 0$$. So the arrows are tangent to circles about the origin, and the pattern is a **counterclockwise swirl**.

> **Worked example.** Describe $$\mathbf{F}(x,y) = \dfrac{\langle x,y\rangle}{2\lVert\mathbf{r}\rVert}$$ where $$\mathbf{r} = \langle x,y\rangle$$.
>
> Dividing a vector by a positive number does not change its direction, so every arrow points straight away from the origin. Its length is $$\dfrac{\lVert\langle x,y\rangle\rVert}{2\lVert\langle x,y\rangle\rVert} = \dfrac12$$ everywhere. All arrows have the same length $$\tfrac12$$; at $$(1,0)$$ the arrow is $$\langle\tfrac12,0\rangle$$ and at $$(1,1)$$ it is $$\langle\tfrac1{2\sqrt2},\tfrac1{2\sqrt2}\rangle$$. This is a uniform **outflow** from the origin (the field is not defined at the origin itself).

In space the idea is the same, only harder to draw. The constant field $$\mathbf{F}(x,y,z) = \langle 0,0,1\rangle$$ is a unit arrow pointing in the $$+z$$ direction at *every* point: a uniform upward push.

### Gravity and electric fields

Newton's law says the force between masses $$M$$ and $$m$$ at distance $$r$$ has magnitude $$GMm/r^2$$ and points toward the other mass. Put $$M$$ at the origin and $$m$$ at $$(x,y,z)$$, so $$\mathbf{r} = \langle x,y,z\rangle$$ and $$r = \lVert\mathbf{r}\rVert$$. The direction toward the origin is $$-\mathbf{r}/r$$, so

$$\mathbf{F}(x,y,z) = -\frac{GMm}{r^2}\cdot\frac{\mathbf{r}}{r} = -\frac{GMm}{r^3}\,\mathbf{r} = -\frac{GMm}{(x^2+y^2+z^2)^{3/2}}\langle x,y,z\rangle.$$

Every arrow points at the origin, and doubling the distance divides its length by $$4$$.

Coulomb's law has the same shape for a charge $$Q$$ at the origin acting on a charge $$q$$. The force is repulsive when $$Qq > 0$$ and attractive when $$Qq < 0$$, and both cases are captured by one formula (here $$\varepsilon$$ is the constant in your notes):

$$\mathbf{F}(x,y,z) = \frac{\varepsilon\,Qq}{r^3}\,\mathbf{r}.$$

The sign of $$Qq$$ decides whether the arrows point out or in. Both fields are *inverse-square* fields.

### Gradient fields and conservative fields

If $$f$$ is a scalar function, its gradient $$\nabla f = \langle f_x, f_y\rangle$$ (or $$\langle f_x,f_y,f_z\rangle$$) is a vector field. It points in the direction of steepest increase of $$f$$, and its length is the rate of that increase.

A field $$\mathbf{F}$$ is **conservative** if $$\mathbf{F} = \nabla f$$ for some scalar function $$f$$, called a **potential function** for $$\mathbf{F}$$. Conservative fields matter because, as §15.3 shows, the work they do depends only on where you start and finish.

> **Worked example.** Find the gradient field of $$f(x,y) = x^2 + x\cos y$$.
>
> Hold $$y$$ fixed to get $$f_x = 2x + \cos y$$, and hold $$x$$ fixed to get $$f_y = -x\sin y$$. So
>
> $$\nabla f(x,y) = \langle 2x + \cos y,\ -x\sin y\rangle.$$
>
> At $$(1, \pi/2)$$ this is $$\langle 2, -1\rangle$$: from that point $$f$$ climbs fastest toward the right and slightly down, where $$f(1,\pi/2) = 1$$.

### A test that rules fields out

If $$\mathbf{F} = \langle P, Q\rangle = \nabla f$$, then $$P = f_x$$ and $$Q = f_y$$, so

$$\frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = f_{xy} = f_{yx} = \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x}$$

by Clairaut's theorem. In space the same argument gives $$P_y = Q_x$$, $$P_z = R_x$$, and $$Q_z = R_y$$. This is the **component test**. If any of these equations fails at some point, $$\mathbf{F}$$ is **not** conservative. Passing the test is not quite the whole story, and §15.3 explains exactly when it is enough.

> **Worked example.** For the gradient field above, $$P = 2x + \cos y$$ and $$Q = -x\sin y$$, so $$P_y = -\sin y = Q_x$$. The test passes, as it must. For the swirl $$\mathbf{F} = \langle -y, x\rangle$$ we get $$P_y = -1$$ but $$Q_x = 1$$, so the test fails: **the swirl is not conservative.** No function $$f$$ has $$\langle -y,x\rangle$$ as its gradient. Keep this in mind. A field that goes around in circles cannot be the "uphill direction" of anything.

### Practice & reflection

1. Explain the difference between a scalar field and a vector field. Give one example of each from engineering or physics.
2. The field $$\langle x,\,y\rangle$$ and the field $$\langle x,\,y\rangle/\lVert\langle x,y\rangle\rVert$$ both point away from the origin. How do their arrows differ? What would a sketch of each look like along the $$x$$-axis?
3. Gravity pulls with strength $$GMm/r^2$$. If you move from distance $$r$$ to distance $$3r$$, by what factor does the force change, and do the arrows still point at the same place?
4. Suppose $$\nabla f(a,b) = \langle 0, 0\rangle$$ at some point. What does the arrow there look like, and what might that say about the surface $$z = f(x,y)$$ above $$(a,b)$$?
5. Your friend says the component test passes for the swirl $$\langle -y, x\rangle$$ because "the two minus signs cancel." Where exactly did the algebra go wrong?
