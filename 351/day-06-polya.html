Paper first, then check in. This page goes with the Day 6 handout, one card per Part. Do Part A on paper, then open the Part A card and tell it what you got: numbers, vectors, matrices. If something is off, the page tries to name the slip, and hints are there when you want them (each one a little more specific). Then on to Part B, and so on.

The paper is what you turn in. You’ll see answers here as you check your work. That’s on purpose: they’re for checking, not copying. Your handout, with your own reasoning, is what goes on Canvas, along with the two Skeptic answers and the reading sentence.

Typing tips: a vector is a column of boxes, one entry per box. Decimals like 4.5 and fractions like 9/2 both work. Your place is saved in this browser, so you can stop and come back.

Walk-through · Part A

Two points: no problem

Part A. Do Part A on paper first, then check in here.
The data: three points \((t, y)\): \((0, 6)\), \((1, 0)\), \((2, 0)\). We want a line \(y = c + m\,t\) (intercept \(c\), slope \(m\)) through the data.
1. Use only points 1 and 2. Write the two equations that say the line passes through both, with unknowns \(c\) and \(m\). Solve them.
2. Write the same system as a matrix equation \(A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}\) with \(\mathbf{x}=\begin{pmatrix}c\\m\end{pmatrix}\). What are \(A\) and \(\mathbf{b}\)? What does each row of \(A\) correspond to?

Goal: Check your line through points 1 and 2, and your \(A\) and \(\mathbf b\) for it. It’s the linear algebra you already know, with one twist that matters all semester: here the unknowns are the line’s \(c\) and \(m\), and the data \(t\) and \(y\) are the coefficients. Part B adds point 3.

Walk-through · Part B

Three points: trouble

Part B. Do Part B on paper first, then check in here.
The data: \((t, y)\) = \((0, 6)\), \((1, 0)\), \((2, 0)\); the line \(y = c + m\,t\).
1. Now use all three points. Write the three equations and the \(3\times 2\) matrix \(A\) and vector \(\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^3\).
2. Row-reduce the augmented matrix \([\,A \mid \mathbf{b}\,]\) (subtract row 1 from rows 2 and 3, then clear the second column). What does the last row say? What does that mean about the system?
3. Plot the three points. Explain in words, without any algebra, why no line can pass through all three.
Skeptic. “Fine, then use a parabola \(y=c+mt+qt^2\). It has three unknowns, so it can hit all three points.” True! Now imagine 392 cars instead of 3 points. How many unknowns would you need to hit every car exactly, and would you trust that model to predict a 393rd car?

Goal: Check your \(3\times 2\) system and its row reduction, and say why it has no solution, in symbols and in a picture. Part A’s line hit two points exactly. A third point turns the system inconsistent, and real data always has more points than unknowns. Part C looks at the same fact through the columns of \(A\).

Walk-through · Part C

The column picture

Part C. Do Part C on paper first, then check in here.
A second way to read \(A\mathbf{x}\): as a combination of the columns of \(A\), \(A\mathbf{x} = c\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\1\end{pmatrix} + m\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\2\end{pmatrix} = c\,\mathbf{a}_1 + m\,\mathbf{a}_2\).
1. The vector \(A\mathbf{x}\) lists the line’s three predictions, one per point. Compute \(A\mathbf{x}\) for the line \(y=6-6t\) from Part A. Which entries match \(\mathbf{b}\)?
2. Solving \(A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}\) means writing \(\mathbf{b}\) as a combination of \(\mathbf{a}_1\) and \(\mathbf{a}_2\). The set of all such combinations is the span of the columns (MML §2.6.1). Here it is a plane through the origin in \(\mathbb{R}^3\). Fill in the sentence: \(A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}\) has a solution exactly when \(\mathbf{b}\) ________.
3. A vector perpendicular to that plane is \(\mathbf{n}=(1,-2,1)\). Check that \(\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{a}_1=0\) and \(\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{a}_2=0\). Then compute \(\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{b}\). How does this number prove, a second way, that the system has no solution?
4. Make up your own data vector \(\mathbf{b}\) (three \(y\)-values at \(t=0,1,2\)) that does lie on a line. Check it with \(\mathbf{n}\).
5. The rank of a matrix is the number of independent columns (MML §2.6.2). What is the rank of \(A\)? Of the augmented matrix \([\,A\mid\mathbf{b}\,]\)? Why does a jump in rank mean “no solution”?
From the reading. In one sentence, in your own words: what is the span of a set of vectors?

Goal: Read \(A\mathbf x\) as a combination of the columns of \(A\), and prove two more ways (a normal vector and rank) that \(\mathbf b\) is out of reach. Part B said “no solution” by row reduction. Here the same fact becomes a picture: every \(A\mathbf x\) lies in one plane, and \(\mathbf b\) sticks out of it. Part D asks how close we can get.

Walk-through · Part D

What should “closest” mean?

Part D. Do Part D on paper first, then check in here.
For any line, the residual vector \(\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{b}-A\mathbf{x}\) lists the three misses (actual minus predicted). Five candidate lines: (i) \(y=6-6t\) (done for you: \(A\mathbf x = (6, 0, -6)\), \(\mathbf r = (0, 0, 6)\), scores 6, 6, 36), (ii) \(y=6-3t\), (iii) \(y=4.5-3t\), (iv) \(y=5-3t\), (v) \(y=2\). Three scores: \(|r_1|+|r_2|+|r_3|\), largest \(|r_i|\), and \(r_1^2+r_2^2+r_3^2\).
1. Fill in the table.
2. Circle the winner in each of the three scoring columns. Do the three scores agree on the best line?
3. Sketch the three points with lines (ii) and (iv). In words, how does each line “compromise”?
4. The third score is \(\|\mathbf{r}\|^2\), the squared length of the residual vector. So it is the squared distance in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) from the data \(\mathbf{b}\) to the prediction \(A\mathbf{x}\). Explain why “make \(\|\mathbf{b}-A\mathbf{x}\|\) as small as possible” means “find the point of the plane nearest to \(\mathbf{b}\).”
5. For line (iv), compute \(\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{a}_1\) and \(\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{a}_2\). Then compare \(\mathbf{r}\) with \(\mathbf{n}\) from Part C. Anything special? (Hold that thought: it’s the whole story on Monday, Feb 1.)
Skeptic. One typo, with more data. Five points sit exactly on the line \(y = t\), at \(t = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4\), but someone typed the first \(y\)-value as 5 instead of 0. Least squares now picks the flat line \(y = 3\). Find all three scores for the honest line \(y = t\) and for the flat line \(y = 3\). Which score lets one bad point drag the line around the most, and which shrugs it off? Is there a reason to prefer squaring anyway?

Goal: Check your residuals and scores, see that “best line” depends on the score, and connect the squared score to the nearest point of a plane. Part C showed that no line hits \(\mathbf b\). Here you compare near misses three ways. The squared score turns “best line” into geometry, and Monday (Feb 1) finishes the story: least squares is a projection.
