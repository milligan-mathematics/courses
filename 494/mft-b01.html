Paper first, then check in. Give yourself 15 minutes for the five problems on paper, no calculator, the way the real test runs. Then open each card. If you already have an answer, press Check my answer from paper: one try. Right, and you can skip to Look back for a faster route or a variation. Not right, or not sure, and the walk-through takes it one step at a time: Understand, Plan, Carry out, Look back.

Every wrong choice has a story. The test writers build the wrong answers out of the slips people actually make. When you pick one here, the page tells you which slip leads there, so you can spot it next time.

Your place is saved in this browser, so you can stop and come back. Bring the one card that bugged you most to our check-in.

Walk-through · Set B1, Problem 1

A 0/0 limit with a cosine

1. \(\displaystyle \lim_{x\to 0}\frac{1-\cos(3x)}{x^2}=\)
(A) \(0\)
(B) \(\frac{3}{2}\)
(C) \(3\)
(D) \(\frac{9}{2}\)
(E) \(9\)

Goal: Find the limit by seeing how fast the top shrinks compared with the bottom. Small-angle facts like \(1-\cos u \approx \frac{u^2}{2}\) and \(\sin u \approx u\) turn many MFT limits into one line of arithmetic.

Walk-through · Set B1, Problem 2

Scaling and inverting a determinant

2. Let \(A\) be a \(3\times 3\) matrix with \(\det(A)=4\). What is \(\det(2A^{-1})\)?
(A) \(\frac{1}{8}\)
(B) \(\frac{1}{2}\)
(C) \(2\)
(D) \(8\)
(E) \(32\)

Goal: Combine two determinant rules: what scaling does, and what inverting does. MFT linear algebra questions lean hard on a handful of determinant rules: products, inverses, transposes, scalar multiples.

Walk-through · Set B1, Problem 3

A huge power, a small remainder

3. What is the remainder when \(7^{100}\) is divided by \(5\)?
(A) \(0\)
(B) \(1\)
(C) \(2\)
(D) \(3\)
(E) \(4\)

Goal: Find the remainder without ever computing \(7^{100}\). Reducing the base, finding the cycle of powers, and Fermat’s little theorem handle almost every “remainder of a big power” question on the test.

Walk-through · Set B1, Problem 4

Which machine made the bad part?

4. A factory has two machines. Machine A makes \(60\%\) of the parts, and \(2\%\) of its parts are defective. Machine B makes the other \(40\%\), and \(5\%\) of its parts are defective. A part is chosen at random and is found to be defective. What is the probability that it was made by machine B?
(A) \(\frac{1}{20}\)
(B) \(\frac{2}{5}\)
(C) \(\frac{1}{2}\)
(D) \(\frac{3}{5}\)
(E) \(\frac{5}{8}\)

Goal: Turn the percentages into counts, then look only at the defective parts. Imagining 1000 items (natural frequencies) is Bayes’ theorem without the formula, and it’s hard to get backwards.

Walk-through · Set B1, Problem 5

Composing functions: who inherits what?

5. Let \(f:A\to B\) and \(g:B\to C\) be functions. Which of the following must be true?
  I. If \(g\circ f\) is one-to-one, then \(f\) is one-to-one.
  II. If \(g\circ f\) is onto, then \(f\) is onto.
  III. If \(g\circ f\) is one-to-one, then \(g\) is one-to-one.
(A) I only
(B) II only
(C) III only
(D) I and II only
(E) I and III only

Goal: Decide each statement by proving it or breaking it with a small example. “Must be true” items with I, II, III are a third of the test’s style. Tiny finite counterexamples settle most of them in under a minute.
