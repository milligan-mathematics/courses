How these work. Each walk-through takes one problem through Pólya’s four phases. You answer one step at a time and get feedback right away; when something isn’t right, read why and try again. Hints are there when you ask for them. Your place is saved in this browser, so you can stop and come back.

Two things to keep in view. Part (b) of 2.0.13 takes the rational-density result, Theorem 2.0.11(a), as given: the reading sketches it, and this problem is about what comes after. Some steps ask you to find the first false line in a classmate’s proof, and some ask you to put a proof’s lines back in order. Read each line as a claim: is it true, and does it follow from the lines above it?

The walk-throughs end in a full proof, so the proof you bring to class should still be your own, in your own words.

Pólya walk-through · Problem 2.0.13

Scale the gap, squeeze, scale back

Problem 2.0.13. (a) Prove that the product of a nonzero rational number and an irrational number is irrational.
(b) Use the result of part (a) to prove Theorem 2.0.11, the density of the rationals and the irrationals.

Goal: Prove part (a) by contradiction. Then, for part (b), use it to put an irrational number between any two distinct real numbers: scale the gap, find a rational in it, and scale back. Part (a) is the tool for 2.1.1 and 2.1.2, where \(\sqrt2 + \sqrt2 = 2\sqrt2\) and \(2^{3/2} = 2\sqrt2\) are irrational. Part (b) has the “couple of details” the reading leaves for this problem: the rational you find may be zero.

Pólya walk-through · Problem 2.1.1

Sums that stay, and sums that don’t

Problem 2.1.1. Determine if each of the following is always rational or always irrational. Justify your answers.
(a) The sum of two rational numbers.
(b) The sum of two irrational numbers.
(c) The sum of a rational and an irrational number.

Goal: Part (c) is a contradiction. Part (b) uses 2.0.13(a): \(\sqrt2 + \sqrt2 = 2\sqrt2\) is irrational, because \(2\) is a nonzero rational. Part (c) is a pattern you’ll use again in limit proofs: subtract what you know, and see what’s left.

Pólya walk-through · Problem 2.1.2

Rational powers can leave the rationals

Problem 2.1.2. Is it possible to have two rational numbers, \(a\) and \(b\), such that \(a^b\) is irrational? If so, display an example of such \(a\) and \(b\). If not, prove that it is not possible.

Goal: Decide the question with an example: find rational \(a\) and \(b\) with \(a^b\) irrational, and justify that it is irrational. It sets up 2.1.3, which asks the reverse question: can an irrational power be rational? The example here uses the reading’s \(\sqrt2\), whose irrationality is the proof on Day 3.

Pólya walk-through · Problem 2.1.3

Irrational to an irrational power

Problem 2.1.3. Decide if it is possible to have two irrational numbers, \(a\) and \(b\), such that \(a^b\) is rational. Prove it in either case.

Goal: Decide without finding a specific pair: split on whether one number, \(x = (\sqrt2)^{\sqrt2}\), is rational, and in each case name a pair of irrationals whose power is rational. It is the reverse of 2.1.2. The proof is non-constructive: it shows a pair exists without saying which one. Later in the course, the Intermediate and Extreme Value Theorems give points the same way.
