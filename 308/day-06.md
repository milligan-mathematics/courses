  <h2>One hand on a 20-hour clock</h2>

  <p>Judson starts with \(3\mathbb Z\) and with \(\langle 2 \rangle\) in \(\mathbb Z_6\): take one element, keep
    adding it to itself, and see what you collect. Here the same idea runs on a 20-hour clock. The hand starts at
    \(k = 1\) and jumps \(k\) hours at a time, wrapping past \(20\). Hour \(0\) is at the top.</p>

  <div id="d06-clock"></div>

  <p>Before you slide the hand to \(k = 8\), predict:</p>
  <ul>
    <li>Starting at \(0\), which hours does the hand visit, in order, before it lands on \(0\) again?</li>
    <li>How many different hours is that?</li>
    <li>Does the hand ever reach an odd hour?</li>
  </ul>

  <p>Now slide it and check. The hours the hand visits form a subgroup of \(\mathbb Z_{20}\). The readout calls it
    \(\langle k \rangle\), the cyclic subgroup generated by \(k\), and gives the order of \(k\). The order is the
    number of hours in that subgroup, which is also the number of jumps before the hand returns to \(0\).</p>
</div>

  <h2>Orders in a pentagon</h2>

  <p>The table holds the ten symmetries of a regular pentagon. Judson's \(D_n\) is the group of symmetries of a regular
    \(n\)-gon, so here \(n = 5\). The element \(r\) is the turn by \(72^\circ\), and \(s\) is a flip. Every other
    symmetry is a product of \(r\)s and \(s\)s.</p>

  <p>Before you shade or click anything, predict how many of the ten symmetries have order \(2\), and how many have
    order \(5\).</p>

  <div id="d06-d5"></div>
  <p><button type="button" class="btn411 ghost" id="d06-shade">Shade by order</button></p>

  <p>Now press the button and check. The shading groups the table by order. Clicking a heading gives that element's
    order, its powers, and its inverse.</p>
  <p>Look at the turns \(e, r, r^2, r^3, r^4\). They form the cyclic subgroup \(\langle r \rangle\), and it has five
    elements. Now look at the flips. Each one is its own inverse, so what is the smallest positive power of a flip that
    gives the identity?</p>

  <h2>Order, and the smallest subgroup</h2>

  <div class="mc" data-answer="b">
    <p class="mc-q">In \(D_5\), let \(r\) be the turn by \(72^\circ\). What is the order of \(r\)?</p>
    <button class="mc-opt" data-key="a">4</button>
    <button class="mc-opt" data-key="b">5</button>
    <button class="mc-opt" data-key="c">6</button>
    <button class="mc-opt" data-key="d">10</button>
    <div class="mc-fb" data-key="a"><p>Four turns would be \(288^\circ\), and that doesn't bring the pentagon back. The
      powers \(r, r^2, r^3, r^4\) are none of them the identity.</p></div>
    <div class="mc-fb" data-key="b"><p>Right. \(r^5\) is the identity, and no smaller positive power is. The distinct
      powers are \(e, r, r^2, r^3, r^4\), five of them.</p></div>
    <div class="mc-fb" data-key="c"><p>Six counts \(e\) twice. The list \(e, r, r^2, r^3, r^4, r^5\) ends with \(r^5 = e\),
      which repeats the start. There are five distinct powers, and five multiplications by \(r\) return you to \(e\).</p></div>
    <div class="mc-fb" data-key="d"><p>Ten is the order of the whole group \(D_5\), its number of symmetries. This question
      asks about one element.</p></div>
  </div>

  <div class="mc" data-answer="b">
    <p class="mc-q">Let \(H\) be any subgroup of \(G\) that contains \(a\). Which statement is always true?</p>
    <button class="mc-opt" data-key="a">\(H = \langle a \rangle\)</button>
    <button class="mc-opt" data-key="b">\(\langle a \rangle \subseteq H\)</button>
    <button class="mc-opt" data-key="c">\(H \subseteq \langle a \rangle\)</button>
    <button class="mc-opt" data-key="d">\(H\) is cyclic</button>
    <div class="mc-fb" data-key="a"><p>Not always, because a subgroup can be bigger. In \(\mathbb Z_6\), the whole group
      contains \(2\), but \(\langle 2 \rangle = \{0, 2, 4\}\) is smaller.</p></div>
    <div class="mc-fb" data-key="b"><p>Right. \(\langle a \rangle\) is the smallest subgroup containing \(a\), so it sits
      inside every subgroup that contains \(a\). Judson proves this by showing that such a subgroup must contain all
      the powers of \(a\).</p></div>
    <div class="mc-fb" data-key="c"><p>Backwards. The small set \(\langle a \rangle\) sits inside \(H\), not the other way
      around. The whole of \(\mathbb Z_6\) isn't inside \(\{0, 2, 4\}\).</p></div>
    <div class="mc-fb" data-key="d"><p>Not always. In the Klein four-group \(\{e, x, y, z\}\), where every non-identity
      element has order \(2\), the whole group contains \(x\) but isn't cyclic.</p></div>
  </div>
</div>

  <h2>Abelian, and does the converse hold?</h2>

  <div class="mc" data-answer="b">
    <p class="mc-q">Judson proves that every cyclic group is abelian. Which statement is true?</p>
    <button class="mc-opt" data-key="a">Every abelian group is cyclic.</button>
    <button class="mc-opt" data-key="b">Some abelian groups are not cyclic.</button>
    <button class="mc-opt" data-key="c">Only infinite groups are abelian.</button>
    <button class="mc-opt" data-key="d">Every cyclic group is finite.</button>
    <div class="mc-fb" data-key="a"><p>The converse fails. The Klein four-group is abelian, but no single element generates
      it. Each non-identity element generates only itself and the identity.</p></div>
    <div class="mc-fb" data-key="b"><p>Right. The Klein four-group is the standard example. Commutativity is not enough to
      make a group cyclic.</p></div>
    <div class="mc-fb" data-key="c"><p>No. \(\mathbb Z_6\) is finite, and it's abelian. Finite groups can be abelian.</p></div>
    <div class="mc-fb" data-key="d"><p>No. \(\mathbb Z\) is cyclic, generated by \(1\), and it's infinite.</p></div>
  </div>

  <div class="mc" data-answer="a">
    <p class="mc-q">In \(D_5\), let \(s\) be a flip. Which set is the cyclic subgroup \(\langle s \rangle\)?</p>
    <button class="mc-opt" data-key="a">\(\{e, s\}\), the identity and the flip</button>
    <button class="mc-opt" data-key="b">\(\{e, r, r^2, r^3, r^4\}\), the turns</button>
    <button class="mc-opt" data-key="c">\(\{s, rs, r^2s, r^3s, r^4s\}\), the flips</button>
    <button class="mc-opt" data-key="d">All ten symmetries of the pentagon</button>
    <div class="mc-fb" data-key="a"><p>Right. \(s \cdot s = e\), so the powers of \(s\) are only \(e\) and \(s\).</p></div>
    <div class="mc-fb" data-key="b"><p>That's the subgroup of turns, \(\langle r \rangle\). It doesn't contain the flip
      \(s\).</p></div>
    <div class="mc-fb" data-key="c"><p>A subgroup has to contain the identity, and this set doesn't. The powers of \(s\)
      start with \(e\), not with a flip.</p></div>
    <div class="mc-fb" data-key="d"><p>That would be the whole group. \(\langle s \rangle\) contains only the powers of
      \(s\), and there are just two of them.</p></div>
  </div>
</div>

<div class="act scaffold">
  <div class="act-type">Proof Scaffold</div>
  <h2>The smallest subgroup containing \(a\)</h2>

  <p>Section 4.1 defines \(\langle a \rangle = \{a^k : k \in \mathbb Z\}\), then proves two things about it: that it is a
    subgroup, and that it is the smallest subgroup containing \(a\). Judson's proof is short, and one phrase, "by closure," hides
    the one place where inverses matter. Commit to each step before you open it.</p>

  <div class="proof-scaffold">
    <ol class="scaffold-steps">
      <li class="sstep">
        <div class="sstep-prompt">To show \(\langle a \rangle\) is a subgroup, what three things must you check?</div>
        <div class="sstep-body"><p>That it contains the identity, that it is closed under the operation, and that it is
          closed under inverses.</p></div>
      </li>
      <li class="sstep">
        <div class="sstep-prompt">Why is the identity in \(\langle a \rangle\)?</div>
        <div class="sstep-body"><p>\(e = a^0\), and \(0\) is an integer.</p></div>
      </li>
      <li class="sstep">
        <div class="sstep-prompt">Take \(g = a^m\) and \(h = a^n\) in \(\langle a \rangle\). What is \(gh\), and why is it in
          \(\langle a \rangle\)?</div>
        <div class="sstep-body"><p>\(gh = a^m a^n = a^{m+n}\). Since \(m + n\) is an integer, \(gh\) is a power of \(a\).</p></div>
      </li>
      <li class="sstep">
        <div class="sstep-prompt">What is the inverse of \(g = a^n\), and why is it in \(\langle a \rangle\)?</div>
        <div class="sstep-body"><p>\(g^{-1} = a^{-n}\), because \(a^n a^{-n} = a^0 = e\). Since \(-n\) is an integer,
          \(a^{-n} \in \langle a \rangle\).</p></div>
      </li>
      <li class="sstep">
        <div class="sstep-prompt">Let \(H\) be any subgroup with \(a \in H\). Why must \(H\) contain every \(a^k\), including
          the negative powers?</div>
        <div class="sstep-body"><p>Closure gives \(a, a^2, a^3, \ldots\). Since \(H\) is a subgroup, it contains
          \(a^{-1}\), and closure applied to \(a^{-1}\) gives \(a^{-2}, a^{-3}, \ldots\). So \(H\) contains all of
          \(\langle a \rangle\). Judson's one-line "by closure" is hiding this inverse step.</p></div>
      </li>
    </ol>
  </div>
</div>

<div class="act flaw">
  <div class="act-type">Spot the Flaw</div>
  <h2>How many generators does a finite cyclic group have?</h2>

  <p>A classmate claims that every finite cyclic group has exactly one generator. Here is the argument. Click the line
    that isn't justified.</p>

  <div class="flaw-widget" data-flaw="5">
    <div class="flawlist">
      <button class="fline" type="button">Let \(G\) be a finite cyclic group, and let \(a\) and \(b\) both generate \(G\). We
        want \(a = b\).</button>
      <button class="fline" type="button">Since \(G\) is finite, \(b = a^k\) for some integer \(k \ge 1\).</button>
      <button class="fline" type="button">Likewise \(a = b^m\) for some integer \(m \ge 1\).</button>
      <button class="fline" type="button">Substituting, \(a = a^{km}\), so \(a^{km - 1} = e\).</button>
      <button class="fline" type="button">So \(km = 1\).</button>
      <button class="fline" type="button">Since \(k, m \ge 1\), we get \(k = m = 1\), so \(b = a\). \(\blacksquare\)</button>
    </div>
    <div class="flaw-verdict" data-key="1"><p><strong>Fine.</strong> This is the setup.</p></div>
    <div class="flaw-verdict" data-key="2"><p><strong>Fine.</strong> Since \(G\) is finite, the powers \(a, a^2, \ldots,
      a^{|G|}\) list every element of \(G\), so some positive power of \(a\) equals \(b\).</p></div>
    <div class="flaw-verdict" data-key="3"><p><strong>Fine.</strong> Same reasoning, with \(b\) in the role of \(a\).</p></div>
    <div class="flaw-verdict" data-key="4"><p><strong>Fine.</strong> Multiply both sides by \(a^{-1}\) to get
      \(a^{km-1} = e\).</p></div>
    <div class="flaw-verdict" data-key="5"><p><strong>This is the flaw.</strong> \(a^{km-1} = e\) does not force
      \(km - 1\) to be \(0\), because a power can be the identity with a nonzero exponent. Test it in \(\mathbb Z_9\),
      written additively, with \(a = 1\) and \(b = 2\). Check that \(2\) generates by listing its multiples. Then
      \(b = 2a\), so \(k = 2\), and \(a = 5b = 10a\), so \(m = 5\). Now \(km = 10\), not \(1\), yet \(9a = 0\), so
      \(a^9 = e\) with \(9 \neq 0\).</p></div>
    <div class="flaw-verdict" data-key="6"><p><strong>Fine as a deduction.</strong> If \(km = 1\) with \(k, m \ge 1\), then
      \(k = m = 1\). The trouble is one line earlier, so this step is only as good as line 5.</p></div>
  </div>
</div>
