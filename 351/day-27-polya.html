Walk-through · Part A

Two bells, one model

Part A. Do Part A on paper first, then check in here.
A used-car lot has two kinds of cars. 40% are big V8s, whose mpg follows bell A (mean 15, standard deviation 2). 60% are small cars, whose mpg follows bell B (mean 27, standard deviation 4). The recipe for one car: flip a weighted coin to pick its kind, then draw its mpg from that kind’s bell.
A bell’s height, without the constant \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\) in front: \(H(x;\,\mu,\sigma) = \frac{1}{\sigma}\,e^{-z^2/2}\), with \(z = \frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\). Values you’ll need:\[\begin{array}{c|ccccc} z & 0 & 0.5 & 1 & 1.5 & 2\\ \hline e^{-z^2/2} & 1 & 0.8825 & 0.6065 & 0.3247 & 0.1353 \end{array}\]\[\begin{array}{c|cccc} z & 2.5 & 3 & 3.5 & 4+\\ \hline e^{-z^2/2} & 0.0439 & 0.0111 & 0.0022 & \approx 0 \end{array}\]The mixture’s height is the weighted sum, MML (11.3) with \(K = 2\): \(p(x) \propto \pi_A\,H(x;\,15,2) + \pi_B\,H(x;\,27,4)\), with \(\pi_A = 0.4\), \(\pi_B = 0.6\).
1. Before you see a car’s mpg, what is the chance it’s a V8? Which word from Day 24 (prior, likelihood, evidence, posterior) fits that number?
2. Compute the mixture height at \(x = 15\) and at \(x = 27\). Which hump in the picture is taller, and why, when 60% of the cars are small?
3. Why must \(\pi_A + \pi_B = 1\)? (Think about the area under the black curve.)

Goal: Read the mixture model: two weighted bells added together, and what makes one hump taller than the other. On Friday one bell didn’t fit mpg, and splitting by cylinders showed two piles. Part A builds the two-bell model; Part B uses Day 24’s Bayes’ theorem on it; Part C is the step that lets a computer learn the bells.

Walk-through · Part B

Who’s responsible?

Part B. Do Part B on paper first, then check in here.
A car gets \(x\) mpg. Which bell did it come from? This is Day 24’s medical test again: “V8” plays the role of “sick,” the mpg plays the role of the test result, and Bayes’ theorem gives the responsibility of bell A for the point \(x\) (MML (11.17)):\[r_A(x) = p(\text{V8}\mid x) = \frac{\pi_A\,H(x;\,15,2)}{\pi_A\,H(x;\,15,2) + \pi_B\,H(x;\,27,4)},\qquad r_B(x) = 1 - r_A(x).\]1. Label the prior, the likelihood, and the evidence in the formula for \(r_A(x)\). Why is it fine to use \(H\) instead of the true density here? (What happens to the \(\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}\)?)
2. Fill in the table for the five cars marked with triangles in the picture. Keep 4 decimals in the heights.\[\begin{array}{c|ccc|ccc|cc} \text{car } x & z_A & H_A & 0.4\,H_A & z_B & H_B & 0.6\,H_B & r_A & r_B\\ \hline 13 & -1 & 0.3033 & 0.1213 & & & & & \\ 17 & & & & & & & & \\ 19 & & & & & & & & \\ 21 & & & & & & & & \\ 31 & & & & & & & & \end{array}\]3. Which car is the bells’ hardest call? Which cars are “obvious”? Mark the hardest one on the picture and say what the two colored curves are doing there.
4. Look at \(x = 19\): it is exactly 2 standard deviations from each mean. Yet the responsibilities aren’t 50–50. Which bell wins, and which of the two differences between the bells (their weights or their widths) is responsible?

Goal: Use Bayes’ theorem to split each car between the two bells, and see what decides the close calls. Part A built the mixture. Here Day 24’s posterior becomes the responsibility: the prior is the weight, the likelihood is the bell height. Part C uses these numbers to move the bells.

Walk-through · Part C

Moving the bells

Part C. Do Part C on paper first, then check in here.
In practice nobody hands us the bells: we have to learn them from the cars. The trick (called EM) is to alternate two steps. You just did the first: given the bells, compute responsibilities. The second: given the responsibilities, update each bell, letting each car count only as much as the bell is responsible for it.
1. The new mean of bell A is a weighted average of the five cars, with weights \(r_A\): \(\displaystyle \mu_A^{\text{new}} = \frac{\sum r_A(x_i)\,x_i}{\sum r_A(x_i)}\). Compute it (the table has columns \(x_i\), \(r_A(x_i)\), \(r_A(x_i)\,x_i\), and a sum row). Did bell A move left or right from 15? Which car pulled it, and why that one?
2. The new weight of bell A is the average responsibility: \(\pi_A^{\text{new}} = \frac15\sum r_A(x_i)\). Compute it. In words, what is it measuring?
3. Write the matching formula for \(\mu_B^{\text{new}}\) (you don’t need to compute it). Why does the car at 31 count fully for bell B and not at all for bell A?
4. Suppose you repeated the two steps over and over. Describe in a sentence or two what you expect to happen. (On Wednesday you’ll watch it.)
Skeptic. “A car at 21 mpg is exactly halfway between 15 and 27, so each bell should be 50% responsible for it. And anyway, why not just give each car to the closer mean and skip the formula?” Use your table to find the flaw. Under what conditions on the two bells would the halfway car be 50–50? (Challenge: a car with only 1 mpg is far to the left of bell A. Which bell is more responsible for it? The answer is surprising; explain it from the widths.)

Goal: Update bell A from the responsibilities, and see why “nearest mean” isn’t the same as “most responsible.” Part B went from bells to responsibilities. Part C goes back, from responsibilities to bells. On Wednesday the computer runs this loop until the bells stop moving, starting from bells that are badly wrong.
