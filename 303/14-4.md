# §14.4: Triple Integrals

A double integral adds up a function over a flat region. A **triple integral** adds up a function over a *solid*. With $$f = 1$$ it gives the volume of the solid. With $$f$$ equal to a density it gives the mass, and with a little more work it gives the center of mass and the moments of inertia that engineers use to ask how hard a part is to spin.

Nothing about the idea is new: cut the solid into tiny boxes, multiply, add, and take a limit. What is new is the work of *describing the solid*. In two dimensions you found limits for a region in the plane. Now you have to find limits for a region in space, and the best order is not always obvious.

By the end of this section you should be able to:

1. Evaluate a triple integral over a box as an iterated integral, in any order.
2. Describe a solid bounded by surfaces with limits of integration, and set up the integral in a chosen order.
3. Rewrite a triple integral in another order, and say which order is easier.
4. Find the volume, mass, center of mass, and moments of inertia of a solid.

### Triple integrals and Riemann sums

Cut a box $$S = [a,b]\times[c,d]\times[e,f]$$ into small boxes, each with volume $$\Delta V = \Delta x\,\Delta y\,\Delta z$$. Pick a sample point $$(x^*, y^*, z^*)$$ in each small box and add up $$f(x^*, y^*, z^*)\,\Delta V$$. The limit as the boxes shrink is the **triple integral**

$$\iiint_S f(x,y,z)\,dV.$$

**Fubini's theorem.** If $$f$$ is continuous on the box $$S = [p,q]\times[r,s]\times[t,u]$$, then

$$\iiint_S f\,dV = \int_p^q \int_r^s \int_t^u f(x,y,z)\,dz\,dy\,dx,$$

and all six orders of integration give the same number. For a box, choose whichever order makes the antiderivatives easiest.

> **Worked example.** Evaluate $$\displaystyle\iiint_S (2xy + 3xz^2)\,dV$$ over $$S = [0,2]\times[1,3]\times[-1,1]$$.
>
> Integrate $$z$$ first, then $$y$$, then $$x$$:
>
> $$\int_{-1}^{1} (2xy + 3xz^2)\,dz = 4xy + 2x, \qquad \int_1^3 (4xy + 2x)\,dy = 20x, \qquad \int_0^2 20x\,dx = 40.$$
>
> The value is $$40$$. Integrating in any of the other five orders gives $$40$$ too.

Triple integrals inherit the properties of double integrals: you can pull out constants and split sums, you can cut a solid into non-overlapping pieces and add the integrals, and if $$f \ge g$$ on $$S$$ then $$\iiint_S f\,dV \ge \iiint_S g\,dV$$. The one to remember:

$$\text{Volume}(S) = \iiint_S 1\,dV.$$

### Solids that are not boxes

For a general solid, the limits depend on the variables outside them. To integrate $$z$$ first:

1. **Sketch** the solid and name its bounding surfaces.
2. **Innermost limits.** A line parallel to the $$z$$-axis enters the solid through a lower surface $$z = h_1(x,y)$$ and leaves through an upper surface $$z = h_2(x,y)$$.
3. **Project** the solid onto the $$xy$$-plane to get a flat region $$R$$.
4. **Middle and outer limits.** Describe $$R$$ the way you did in §14.1: $$y$$ between two curves in $$x$$, then $$x$$ between two numbers.

$$\iiint_S f\,dV = \int_a^b \int_{g_1(x)}^{g_2(x)} \int_{h_1(x,y)}^{h_2(x,y)} f(x,y,z)\,dz\,dy\,dx.$$

The innermost limits can involve two variables, the middle ones one variable, and only the outermost are plain numbers. Putting a variable in the wrong slot is the most common mistake. To integrate in a different order, project onto a different coordinate plane: onto the $$xz$$-plane to integrate $$y$$ first, onto the $$yz$$-plane to integrate $$x$$ first.

> **Worked example.** Find the volume of the solid bounded below by $$z = x^2 + y^2$$ and above by $$z = 8 - x^2 - 3y^2$$.
>
> The surfaces meet where $$x^2 + y^2 = 8 - x^2 - 3y^2$$, that is $$x^2 + 2y^2 = 4$$, an ellipse. That ellipse is the boundary of the projection $$R$$, with $$-2 \le x \le 2$$ and $$\lvert y\rvert  \le \sqrt{(4 - x^2)/2}$$. The height of the solid above $$(x,y)$$ is $$(8 - x^2 - 3y^2) - (x^2 + y^2) = 8 - 2x^2 - 4y^2$$, so
>
> $$V = \int_{-2}^{2} \int_{-\sqrt{(4-x^2)/2}}^{\sqrt{(4-x^2)/2}} \int_{x^2+y^2}^{8-x^2-3y^2} dz\,dy\,dx = \int_{-2}^{2} \int_{-\sqrt{(4-x^2)/2}}^{\sqrt{(4-x^2)/2}} (8 - 2x^2 - 4y^2)\,dy\,dx.$$
>
> The $$y$$-integral simplifies to $$\tfrac{4\sqrt2}{3}(4 - x^2)^{3/2}$$, and $$\int_{-2}^{2}(4 - x^2)^{3/2}dx = 6\pi$$ by the substitution $$x = 2\sin\theta$$ (Section 7.3). So
>
> $$V = \tfrac{4\sqrt2}{3}\cdot 6\pi = 8\sqrt2\,\pi \approx 35.54.$$
>
> The other order, $$dz\,dx\,dy$$, has the same $$z$$-limits and the same ellipse, now read as $$\lvert x\rvert  \le \sqrt{4 - 2y^2}$$ and $$-\sqrt2 \le y \le \sqrt2$$. It gives the same volume.

Sometimes the order matters a great deal, as the next solid shows.

> **Worked example.** Let $$S$$ be the solid in the first octant bounded by the coordinate planes, the vertical plane $$x + y = 2$$, and the sloping plane $$z = x + 1$$. Write $$\iiint_S dV$$ in three orders.
>
> **Order $$dz\,dy\,dx$$.** Project onto the $$xy$$-plane: the triangle $$0 \le y \le 2 - x$$, $$0 \le x \le 2$$. Above it, $$0 \le z \le x + 1$$.
>
> $$V = \int_0^2 \int_0^{2-x} \int_0^{x+1} dz\,dy\,dx = \int_0^2 (2 - x)(x + 1)\,dx = \frac{10}{3}.$$
>
> **Order $$dy\,dz\,dx$$.** Project onto the $$xz$$-plane: $$0 \le x \le 2$$, $$0 \le z \le x + 1$$. Along each line parallel to the $$y$$-axis, $$0 \le y \le 2 - x$$:
>
> $$V = \int_0^2 \int_0^{x+1} \int_0^{2-x} dy\,dz\,dx = \frac{10}{3}.$$
>
> **Order $$dx\,dz\,dy$$.** Project onto the $$yz$$-plane: $$0 \le y \le 2$$ and $$0 \le z \le 3 - y$$ (the sloping plane meets the edge $$x + y = 2$$ at height $$3 - y$$). Now the line parallel to the $$x$$-axis enters through $$x = 0$$ when $$z \le 1$$ but through the sloping plane, at $$x = z - 1$$, when $$z \ge 1$$. The region needs two pieces:
>
> $$V = \int_0^2 \left[ \int_0^1 \int_0^{2-y} dx\,dz + \int_1^{3-y} \int_{z-1}^{2-y} dx\,dz \right] dy = \int_0^2 \left[(2 - y) + \tfrac12(2 - y)^2\right] dy = \frac{10}{3}.$$
>
> All three agree. The first two orders need one piece each; the third needs two. A sketch tells you that before you start.

### Mass, center of mass, and moments

Let a solid $$S$$ have density $$\rho(x,y,z)$$. Then:

- **Mass:** $$M = \iiint_S \rho\,dV$$.
- **First moments about the coordinate planes:** $$M_{yz} = \iiint_S x\rho\,dV$$, $$M_{xz} = \iiint_S y\rho\,dV$$, $$M_{xy} = \iiint_S z\rho\,dV$$.
- **Center of mass:** $$\bar x = M_{yz}/M$$, $$\bar y = M_{xz}/M$$, $$\bar z = M_{xy}/M$$. With constant density this is the **centroid**.
- **Second moments (moments of inertia):** $$I_x = \iiint_S (y^2 + z^2)\rho\,dV$$, $$I_y = \iiint_S (x^2 + z^2)\rho\,dV$$, $$I_z = \iiint_S (x^2 + y^2)\rho\,dV$$.
- **Radii of gyration:** $$r_x = \sqrt{I_x/M}$$, $$r_y = \sqrt{I_y/M}$$, $$r_z = \sqrt{I_z/M}$$.

Each factor in a second moment is the squared distance from the axis. That is why $$I_x$$ has $$y^2 + z^2$$ and not $$x^2$$.

> **Worked example.** Take the wedge from the last example, with density $$\rho(x,y,z) = z$$ (heavier toward the top). Find its mass, center of mass, and $$r_z$$.
>
> Using $$dz\,dy\,dx$$ with $$0 \le x \le 2$$, $$0 \le y \le 2 - x$$, $$0 \le z \le x + 1$$, the innermost integral $$\int_0^{x+1} z\,dz = \tfrac12(x+1)^2$$ leads to
>
> $$M = \int_0^2 \tfrac12 (2 - x)(x + 1)^2\,dx = 3, \qquad M_{yz} = \int_0^2 \tfrac12\, x(2 - x)(x + 1)^2\,dx = \frac{14}{5}.$$
>
> In the same way $$M_{xz} = \tfrac85$$ and $$M_{xy} = \tfrac{58}{15}$$, so
>
> $$(\bar x, \bar y, \bar z) = \left(\frac{14}{15},\ \frac{8}{15},\ \frac{58}{45}\right) \approx (0.933,\ 0.533,\ 1.289).$$
>
> For comparison, with constant density the centroid is $$\left(\tfrac45, \tfrac35, \tfrac9{10}\right)$$; the density $$\rho = z$$ pulls the center of mass up and toward the tall end. For the second moment, $$I_z = \iiint_S (x^2 + y^2)\,z\,dV = \tfrac{212}{45}$$, so $$r_z = \sqrt{I_z/M} = \sqrt{212/135} \approx 1.253$$.

### Practice & reflection

1. In what way is a triple integral a natural extension of single and double integrals? What new difficulty appears in three dimensions that was not there in two?
2. For a solid that is not a box, why can the order of integration change how hard the setup is? What do you look at in the sketch to choose?
3. If $$\rho(x,y,z)$$ is the temperature at each point of a solid $$S$$, what does $$\iiint_S \rho\,dV$$ mean physically? What about $$\dfrac{1}{\text{Volume}(S)}\iiint_S \rho\,dV$$?
4. What is the difference between the centroid and the center of mass of a solid? When are they the same point?
5. Explain in your own words why $$I_x$$ uses $$y^2 + z^2$$. What would the formula for the moment of inertia about the line $$y = 3,\ z = 0$$ (parallel to the $$x$$-axis) look like?
