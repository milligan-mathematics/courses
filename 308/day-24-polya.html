How these work. Each walk-through takes one exercise through Pólya’s four phases. You answer one step at a time and get feedback right away; when something isn’t right, read why and try again. Hints are there when you ask for them. Your place is saved in this browser, so you can stop and come back.

The walk-throughs end in full solutions, so the work you present in class should still be your own, in your own words.

Pólya walk-through · Exercise 12.4.2

Orthogonal matrices form a group

Exercise 12.4.2. Show that \(O(n)\) is a group.

Goal: Decide which group properties really need proving, and prove them with the one rule \(A^{-1} = A^{\mathsf T}\). It uses the definition of an orthogonal matrix from Section 12.1 and the subgroup ideas of Chapter 3: \(O(n)\) sits inside \(GL_n(\mathbb R)\). Exercises 3 and 10 both use the group you build here.

Pólya walk-through · Exercise 12.4.3

Four matrices, orthogonal and with determinant one

Exercise 12.4.3. Prove that the following matrices are orthogonal. Are any of these matrices in \(SO(n)\)?
(a) \(\begin{pmatrix} 1/\sqrt{2} & -1/\sqrt{2} \\ 1/\sqrt{2} & 1/\sqrt{2} \end{pmatrix}\)
(b) \(\begin{pmatrix} 1/\sqrt{5} & 2/\sqrt{5} \\ -2/\sqrt{5} & 1/\sqrt{5} \end{pmatrix}\)
(c) \(\begin{pmatrix} 4/5 & 0 & 3/5 \\ -3/5 & 0 & 4/5 \\ 0 & -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\)
(d) \(\begin{pmatrix} 1/3 & 2/3 & -2/3 \\ -2/3 & 2/3 & 1/3 \\ 2/3 & 1/3 & 2/3 \end{pmatrix}\)

Goal: Turn “orthogonal” into a test you can run on any matrix, run it on four, and then sort them by determinant. It uses the orthonormal-columns characterization (Theorem 12.1.8) and the determinant facts from Section 12.1. The group \(O(n)\) from Exercise 2 is where these matrices live; Exercise 10 is about the part with determinant \(1\).

Pólya walk-through · Exercise 12.4.6

The Euclidean group

Exercise 12.4.6. Verify that \[E(n) = \{(A, \mathbf x) : A \in O(n) \text{ and } \mathbf x \in \mathbb R^n\}\] is a group, with the product \((A, \mathbf x)(B, \mathbf y) = (AB,\ A\mathbf y + \mathbf x)\).

Goal: Check the group axioms for a product you have not seen before, noticing which ones need real work. It uses \(O(n)\) as a group from Exercise 2 and the axioms from Section 3.2. The matrix part of a pair is an orthogonal matrix and the vector part is a slide, which is how \(E(n)\) holds both kinds of isometry.

Pólya walk-through · Exercise 12.4.10

Determinant one is a normal subgroup

Exercise 12.4.10. Prove that \(SO(n)\) is a normal subgroup of \(O(n)\).

Goal: Use the determinant, once as a homomorphism and once directly, to see why conjugating a determinant-one matrix cannot change its determinant. It uses the facts about \(O(n)\) from Section 12.1 and Exercise 2, and the kernel and normality ideas of Chapters 10 and 11 (Theorem 11.1.5). The quotient it produces is the first one you can see geometrically: turns against flips.
