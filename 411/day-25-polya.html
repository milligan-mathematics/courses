How these work. Each walk-through takes one problem through Pólya’s four phases. You answer one step at a time and get feedback right away; when something isn’t right, read why and try again. Hints are there when you ask for them. Your place is saved in this browser, so you can stop and come back.

Today the sequence is the tool. Theorem 8.2.1 turns a question about a function at a point into a question about sequences. Most steps ask you to choose a sequence, to say what it does to the function, or to name the theorem that justifies a step. Some steps ask you to pick the right formula for a sequence from a list.

One fact runs through several steps: a sequence can’t converge to two different numbers. If it could, take \(\varepsilon\) to be half the distance between them; no term can be within \(\varepsilon\) of both. So if \(f(x_n) \to L\) and \(L \neq f(a)\), then \(f(x_n)\) does not converge to \(f(a)\).

A few steps ask for a number, which you type in plain form, such as 1/2 or 3/4. The walk-throughs end in full proofs, so the proof you bring to class should still be your own, in your own words.

Pólya walk-through · Problem 8.2.3

Two sides, one value

Problem 8.2.3. Use Theorem 8.2.1 to show that
\(f(x)=\begin{cases}\frac{|x|}{x}, & \text{ if } x\neq 0\\ a, & \text{ if } x=0\end{cases}\)
is not continuous at \(0\), no matter what value \(a\) is.

Goal: Show that \(f\) is not continuous at \(0\), whatever \(a\) is. Think about what one sequence can show when \(a\) is arbitrary. Example 8.2.2 in the reading uses the single sequence \(\frac1n\) for the case \(f(0) = 0\). Here \(f(0) = a\) can be any number, and that changes what one sequence can show.

Pólya walk-through · Problem 8.2.4

Rational points and irrational points

Problem 8.2.4. Use Theorem 8.2.1 to show that
\(D(x)=\begin{cases}x, & \text{ if } x \text{ is rational}\\ 0, & \text{ if } x \text{ is irrational}\end{cases}\)
is not continuous at \(a\), for every \(a \neq 0\).

Goal: Show that \(D\) is not continuous at each \(a \neq 0\). The rational and irrational values of \(a\) need different sequences, and both cases have to be covered. Problem 8.1.20 on Day 24 did this with the \(\varepsilon\)–\(\delta\) definition, by showing that no \(\delta\) works for \(\varepsilon = |a|\). The sequence proof does not need to search for a \(\delta\).

Pólya walk-through · Problem 8.2.5

The topologist’s sine curve

Problem 8.2.5. The function \(T(x)=\sin\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)\) for \(x \neq 0\), with \(T(0) = b\), is often called the topologist’s sine curve. Use Theorem 8.2.1 to show that \(T\) is not continuous at \(0\), no matter what value is chosen for \(b\).

Goal: Show that \(T\) is not continuous at \(0\), for every \(b\). The sequences have to be built: find the points where \(\sin\) takes the values you need, near \(0\). It uses the same idea as Problem 8.2.3, two sequences with different limits. Here the sequences come from the sine curve itself, so you have to build them.

Pólya walk-through · Problem 8.2.6

Both directions of the bridge

Problem 8.2.6. Turn the ideas of the previous two paragraphs into a formal proof of Theorem 8.2.1: \(f\) is continuous at \(a\) if and only if, for every sequence \((x_n)\), if \(\lim_{n\to\infty} x_n = a\) then \(\lim_{n\to\infty} f(x_n) = f(a)\).

Goal: Write both directions of Theorem 8.2.1. The forward direction is a chain of definitions. The converse goes by contrapositive, and its hard part is writing the negation of continuity. Every use of Theorem 8.2.1 on this day rests on this result. Its converse is why one failing sequence is enough to disprove continuity.

Pólya walk-through · Problem 8.2.8

A product of limits

Problem 8.2.8. Use Theorem 8.2.1 to show that if \(f\) and \(g\) are continuous at \(a\), then \(f \cdot g\) is continuous at \(a\).

Goal: Turn continuity of the product into a statement about sequences, and find the Chapter 6 theorem that finishes the job. Theorem 8.2.7 states this result, and the reading says the product case is “similar.” Here you write that case out, using Theorem 8.2.1 and the product theorem for sequences.

Pólya walk-through · Problem 8.2.10

Staying away from zero

Problem 8.2.10. Prove Lemma 8.2.9: if \(g\) is continuous at \(a\) and \(g(a) \neq 0\), then there exists \(\delta > 0\) such that \(g(x) \neq 0\) for all \(x \in (a - \delta, a + \delta)\).

Goal: Prove a lemma that clears a neighborhood of zeros. The key is to choose \(\varepsilon\) from \(g(a)\) itself, and then use the reverse triangle inequality. This lemma makes the quotient rule usable. The reading uses it to keep \(g(x_n) \neq 0\) for large \(n\) before it applies Theorem 6.2.12 to \(\frac{f}{g}\). The reverse triangle inequality is Lemma 6.2.2 from Day 19.
