# §11.4: The Cross Product

The dot product handed us back a single number — useful, but a little flat. It could tell us *how aligned* two vectors are, but it couldn't hand us a new direction to work with. The **cross product** fixes that. Feed it two vectors in $$\mathbb{R}^3$$, and it hands back a brand-new *vector* — one that stands at a perfect right angle to both of the vectors you started with.

Why would we want that? Because "give me a direction perpendicular to these two vectors" turns out to be exactly the question we ask constantly in three dimensions: What's normal to this plane? What's the area of this tilted parallelogram? Which way does this wrench twist the bolt? The cross product answers all of these at once, which is why it's worth getting comfortable with.

By the end of this section you should be able to:

1. Compute $$\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}$$ using the determinant formula.
2. Explain what the magnitude and direction of $$\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}$$ mean geometrically.
3. Use a cross product to find a vector normal to a plane, and to compute areas and volumes.
4. Recognize the triple scalar product $$\mathbf{u} \cdot (\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{w})$$ and what it measures.

### The geometric picture

Take two nonzero, nonparallel vectors $$\mathbf{u}$$ and $$\mathbf{v}$$ in $$\mathbb{R}^3$$. Their cross product $$\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}$$ is defined by two properties:

- **Magnitude.** $$\lVert \mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v} \rVert = \lVert \mathbf{u} \rVert \, \lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert \sin\theta$$, where $$\theta$$ is the angle between them. Geometrically, this number is the **area of the parallelogram** that $$\mathbf{u}$$ and $$\mathbf{v}$$ sweep out when placed tail-to-tail. That's a strange thing for a "product" to compute, but it makes sense once you picture it: two parallel vectors span a squashed-flat parallelogram with zero area, and $$\sin\theta = 0$$ exactly when $$\theta$$ is $$0$$ or $$\pi$$. Zero in, zero out.
- **Direction.** $$\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}$$ is perpendicular to *both* $$\mathbf{u}$$ and $$\mathbf{v}$$ — it points straight out of the plane they define. Which of the two perpendicular directions? Whichever one your right thumb points to when you curl the fingers of your right hand from $$\mathbf{u}$$ toward $$\mathbf{v}$$. This is the **right-hand rule**, and it's the reason order matters: curling from $$\mathbf{v}$$ toward $$\mathbf{u}$$ instead sends your thumb the opposite way, so

$$\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v} = -(\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{u})$$

The cross product is *anticommutative* rather than commutative — swap the order and you flip the sign. That single fact trips people up more than anything else in this section, so it's worth tattooing on your brain now.

One more consequence worth noting up front: the cross product only lives in $$\mathbb{R}^3$$ (well, and trivially in $$\mathbb{R}^7$$, but let's not go there). There's no 2D cross product in the same sense — though, as we'll see, 2D vectors can borrow the idea by tucking a $$0$$ into a third coordinate.

### Computing it: the determinant formula

The geometric definition tells you *what* $$\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}$$ means, but it's not how you'd actually calculate it from components. For that, we package $$\mathbf{u} = \langle u_1, u_2, u_3 \rangle$$ and $$\mathbf{v} = \langle v_1, v_2, v_3 \rangle$$ into a symbolic $$3\times 3$$ determinant:

$$\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v} = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k} \\ u_1 & u_2 & u_3 \\ v_1 & v_2 & v_3 \end{vmatrix} = \langle u_2 v_3 - u_3 v_2,\ u_3 v_1 - u_1 v_3,\ u_1 v_2 - u_2 v_1 \rangle$$

That formula falls straight out of expanding $$\mathbf{u} = u_1\mathbf{i} + u_2\mathbf{j} + u_3\mathbf{k}$$ and $$\mathbf{v} = v_1\mathbf{i} + v_2\mathbf{j} + v_3\mathbf{k}$$, distributing, and using the handful of cross products among the standard basis vectors: $$\mathbf{i}\times\mathbf{j} = \mathbf{k}$$, $$\mathbf{j}\times\mathbf{k} = \mathbf{i}$$, $$\mathbf{k}\times\mathbf{i}=\mathbf{j}$$ (cycle forward and you get a plus sign; cycle backward and you get a minus sign; cross anything with itself and you get $$\mathbf{0}$$). You don't need to memorize that derivation — just the determinant setup, and enough practice expanding $$2\times 2$$ minors to trust yourself.

A quick set of properties worth having on hand, all provable directly from the definition above:

$$\mathbf{u} \times (\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{w}) = (\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}) + (\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{w}), \qquad \mathbf{0} \times \mathbf{u} = \mathbf{0}, \qquad \mathbf{u} \cdot (\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{w}) = (\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}) \cdot \mathbf{w}$$

That last one — the **triple scalar product** — deserves its own spotlight in a moment.

> **Worked example.** Find $$\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}$$ for $$\mathbf{u} = \langle -2, 5, 1 \rangle$$ and $$\mathbf{v} = \langle 1, 3, -1 \rangle$$.
>
> $$\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v} = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k} \\ -2 & 5 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 & -1 \end{vmatrix} = \mathbf{i}\begin{vmatrix} 5 & 1 \\ 3 & -1\end{vmatrix} - \mathbf{j}\begin{vmatrix} -2 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{vmatrix} + \mathbf{k}\begin{vmatrix} -2 & 5 \\ 1 & 3 \end{vmatrix}$$
>
> Each $$2\times 2$$ determinant is just "top-left times bottom-right, minus top-right times bottom-left": $$-8$$, $$1$$, and $$-11$$. So
>
> $$\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v} = -8\mathbf{i} - \mathbf{j} - 11\mathbf{k} = \langle -8, -1, -11 \rangle$$
>
> As a sanity check, this vector really is perpendicular to both inputs: $$\mathbf{u} \cdot (\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}) = 0$$ and $$\mathbf{v} \cdot (\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}) = 0$$. Try the demo below with these same vectors and watch the geometry match the algebra.

### Finding a vector normal to a plane

Here's the single most useful trick the cross product gives you: if you have *any* two nonparallel vectors that lie in a plane, their cross product is normal to that plane. So if you're handed three points instead of two vectors, just build two vectors that lie in the plane by subtracting one point from the others.

> **Worked example.** Find a vector normal to the plane through $$P(-2,1,3)$$, $$Q(4,-3,1)$$, and $$R(5,5,3)$$.
>
> Build two vectors that live in the plane using $$P$$ as a common tail:
>
> $$\vec{PQ} = \langle 6, -4, -2 \rangle, \qquad \vec{PR} = \langle 7, 4, 0 \rangle$$
>
> Their cross product is normal to both — and therefore normal to the whole plane:
>
> $$\mathbf{n} = \vec{PQ} \times \vec{PR} = \langle 8, -14, 52 \rangle$$
>
> That's it. This exact move — differences of points, then cross them — is how you'll find the equation of a plane through three points, since a plane is fully determined by one point on it plus a normal vector.

### Area, volume, and the triple scalar product

Once you have the cross product, three classic geometric quantities fall out almost for free:

- **Area of a parallelogram** spanned by $$\mathbf{u}$$ and $$\mathbf{v}$$: just $$\lVert \mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v} \rVert$$. (Got 2D vectors? Tuck a third coordinate of $$0$$ onto each one and proceed exactly the same way — you'll notice only the $$\mathbf{k}$$-component survives, and it matches the familiar $$2\times 2$$ determinant $$u_1v_2 - u_2v_1$$ you may have seen before.)
- **Area of a triangle** with two sides $$\mathbf{u}$$ and $$\mathbf{v}$$: half of the parallelogram, so $$\tfrac{1}{2}\lVert \mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v} \rVert$$. A triangle is, after all, exactly half a parallelogram cut along the diagonal.
- **Volume of a parallelepiped** (the 3D "slanted box" spanned by three vectors $$\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w}$$): the absolute value of the **triple scalar product**,

$$\mathbf{u} \cdot (\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{w}) = \begin{vmatrix} u_1 & u_2 & u_3 \\ v_1 & v_2 & v_3 \\ w_1 & w_2 & w_3 \end{vmatrix}$$

The intuition: $$\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{w}$$ already gives the area of the base parallelogram, pointing straight up out of it. Dotting with $$\mathbf{u}$$ then measures how much of $$\mathbf{u}$$ points along that "straight up" direction — exactly the height you'd need to turn a base area into a volume. And if that triple product comes out to $$0$$? Then $$\mathbf{u}$$ has no component sticking out of the base plane at all — meaning all three vectors are **coplanar**.

There's also a genuine physics payoff here: **torque**, $$\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F}$$, where $$\mathbf{r}$$ is the position vector from a pivot to where a force $$\mathbf{F}$$ is applied. Its magnitude, $$\lVert \mathbf{r} \rVert \lVert \mathbf{F} \rVert \sin\theta$$, tells you how hard something is twisting; its direction (right-hand rule, again) tells you the axis it twists around. Same formula, brand-new context — that's the cross product's whole personality.

### Practice & reflection

1. Why must $$\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{u} = \mathbf{0}$$ for every vector $$\mathbf{u}$$? Explain it both from the geometric definition (area/angle) and from the determinant formula (what happens when two rows of a determinant are identical).
2. If $$\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b} = \mathbf{c}$$, what is $$(\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b}) \cdot \mathbf{a}$$? You shouldn't need to compute anything — think about what direction $$\mathbf{c}$$ must point.
3. Three vectors have a triple scalar product of $$0$$. Does that mean one of the three vectors is $$\mathbf{0}$$? If not, what *does* it guarantee?
4. A robotic arm extends along $$\mathbf{r} = \langle 1, 2, 0 \rangle$$ meters and a force $$\mathbf{F} = \langle 0, 5, 0 \rangle$$ newtons is applied at its tip. Find the torque vector $$\mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F}$$ and describe, in words, the axis it rotates the arm around.
