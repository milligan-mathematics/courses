Paper first, then check in. Give yourself 15 minutes for the five problems on paper, no calculator, the way the real test runs. Then open each card. If you already have an answer, press Check my answer from paper: one try. Right, and you can skip to Look back for a faster route or a variation. Not right, or not sure, and the walk-through takes it one step at a time: Understand, Plan, Carry out, Look back.

Every wrong choice has a story. The test writers build the wrong answers out of the slips people actually make. When you pick one here, the page tells you which slip leads there, so you can spot it next time.

Your place is saved in this browser, so you can stop and come back. Bring the one card that bugged you most to our check-in.

Walk-through · Set B11, Problem 1

What one integral tells you

1. Let \(f\) be continuous on \([0,4]\) with \(\displaystyle\int_0^4 f(x)\,dx=10\). Which of the following must be true?
  I. \(f(c)=\frac{5}{2}\) for some \(c\) in \([0,4]\).
  II. \(\displaystyle\int_0^2 f(2x)\,dx=5\)
  III. \(\displaystyle\int_0^4\big(f(x)+1\big)\,dx=11\)
(A) I only
(B) II only
(C) I and II only
(D) II and III only
(E) I, II, and III

Goal: Decide each statement with a theorem, a substitution, or a counterexample. The average value, \(u\)-substitution with changed limits, and linearity of the integral show up all over the calculus third of the MFT, often bundled as I/II/III items like this one.

Walk-through · Set B11, Problem 2

Counting elements of order 2

2. How many elements of order \(2\) are there in the symmetric group \(S_4\)?
(A) \(3\)
(B) \(4\)
(C) \(6\)
(D) \(9\)
(E) \(12\)

Goal: Sort the permutations of \(S_4\) by cycle type and count the ones of order \(2\). Cycle types turn questions about orders in \(S_n\) into counting. The MFT likes them because they mix group theory with combinatorics.

Walk-through · Set B11, Problem 3

Finding the imaginary part

3. The function \(f(z)=u(x,y)+i\,v(x,y)\) is analytic on the whole complex plane, and \(u(x,y)=x^2-y^2+3x\). Which of the following could be \(v(x,y)\)?
(A) \(2xy+3y\)
(B) \(-2xy+3y\)
(C) \(2xy-3x\)
(D) \(x^2-y^2+3y\)
(E) \(2xy+3x\)

Goal: Use the Cauchy–Riemann equations to rebuild \(v\) from \(u\). The Cauchy–Riemann equations are the most-tested fact in the MFT’s complex variables questions: checking analyticity, finding a harmonic conjugate, or spotting which function can’t be analytic.

Walk-through · Set B11, Problem 4

At least one of each

4. A committee of \(4\) students is to be chosen from \(6\) mathematics majors and \(5\) physics majors. The committee must include at least one major of each kind. How many different committees are possible?
(A) \(150\)
(B) \(310\)
(C) \(315\)
(D) \(330\)
(E) \(1080\)

Goal: Count the committees by counting the ones that break the rule and subtracting. “At least one” almost always means count the complement. The overcount from “pick one of each, then fill in” is one of the most common wrong answers the MFT builds.

Walk-through · Set B11, Problem 5

A sum that turns into an integral

5. \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{n}{n^2+k^2}=\)
(A) \(0\)
(B) \(\frac{1}{4}\)
(C) \(\frac{1}{2}\)
(D) \(\ln 2\)
(E) \(\frac{\pi}{4}\)

Goal: Recognize the sum as a Riemann sum and evaluate the integral it approaches. Limits of sums with a \(\frac1n\) hiding inside are Riemann sums in disguise. The MFT uses them to test whether you see the definite integral behind the sum.
