How these work. Each walk-through takes one problem through Pólya’s four phases. You answer one step at a time and get feedback right away; when something isn’t right, read why and try again. Hints are there when you ask for them. Your place is saved in this browser, so you can stop and come back.

Something new: you name the term that escapes. To show a sequence does not converge to \(L\), you pick one \(\varepsilon\), and then your opponent names any \(N\) they like. Some steps ask you to answer with a formula for \(n\) in terms of their \(N\) (type a capital N). The page then plays the opponent: it names \(N = 1, 2, 3, \ldots\) up to a billion and checks that your \(n\) is a whole number past \(N\) whose term is at least \(\varepsilon\) away from \(L\). Any formula that works is right. And Problem 6.1.13 brings back the Day 17 steps where you name \(N\) and the page throws challenges at it.

The walk-throughs end in a full proof, so the proof you bring to class should still be your own, in your own words.

Pólya walk-through · Problem 6.1.9

Turn the crank on the definition

Problem 6.1.9. Negate the definition of \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty} s_n = 0\) to provide a formal definition for \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty} s_n \ne 0\).

Goal: Negate a statement with three nested quantifiers and an implication, step by step, and read the result as a plan for a proof. Problems 6.1.10 and 6.1.14 use the definition you write here, and Rule 5 on the Day 18 page reads it as moves in a game.

Pólya walk-through · Problem 6.1.10

Pick your \(\varepsilon\), then beat every \(N\)

Problem 6.1.10. Use the definition to prove \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n}{n+100} \ne 0\).

Goal: Prove a sequence does not converge to a number: choose one \(\varepsilon\), then, for every \(N\), name a term past \(N\) that stays at least \(\varepsilon\) away. It uses the definition from 6.1.9, and the reading’s Example 6.1.12 then proves these same terms converge to \(1\). Problem 6.1.14 runs the same plan on \((-1)^n\).

Pólya walk-through · Problem 6.1.13

Logarithms find \(N\); cases keep it honest

Problem 6.1.13. Let \(b > 0\). Use the definition to prove \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty} b^{\left(\frac1n\right)} = 1\).

Goal: Solve \(|s_n - L| < \varepsilon\) for \(n\) with logarithms, splitting into cases where the absolute value opens differently, and keep track of which way each inequality points. It uses Definition 6.1.11 (a limit other than \(0\)) and the logarithm move from Day 17’s 6.1.7, where dividing by a negative logarithm flipped an inequality. The same flip is waiting here.

Pólya walk-through · Problem 6.1.14

No number is close to both \(1\) and \(-1\)

Problem 6.1.14. Provide a rigorous definition for \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty} s_n \ne s\). Use your definition to show that for any real number \(a\), \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\big((-1)^n\big) \ne a\). Choose \(\varepsilon = 1\) and use the fact that \(\big|a - (-1)^n\big| < 1\) is equivalent to \((-1)^n - 1 < a < (-1)^n + 1\) to show that no choice of \(N\) will work for this \(\varepsilon\).

Goal: Prove a sequence converges to no number at all: one \(\varepsilon\) that defeats every candidate limit \(a\), with cases on \(a\) deciding which term escapes. It generalizes 6.1.9 from \(0\) to any \(s\), and on Day 21 you’ll call such a sequence divergent (Problem 6.3.8 starts with this very sequence).
