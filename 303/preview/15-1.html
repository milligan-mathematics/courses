§15.1: Vector Fields

Until now a function of several variables handed you a number at each point: a temperature, a height, a density. Plenty of things in engineering are not numbers. Water in a channel has a speed and a direction at every point. Start there.

A straight channel 2 m wide, banks at \(y=\pm1\). The water's velocity at height \(y\) is \(\mathbf{v}(x,y)=\langle 1-y^2,\ 0\rangle\) m/s: fastest in the middle, still at the banks. Tap the water to drop a boat.
Predict, then check

Both boats start 8 m above the bridge. Boat A rides the middle (\(y=0\)); boat B starts near the bank at \(y=0.8\). How many times as long does B take?

The rule that hands you an arrow at every point is a vector field. The toggle above shows the difference that matters: speed \(\lVert\mathbf v\rVert\) is a scalar field (one number per point, drawn as shading), while velocity \(\mathbf v\) is a vector field (an arrow per point). Wind, gravity, electric force and fluid flow are all vector fields, and they are the setting for the rest of the course: next we add a field up along a curve (§15.2) and ask when the answer depends only on the endpoints (§15.3).

By the end of this section you should be able to:
  1. Evaluate a vector field at a point and tell a vector field from a scalar field.
  2. Describe a field from its formula (direction pattern, how the length changes) and sketch it.
  3. Write the gradient field \(\nabla f\) of a scalar function, and say what conservative means.
  4. Use the component test \(\partial P/\partial y = \partial Q/\partial x\) to rule out conservative fields.

Reading a vector field

A vector field in the plane assigns to each point \((x,y)\) of a region a vector

\[\mathbf{F}(x,y) = \langle P(x,y),\,Q(x,y)\rangle = P\,\mathbf{i} + Q\,\mathbf{j},\]

where \(P\) and \(Q\) are ordinary scalar functions. In space, \(\mathbf{F}=\langle P,Q,R\rangle\). To sketch one, pick a grid of points, compute \(\mathbf F\) at each, and draw the arrow with its tail at that point.

Quick check

Which way does \(\mathbf F(x,y)=\langle -y,\ x\rangle\) point at \((0,2)\)?

Quick check

How long is the arrow of \(\langle -y,\ x\rangle\) at \((1,2)\)?

Worked example: describe \(\mathbf F(x,y)=\langle -y,\ x\rangle\)

At \((1,0)\): \(\langle 0,1\rangle\), up. At \((0,1)\): \(\langle -1,0\rangle\), left. At \((-1,0)\): \(\langle 0,-1\rangle\), down. At \((0,-1)\): \(\langle 1,0\rangle\), right. The diagonals: \(\mathbf F(1,1)=\langle -1,1\rangle\), \(\mathbf F(-1,1)=\langle -1,-1\rangle\), \(\mathbf F(-1,-1)=\langle 1,-1\rangle\), \(\mathbf F(1,-1)=\langle 1,1\rangle\).

Length: \(\lVert\mathbf F\rVert=\sqrt{y^2+x^2}\), the distance from the origin, so arrows grow as you move out. Direction: \(\mathbf F\cdot\langle x,y\rangle=-yx+xy=0\), so every arrow is tangent to a circle about the origin. The pattern is a counterclockwise swirl.

Worked example: describe \(\mathbf F(x,y)=\dfrac{\langle x,y\rangle}{2\lVert\mathbf r\rVert}\), where \(\mathbf r=\langle x,y\rangle\)

Dividing by a positive number keeps the direction, so every arrow points straight away from the origin. The length is \(\dfrac{\lVert\mathbf r\rVert}{2\lVert\mathbf r\rVert}=\tfrac12\) at every point. At \((1,0)\) the arrow is \(\langle\tfrac12,0\rangle\); at \((1,1)\) it is \(\langle\tfrac1{2\sqrt2},\tfrac1{2\sqrt2}\rangle\). A steady outflow from the origin, where the field is not defined. (Choose Steady outflow in the lab: the length readout never changes.)

In space the idea is the same, only harder to draw. The constant field \(\mathbf F(x,y,z)=\langle 0,0,1\rangle\) is a unit arrow pointing in the \(+z\) direction at every point: a uniform upward push, like the lift on a level wing in still air.

Gravity and electric fields

Newton: masses \(M\) and \(m\) at distance \(r\) attract with force \(GMm/r^2\), directed toward each other. Put \(M\) at the origin and \(m\) at \(\mathbf r=\langle x,y,z\rangle\). The unit vector toward the origin is \(-\mathbf r/r\), so

\[\mathbf F(x,y,z)=-\frac{GMm}{r^2}\cdot\frac{\mathbf r}{r}=-\frac{GMm}{r^3}\,\mathbf r=-\frac{GMm}{(x^2+y^2+z^2)^{3/2}}\langle x,y,z\rangle.\]

Coulomb's law has the same shape for a charge \(Q\) at the origin acting on a charge \(q\) (\(\varepsilon\) is the constant in your notes):

\[\mathbf F(x,y,z)=\frac{\varepsilon\,Qq}{r^3}\,\mathbf r.\]

The sign of \(Qq\) decides the direction: like charges (\(Qq>0\)) push the arrows outward, opposite charges pull them in. Both are inverse-square fields.

Gradient fields and conservative fields

Any scalar function \(f\) gives a vector field, its gradient \(\nabla f=\langle f_x,f_y\rangle\) (or \(\langle f_x,f_y,f_z\rangle\)). It points in the direction of steepest increase of \(f\), and its length is that rate of increase. Below, the green curves are level curves of \(f(x,y)=x^2+x\cos y\) and the arrows are \(\nabla f\). Release a cloud: every particle climbs.

Look at where the arrows cross the green curves: always at right angles, because moving along a level curve does not change \(f\).

A field \(\mathbf F\) is conservative if \(\mathbf F=\nabla f\) for some scalar function \(f\), called a potential function for \(\mathbf F\). Conservative fields matter because, as §15.3 shows, the work they do depends only on where you start and finish.

A test that rules fields out

If \(\mathbf F=\langle P,Q\rangle=\nabla f\), then \(P=f_x\) and \(Q=f_y\), and Clairaut's theorem says

\[\frac{\partial P}{\partial y}=f_{xy}=f_{yx}=\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x}.\]

In space the same argument gives \(P_y=Q_x\), \(P_z=R_x\), \(Q_z=R_y\). This is the component test: if any of these fails at some point, \(\mathbf F\) is not conservative. (Passing is not quite the whole story; §15.3 says exactly when it is enough.)

A picture of the test. Drop a tiny paddle wheel into a flowing fluid. It spins when the flow pushes harder on one side than the other, and its spin rate is \(\tfrac12\big(\partial Q/\partial x-\partial P/\partial y\big)\). (This quantity returns later in the course as curl.) So the component test says: in a gradient field, paddle wheels don't spin. In the second lab, switch between \(\nabla f\) and the swirl and watch the wheel on the probe.

Practice & reflection

  1. Explain the difference between a scalar field and a vector field, with one example of each from engineering or physics.
    The river: water speed at each point is a scalar field, water velocity is a vector field. Likewise air temperature (scalar) and wind (vector).
  2. The fields \(\langle x,y\rangle\) and \(\langle x,y\rangle/\lVert\langle x,y\rangle\rVert\) both point away from the origin. How do their arrows differ? What would each look like along the \(x\)-axis?
    The first has length equal to the distance from the origin, so arrows grow as you move out (\(\langle 3,0\rangle\) at \((3,0)\)). The second has length 1 everywhere (\(\langle 1,0\rangle\) at \((3,0)\)) and is undefined at the origin.
  3. Gravity pulls with strength \(GMm/r^2\). Moving from distance \(r\) to \(3r\), by what factor does the force change, and do the arrows still point at the same place?
    It becomes \(\tfrac19\) as strong, and it still points at the center.
  4. Suppose \(\nabla f(a,b)=\langle 0,0\rangle\). What does the arrow there look like, and what might that say about the surface \(z=f(x,y)\) above \((a,b)\)?
    No arrow at all. The tangent plane is horizontal there: a critical point (a max, a min, or a saddle, as in §13.7).
  5. Your friend says the component test passes for the swirl \(\langle -y,x\rangle\) because "the two minus signs cancel." Where exactly did the algebra go wrong?
    There is only one minus sign. \(P=-y\) gives \(P_y=-1\); \(Q=x\) gives \(Q_x=+1\). Nothing cancels, and the test fails.
