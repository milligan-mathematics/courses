# §11.3: The Dot Product

So far, adding and scaling vectors has felt a lot like ordinary arithmetic. Now we ask a new question: what happens when we "multiply" two vectors together? There are two useful answers to that question in this course, and this section covers the first one: the **dot product**, which takes two vectors and hands back a single number. Don't let that simplicity fool you — this one number secretly encodes the angle between the vectors, tells you instantly whether they're perpendicular, and lets you split any vector into "parallel" and "leftover" pieces relative to another vector. That's a lot of geometry packed into one arithmetic rule.

By the end of this section you should be able to:

1. Compute the dot product of two vectors in $$\mathbb{R}^2$$ or $$\mathbb{R}^3$$.
2. Use the dot product to find the angle between two vectors, and recognize orthogonal and parallel vectors.
3. Compute the scalar and vector projection of one vector onto another.
4. Apply the dot product to a physical problem involving work.

### The definition (it's just multiply-and-add)

Given $$\mathbf{u} = \langle u_1, u_2, u_3 \rangle$$ and $$\mathbf{v} = \langle v_1, v_2, v_3 \rangle$$, the **dot product** is

$$\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = u_1v_1 + u_2v_2 + u_3v_3$$

(In $$\mathbb{R}^2$$, just drop the last term.) Multiply matching components and add them up — that's the entire computation. The result is a plain number, not a vector, which is why this is also called the *scalar product*.

> **Example.** $$\langle 3, -1, 4 \rangle \cdot \langle 2, 5, -2 \rangle = (3)(2) + (-1)(5) + (4)(-2) = 6 - 5 - 8 = -7$$

One special case is worth flagging right away: dot a vector with itself, and

$$\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{u} = u_1^2 + u_2^2 + u_3^2 = \lVert \mathbf{u} \rVert^2$$

which is exactly the squared length of $$\mathbf{u}$$. Keep that identity handy — it shows up constantly.

The dot product also behaves the way you'd hope: it's commutative ($$\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{u}$$), it distributes over vector addition ($$\mathbf{u}\cdot(\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{w}) = \mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{w}$$), and scalars slide in and out freely ($$(c\mathbf{u})\cdot\mathbf{v} = c(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v})$$). Nothing exotic yet — the geometry is where this gets interesting.

### Where the geometry comes from

Here's the payoff. If $$\theta$$ is the angle between two nonzero vectors $$\mathbf{u}$$ and $$\mathbf{v}$$ (placed tail-to-tail, so $$0 \le \theta \le \pi$$), then

$$\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = \lVert \mathbf{u} \rVert \, \lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert \cos\theta$$

You can prove this with the Law of Cosines applied to the triangle formed by $$\mathbf{u}$$, $$\mathbf{v}$$, and $$\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}$$ — a tidy few lines of algebra if you want to work it out yourself. But the *idea* matters more than the derivation: **the dot product measures how much two vectors point the same way.** Rearranging gives an honest angle-finding formula,

$$\cos\theta = \frac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{\lVert \mathbf{u} \rVert \, \lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert}$$

and since $$\cos\theta$$ only changes sign at $$\theta = \pi/2$$, the *sign* of the dot product alone already tells you the flavor of angle you're looking at:

| Sign of $$\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}$$ | What it means |
| :--- | :--- |
| positive | angle is acute — vectors lean the same way |
| zero | angle is exactly $$90^\circ$$ — **orthogonal** |
| negative | angle is obtuse — vectors lean opposite ways |

That middle row is the one you'll lean on the most.

#### Orthogonal and parallel vectors

Two nonzero vectors are **orthogonal** exactly when $$\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = 0$$ — no square roots, no inverse cosine, just check whether the dot product vanishes. (By convention, the zero vector is considered orthogonal to everything.) At the other extreme, $$\mathbf{u}$$ and $$\mathbf{v}$$ are **parallel** when one is a scalar multiple of the other; then $$\theta$$ is $$0$$ or $$\pi$$, and $$\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = \pm \lVert\mathbf{u}\rVert\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert$$.

> **Example.** Find the angle between $$\mathbf{u} = \langle -3, 1, 4 \rangle$$ and $$\mathbf{v} = \langle 2, 5, -1 \rangle$$.
>
> First the pieces: $$\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = -6+5-4 = -5$$, $$\lVert\mathbf{u}\rVert = \sqrt{26}$$, $$\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert = \sqrt{30}$$. Then
> $$\cos\theta = \frac{-5}{\sqrt{26}\sqrt{30}} \approx -0.179 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \theta \approx 100.3^\circ$$
> Negative dot product, obtuse angle — exactly what the sign rule predicted.

> **Example.** For which $$k$$ are $$\langle -2, 4, -3 \rangle$$ and $$\langle 1, 2, k \rangle$$ orthogonal? Set the dot product equal to zero: $$-2 + 8 - 3k = 0$$, so $$k = 2$$.

*Aside — direction angles.* The same idea tells you the angle a vector $$\mathbf{u} = \langle u_1,u_2,u_3\rangle$$ makes with each coordinate axis: dotting $$\mathbf{u}$$ with $$\mathbf{i}$$, $$\mathbf{j}$$, or $$\mathbf{k}$$ gives $$\cos\alpha = u_1/\lVert\mathbf{u}\rVert$$, $$\cos\beta = u_2/\lVert\mathbf{u}\rVert$$, $$\cos\gamma = u_3/\lVert\mathbf{u}\rVert$$. These are called the *direction cosines*, and it's a nice exercise to check that $$\cos^2\alpha + \cos^2\beta + \cos^2\gamma = 1$$ always — it falls straight out of $$\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{u} = \lVert\mathbf{u}\rVert^2$$.

### Projections: casting a shadow

Here's a question the dot product answers beautifully: how much of $$\mathbf{u}$$ points in the direction of $$\mathbf{v}$$? Picture shining a light straight down onto $$\mathbf{v}$$, perpendicular to it — the shadow $$\mathbf{u}$$ casts on $$\mathbf{v}$$'s line is its **projection**.

The signed length of that shadow (negative if $$\mathbf{u}$$ leans away from $$\mathbf{v}$$) is the **scalar projection**:

$$\text{comp}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{u} = \frac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert}$$

Multiply that shadow length by the unit vector pointing along $$\mathbf{v}$$, and you get an actual vector — the **vector projection**:

$$\text{proj}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{u} = \frac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert^2}\,\mathbf{v}$$

What makes this genuinely useful is that it splits $$\mathbf{u}$$ into two pieces that never interfere with each other: one parallel to $$\mathbf{v}$$ (the projection itself), and one perpendicular to $$\mathbf{v}$$ (whatever is left over, $$\mathbf{u} - \text{proj}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{u}$$). You can confirm that leftover piece really is perpendicular to $$\mathbf{v}$$ by dotting it with $$\mathbf{v}$$ and watching everything cancel to zero.

> **Example.** Split $$\mathbf{u} = \langle 4, -1, 3 \rangle$$ into a piece parallel to $$\mathbf{v} = \langle -3, 0, 1 \rangle$$ and a piece perpendicular to it.
>
> First, $$\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = -12+0+3 = -9$$ and $$\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert^2 = 10$$, so
> $$\mathbf{u}_{\parallel} = \frac{-9}{10}\langle -3,0,1\rangle = \left\langle \tfrac{27}{10},\, 0,\, -\tfrac{9}{10}\right\rangle$$
> Subtracting from $$\mathbf{u}$$ gives the leftover piece,
> $$\mathbf{u}_{\perp} = \mathbf{u} - \mathbf{u}_{\parallel} = \left\langle \tfrac{13}{10},\, -1,\, \tfrac{39}{10}\right\rangle$$
> and you can check $$\mathbf{u}_{\perp}\cdot\mathbf{v} = 0$$ if you want proof it's really perpendicular.

Building your own vectors and watching this decomposition happen live makes the "shadow" idea click much faster than the formula does on its own — try it in the demo below.

### One application: work

If you push or pull something with a constant force $$\mathbf{F}$$ while it moves along a displacement $$\mathbf{D}$$, physics defines the **work** done as the component of the force *in the direction of motion*, times how far the object moved. That's exactly the scalar projection of $$\mathbf{F}$$ onto $$\mathbf{D}$$, multiplied by $$\lVert\mathbf{D}\rVert$$ — and that combination collapses into one clean formula:

$$W = \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{D}$$

Only the part of the force that actually points along the motion contributes anything; a force applied entirely sideways to the motion (orthogonal to $$\mathbf{D}$$) does zero work, which matches the dot-product-zero intuition exactly.

> **Example.** You pull a sled with $$50$$ N of force directed $$30^\circ$$ above the ground, dragging it $$15$$ m horizontally. Then $$\mathbf{F} = \langle 50\cos30^\circ,\, 50\sin30^\circ\rangle$$ and $$\mathbf{D} = \langle 15,\, 0\rangle$$, so
> $$W = \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{D} = 50\cos(30^\circ)(15) \approx 649.5 \text{ J}$$

### Practice & reflection

1. If $$\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} > 0$$, what can you say about the angle between them? What about $$\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} < 0$$?
2. Describe, in words, the set of all vectors orthogonal to $$\mathbf{j} = \langle 0,1,0\rangle$$. What shape does that set trace out in space?
3. Why must $$\mathbf{u}_{\perp} = \mathbf{u} - \text{proj}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{u}$$ always come out orthogonal to $$\mathbf{v}$$, no matter what $$\mathbf{u}$$ and $$\mathbf{v}$$ you start with? (Try dotting it with $$\mathbf{v}$$ and see what forces the cancellation.)
4. A plane has equation $$4x + 5y + 2z = 12$$. Which vector would you dot with $$\langle x,y,z\rangle$$ to reproduce the left-hand side, and why does that same vector end up perpendicular to the plane?
5. **Challenge.** Let $$P$$ be any point on the unit circle other than $$(\pm 1, 0)$$. Using the vectors from $$P$$ to $$(-1,0)$$ and from $$P$$ to $$(1,0)$$, show their dot product is always zero — meaning every triangle inscribed in a semicircle has a right angle at $$P$$. (This is Thales' Theorem.)
