How these work. Each walk-through takes one exercise through Pólya’s four phases. You answer one step at a time and get feedback right away; when something isn’t right, read why and try again. Hints are there when you ask for them. Your place is saved in this browser, so you can stop and come back.

The walk-throughs end in full solutions, so the work you present in class should still be your own, in your own words.

Pólya walk-through · Exercise 5.4.1

Loops of arrows

Exercise 5.4.1. Write the following permutations in cycle notation.
(a) \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 \\ 2 & 4 & 1 & 5 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\)
(b) \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 \\ 4 & 2 & 5 & 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\)
(c) \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 \\ 3 & 5 & 1 & 4 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\)
(d) \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 \\ 1 & 4 & 3 & 2 & 5 \end{pmatrix}\)

Goal: Read a permutation as arrows, follow each loop of arrows once around, and write it in cycle notation without losing a number. It uses the two-line notation from Section 5.1 and switches to cycle notation, as Remark 5.1.11 says we will from this point on. Every later exercise in Chapter 5 is written this way.

Pólya walk-through · Exercise 5.4.2

Right to left, one factor at a time

Exercise 5.4.2. Compute each of the following.
(a) \((1\,3\,4\,5)(2\,3\,4)\)
(d) \((1\,4\,2\,3)(3\,4)(5\,6)(1\,3\,2\,4)\)
(g) \((1\,2\,5\,4)^{-1}(1\,2\,3)(4\,5)(1\,2\,5\,4)\)
(j) \((1\,2\,5\,4)^{100}\)
(m) \((1\,2)^{-1}\)
(p) \([(1\,2\,3\,5)(4\,6\,7)]^{-1}\)

Goal: Multiply permutations right to left, one factor at a time, and carry inverses and powers through without losing track of a number. It uses the right-to-left product of Remark 5.1.3 and Example 5.1.6, and cycle notation from Remark 5.1.11. The inverse of a permutation comes from the group axioms in Chapter 3.

Pólya walk-through · Exercise 5.4.3

Cycles as chains of swaps

Exercise 5.4.3. Express the following permutations as products of transpositions and identify them as even or odd.
(a) \((1\,4\,3\,5\,6)\)
(b) \((1\,5\,6)(2\,3\,4)\)
(c) \((1\,4\,2\,6)(1\,4\,2)\)
(d) \((1\,7\,2\,5\,4)(1\,4\,2\,3)(1\,5\,4\,6\,3\,2)\)
(e) \((1\,4\,2\,6\,3\,7)\)

Goal: Write a permutation as a product of swaps, and count the swaps: the count decides whether the permutation is even or odd. It uses the formula for a cycle as a product of transpositions in the Transpositions subsection, which leads to Proposition 5.1.12, and the right-to-left product from Exercise 5.4.2. Example 5.1.13 says that a permutation cannot be both an even and an odd product of transpositions. Day 10’s reading proves it.
