§15.2: Line Integrals

In single-variable calculus you integrated over an interval of the \(x\)-axis. But a wire is not straight, and a force does work on a particle that travels along a winding path. We need to integrate along a curve. Start back at the river from §15.1.

Same channel as §15.1: banks at \(y=\pm1\), current \(\mathbf{v}(x,y)=\langle 1-y^2,\ 0\rangle\) m/s. A boat rides a route. At each step the current is split into the part along the route (green if it helps, red if it fights) and the part across it (gray), and the meter adds up (current along the route) × (distance). Pick a route, or drag on the water to draw your own.
Predict, then check

The route Straight across runs from \((3,-0.8)\) to \((3,0.8)\), right through the fastest water. What will the meter read at the far side?

That meter is a line integral, and line integrals come in two flavors that you must keep apart:

  • a scalar line integral \(\displaystyle\int_C f\,ds\) adds up a number (a density, say) along the curve, weighted by arc length. It does not care which way you walk along \(C\);
  • a line integral of a vector field \(\displaystyle\int_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\) adds up how much the field pushes along the direction of travel. It does care, and reversing direction changes its sign. The river meter is this kind.
By the end of this section you should be able to:
  1. Parametrize a line segment, a circle, or a given curve and compute \(\lVert\mathbf{r}'(t)\rVert\).
  2. Evaluate \(\int_C f\,ds\) and use it for mass, moments, and center of mass of a wire.
  3. Evaluate \(\int_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\) and interpret it as work or as flow.
  4. Say what happens to each kind of line integral when the direction of \(C\) is reversed.

Parametrized curves and arc length

A smooth curve \(C\) is described by \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle x(t), y(t), z(t)\rangle\) for \(a \le t \le b\), with \(\mathbf{r}'(t)\) continuous and not zero. A segment from \((x_0,y_0)\) to \((x_1,y_1)\) is \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle x_0 + (x_1-x_0)t,\ y_0 + (y_1-y_0)t\rangle\) for \(0\le t\le1\). A circle of radius \(a\) about the origin is \(\langle a\cos t, a\sin t\rangle\).

A small step \(dt\) in the parameter moves you a distance

\[ds = \lVert\mathbf{r}'(t)\rVert\,dt = \sqrt{x'(t)^2 + y'(t)^2 + z'(t)^2}\,dt.\]

The scalar line integral

\[\int_C f(x,y,z)\,ds = \int_a^b f\bigl(x(t),y(t),z(t)\bigr)\,\lVert\mathbf{r}'(t)\rVert\,dt.\]

Four steps: parametrize \(C\); substitute the parametrization into \(f\); compute \(\lVert\mathbf{r}'\rVert\); integrate in \(t\). The value does not depend on which parametrization you choose, nor on the direction you traverse \(C\).

The helix \(\mathbf r(t)=\langle\cos t,\ \sin t,\ t\rangle\) winds around the unit circle while it climbs. How fast does it move, \(\lVert\mathbf r'(t)\rVert\)?

Worked example: \(\displaystyle\int_C (x+y+z)\,ds\) on the helix \(\langle\cos t,\sin t,t\rangle\), \(0\le t\le\pi\)

Here \(\mathbf{r}' = \langle-\sin t,\cos t,1\rangle\), so \(\lVert\mathbf{r}'\rVert = \sqrt{\sin^2t+\cos^2t+1} = \sqrt2\). Then

\[\int_C (x+y+z)\,ds = \sqrt2\int_0^\pi(\cos t+\sin t+t)\,dt = \sqrt2\Bigl[\sin t-\cos t+\tfrac{t^2}{2}\Bigr]_0^\pi = \sqrt2\Bigl(2+\frac{\pi^2}{2}\Bigr).\]

Mass, moments, and center of mass of a wire

If a thin wire lies along \(C\) and has linear density \(\rho(x,y)\) (mass per unit length), then each piece \(ds\) has mass \(\rho\,ds\), and

\[M = \int_C \rho\,ds,\qquad M_x = \int_C y\,\rho\,ds,\qquad M_y = \int_C x\,\rho\,ds,\qquad \bar x = \frac{M_y}{M},\quad \bar y = \frac{M_x}{M}.\]

(In space, \(M_{yz} = \int_C x\rho\,ds\), \(M_{xz} = \int_C y\rho\,ds\), \(M_{xy} = \int_C z\rho\,ds\) and the center of mass is \((M_{yz}/M,\,M_{xz}/M,\,M_{xy}/M)\).) The second moments are \(I_x = \int_C y^2\rho\,ds\), \(I_y = \int_C x^2\rho\,ds\), and \(I_0 = I_x + I_y\), with radii of gyration \(R = \sqrt{I/M}\).

Predict, then check

A wire is bent into the upper semicircle \(x^2+y^2=a^2\), \(y\ge0\), with density \(\rho = ky\): light at the two ends, heaviest at the top. Where is its balance point?

Worked example: mass and center of mass of the semicircular wire, \(\rho=ky\)

Take \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle a\cos t,a\sin t\rangle\), \(0\le t\le\pi\), so \(ds = a\,dt\) and \(\rho = ka\sin t\). Then

\[M = \int_0^\pi ka\sin t\cdot a\,dt = 2ka^2,\qquad M_x = \int_0^\pi a\sin t\cdot ka\sin t\cdot a\,dt = ka^3\cdot\frac\pi2,\] \[M_y = \int_0^\pi a\cos t\cdot ka\sin t\cdot a\,dt = ka^3\int_0^\pi\sin t\cos t\,dt = 0.\]

So \(\bar x = 0\) and \(\bar y = \dfrac{\pi ka^3/2}{2ka^2} = \dfrac{\pi a}{4}\). The zero is no surprise: the wire and its density are both symmetric left to right, and \(x\) is odd, so the contributions cancel in pairs.

Try Walk it backward in the lab: the mass and the balance point do not move. Lengths are positive whichever way you walk, so \(\int_C f\,ds\) ignores direction.

The line integral of a vector field

\[\int_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = \int_a^b \mathbf{F}\bigl(\mathbf{r}(t)\bigr)\cdot\mathbf{r}'(t)\,dt,\qquad d\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{r}'(t)\,dt = \langle dx,dy,dz\rangle.\]

Steps: parametrize; compute \(\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(t))\); compute \(\mathbf{r}'(t)\); take the dot product; integrate. Notice what the dot product does. With \(\mathbf{T} = \mathbf{r}'/\lVert\mathbf{r}'\rVert\) the unit tangent, \(\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = (\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{T})\,ds\), so the line integral adds up the component of \(\mathbf{F}\) along the path, step by step. The part of the field pushing sideways to the path contributes nothing. That is exactly what the river meter did.

  • If \(\mathbf{F}\) is a force, \(\int_C\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\) is the work done by the force along \(C\).
  • If \(\mathbf{F}\) is the velocity of a fluid, it is the flow along \(C\); for a closed curve it is the circulation. Positive means the fluid moves, on balance, with the direction of \(C\).
Because \(\mathbf{r}'\) changes sign when you walk the other way, reversing the direction of \(C\) changes the sign of \(\int_C\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\). The scalar integral \(\int_C f\,ds\) does not change, because \(ds\) is a length and lengths are positive.

In this lab the blue arrow is \(\mathbf F\) at the walker and the thick arrow is its component along the path (green helps, red fights). The lower graph is the integrand \(\mathbf F\bigl(\mathbf r(t)\bigr)\cdot\mathbf r'(t)\); area above the axis counts as positive work, area below as negative.

Fields whose circulation around every closed loop is zero are special: they are exactly the conservative fields \(\nabla f\) from §15.1, and for them the work depends only on where you start and finish. That is §15.3.

Practice & reflection

  1. In your own words, what is the difference between what \(\int_C f\,ds\) measures and what \(\int_C\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\) measures? Which one changes sign if you walk \(C\) backward, and why?
    \(\int_C f\,ds\) adds up an amount per unit length, such as the mass of a wire; it has no direction. \(\int_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r\) adds up how hard the field pushes along the direction of travel, such as work or flow. Only the second changes sign: walking backward flips every \(\mathbf r'(t)\), but \(\lVert\mathbf r'(t)\rVert\) stays positive.
  2. Why does \(ds\) use the length \(\lVert\mathbf{r}'(t)\rVert\), while \(d\mathbf{r}\) uses the vector \(\mathbf{r}'(t)\)?
    \(ds\) is a distance traveled, so it needs only how far you moved. \(d\mathbf r\) is a small displacement, so it keeps the direction too; dotting it with \(\mathbf F\) picks out the part of \(\mathbf F\) along the motion.
  3. The segment from \((0,0)\) to \((1,2)\) can also be parametrized by \(\mathbf{r}(s) = \langle s/2, s\rangle\), \(0\le s\le2\). Without integrating, why must \(\int_C(x^2+y^2)\,ds\) come out as \(5\sqrt5/3\) again? Then check by integrating.
    It is the same wire with the same density; only the speed of the walk changed, and \(ds\) measures distance, not time. Check: \(\lVert\mathbf r'(s)\rVert=\sqrt{1/4+1}=\sqrt5/2\) and \(x^2+y^2=\tfrac54s^2\), so the integral is \(\int_0^2\tfrac54s^2\cdot\tfrac{\sqrt5}{2}\,ds=\tfrac{5\sqrt5}{8}\cdot\tfrac83=\tfrac{5\sqrt5}{3}\).
  4. Give two different reasons why \(\int_C\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\) might be \(0\) even though \(\mathbf{F}\) is not the zero field.
    (1) \(\mathbf F\) is perpendicular to the path at every point, like the current on the route straight across the river. (2) The field helps on part of the path and fights on another part, and the two cancel exactly. In the second lab, \(\langle0,x\rangle\) on the segment from \((-2,-\tfrac12)\) to \((2,\tfrac12)\) fights on the left half and helps on the right half, for a total of 0.
  5. A wire has density \(\rho\), and another has density \(3\rho\) along the same curve. How do their masses compare? Their centers of mass?
    The second is three times as heavy. The center of mass is the same: \(M_x\), \(M_y\) and \(M\) all triple, and the 3 cancels in \(M_y/M\) and \(M_x/M\).
