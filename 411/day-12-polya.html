How these work. Each walk-through takes one problem through Pólya’s four phases. You answer one step at a time and get feedback right away; when something isn’t right, read why and try again. Hints are there when you ask for them. Your place is saved in this browser, so you can stop and come back.

Something new: you type formulas. Some steps ask for a formula, such as a derivative or a recurrence. Type it with ^ for powers, exp(...) for the exponential and * for multiplication. The page checks what you type at several sample values, so any correct way of writing the formula passes. If yours matches a common slip, you’ll see the comment for that slip.

The walk-throughs end in a full proof, so the proof you bring to class should still be your own, in your own words.

Pólya walk-through · Problem 4.1.2

Differentiate, then set x = a

Problem 4.1.2. Prove Theorem 4.1.1: if \(f(x)=\sum_{n=0}^\infty a_n(x-a)^n\), then \(a_n=\frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}\), where \(f^{(n)}(a)\) represents the \(n\)th derivative of \(f\) evaluated at \(a\).

Goal: Show that the coefficients of a power series about \(a\) are forced to be \(\frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}\), by differentiating term by term and then setting \(x=a\). It builds on term-by-term differentiation of power series from Chapter 3. Its hypothesis, that \(f\) already has a series, is the part that Cauchy’s function fails to satisfy in 4.1.5 and 4.1.6.

Pólya walk-through · Problem 4.1.5

Differentiate in x, rewrite in y

Problem 4.1.5. Cauchy’s Counterexample, Part 1. Adopting the notation \(y=x^{-1}\) and \(f^{(n)}(x)=p_n(y)e^{-y^2}\), find \(p_{n+1}(y)\) in terms of \(p_n(y)\). [Note: Don’t forget that you are differentiating with respect to \(x\), not \(y\).] Use induction on \(n\) to show that \(p_n(y)\) is a polynomial for all \(n\in\mathbb{N}\).

Goal: Find \(p_{n+1}\) from \(p_n\) with the product and chain rules, then prove by induction that every \(p_n\) is a polynomial. It builds on the chain rule from Calculus I and on the first derivatives of Cauchy’s function in the reading. It leads to 4.1.6, which needs the limit of \(\frac{y\,p_n(y)}{e^{y^2}}\) as \(y\to\pm\infty\).

Pólya walk-through · Problem 4.1.6

Flatter than every power

Problem 4.1.6. Cauchy’s Counterexample, Part 2. (a) Let \(m\) be a nonnegative integer. Show that \(\displaystyle\lim_{y\to\pm\infty}\frac{y^m}{e^{y^2}}=0\). Induction and a dash of L’Hôpital’s rule should do the trick. (b) Prove that \(\displaystyle\lim_{y\to\pm\infty}\frac{q(y)}{e^{y^2}}=0\) for any polynomial \(q\). (c) Let \(f(x)\) be as in Cauchy’s counterexample and show that for every nonnegative integer \(n\), \(f^{(n)}(0)=0\).

Goal: Show that \(e^{-y^2}\) beats every power of \(y\), then use that to show every derivative of Cauchy’s function is \(0\) at the origin. It builds on L’Hôpital’s rule from Calculus I and on the recurrence in 4.1.5. It finishes Cauchy’s counterexample: all the derivatives at \(0\) vanish, yet \(f\) is not the zero function.
