# §14.1: Double Integrals

A single integral adds up thin strips to find the area under a curve. A double integral does the same thing one dimension higher: it adds up thin *columns* to find the volume under a surface. The same limit that gave you area now gives you volume, and, if the surface dips below the $$xy$$-plane, a *signed* volume that counts the part below as negative.

Engineers meet this the moment a quantity is spread over a region instead of along a line: the water on a roof, the load on a plate, the mass of a sheet whose thickness changes. The hard part is not the idea but the bookkeeping, since a double integral is computed as two single integrals, one inside the other, and you have to get the limits right.

By the end of this section you should be able to:

1. Form a double Riemann sum over a rectangle and explain how it approaches the double integral.
2. Evaluate a double integral over a rectangle as an iterated integral, in either order.
3. Describe a region as Type I or Type II, and set up the limits of an iterated integral over it.
4. Reverse the order of integration when one order is hard or impossible.
5. Use the properties of double integrals (constant multiples, sums, splitting a region).

### Riemann sums in two variables

Let $$f(x,y)$$ be defined on a rectangle $$R = [a,b] \times [c,d]$$. Cut $$[a,b]$$ into $$m$$ pieces of width $$\Delta x = \frac{b-a}{m}$$ and $$[c,d]$$ into $$n$$ pieces of width $$\Delta y = \frac{d-c}{n}$$. This cuts $$R$$ into $$mn$$ small rectangles, each of area $$\Delta A = \Delta x\,\Delta y$$. Choose a sample point $$(x_i^*, y_j^*)$$ in each one (a corner, the midpoint, any point) and build a box of height $$f(x_i^*, y_j^*)$$ over it. The **double Riemann sum** is the total volume of the boxes, and the **double integral** is the limit as the boxes shrink:

$$\iint_R f(x,y)\,dA = \lim_{m,n \to \infty} \sum_{j=1}^{n} \sum_{i=1}^{m} f(x_i^*, y_j^*)\,\Delta x\,\Delta y.$$

If the limit exists, $$f$$ is **integrable** on $$R$$, and the double integral is the signed volume between the surface $$z = f(x,y)$$ and the $$xy$$-plane.

> **Worked example.** Let $$f(x,y) = 10 - x^2 - y^2$$ on $$R = [0,2] \times [0,2]$$. Estimate the volume with $$m = n = 2, 4, 8, 16$$, using the top-right corner of each small rectangle.
>
> With $$m = n = 2$$ we have $$\Delta x = \Delta y = 1$$, the corners are $$(1,1), (1,2), (2,1), (2,2)$$, and the heights are $$8, 5, 5, 2$$. The sum is $$(8 + 5 + 5 + 2)(1)(1) = 20$$. A computer does the rest:
>
> | $$m = n$$ | 2 | 4 | 8 | 16 |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | Riemann sum | $$20$$ | $$25$$ | $$27.25$$ | $$28.3125$$ |
>
> The exact value (found below with Fubini's theorem) is $$88/3 \approx 29.33$$. Each doubling of $$m$$ and $$n$$ cuts the error roughly in half, from $$9.33$$ to $$4.33$$ to $$2.08$$ to $$1.02$$. All the sums are *under* the true value because $$f$$ decreases as $$x$$ and $$y$$ grow, and the top-right corner is always the lowest corner of its rectangle.

### Fubini's theorem

Riemann sums are slow to compute by hand. Fortunately there is a shortcut: slice the solid. Fix $$x$$ and integrate over $$y$$ to get the area $$A(x)$$ of one cross-section; then add up the slices.

**Fubini's Theorem.** If $$f$$ is continuous on $$R = [a,b] \times [c,d]$$, then

$$\iint_R f(x,y)\,dA = \int_a^b \int_c^d f(x,y)\,dy\,dx = \int_c^d \int_a^b f(x,y)\,dx\,dy.$$

In $$\int_a^b \int_c^d f\,dy\,dx$$ you work from the inside out: integrate with respect to $$y$$ first, treating $$x$$ as a constant, then integrate the result with respect to $$x$$.

> **Worked example.** Evaluate $$\displaystyle\iint_R (6xy^2 - 4y)\,dA$$ over $$R = [0,1] \times [0,2]$$.
>
> $$\int_0^2 (6xy^2 - 4y)\,dy = \Big[\,2xy^3 - 2y^2\,\Big]_0^2 = 16x - 8, \qquad \int_0^1 (16x - 8)\,dx = \Big[\,8x^2 - 8x\,\Big]_0^1 = 0.$$
>
> The signed volume is $$0$$. This does not mean the surface is flat: part of it lies above the $$xy$$-plane and part below, and the two cancel. Doing the $$x$$-integral first gives the same $$0$$.

Now the exact value promised in the Riemann-sum example, with the same $$f$$ and $$R$$:

$$\int_0^2 \int_0^2 (10 - x^2 - y^2)\,dy\,dx = \int_0^2 \left(\tfrac{52}{3} - 2x^2\right) dx = \tfrac{104}{3} - \tfrac{16}{3} = \tfrac{88}{3} \approx 29.33.$$

### General regions: Type I and Type II

Most regions are not rectangles. Fubini's theorem still works, but now the *inner* limits can depend on the outer variable.

- $$R$$ is **Type I** if $$R = \{(x,y) : a \le x \le b,\ g_1(x) \le y \le g_2(x)\}$$. Then $$\displaystyle\iint_R f\,dA = \int_a^b \int_{g_1(x)}^{g_2(x)} f(x,y)\,dy\,dx$$.
- $$R$$ is **Type II** if $$R = \{(x,y) : c \le y \le d,\ h_1(y) \le x \le h_2(y)\}$$. Then $$\displaystyle\iint_R f\,dA = \int_c^d \int_{h_1(y)}^{h_2(y)} f(x,y)\,dx\,dy$$.

Sketch the region first. For Type I, draw a vertical line through it: the curve where the line enters is $$g_1$$ and the curve where it leaves is $$g_2$$. The common mistake: the *outer* limits must be constants. Only the inner limits may contain the outer variable.

> **Worked example.** Evaluate $$\displaystyle\iint_R 5y\,dA$$ where $$R$$ lies between $$y = 3x^2$$ and $$y = 2 + x^2$$.
>
> The curves meet where $$3x^2 = 2 + x^2$$, so $$x = \pm 1$$, at height $$y = 3$$. Between them the lower curve is $$3x^2$$ and the upper is $$2 + x^2$$, so $$R$$ is Type I:
>
> $$\int_{-1}^{1} \int_{3x^2}^{2+x^2} 5y\,dy\,dx = \int_{-1}^{1} \tfrac52\big[(2+x^2)^2 - 9x^4\big]\,dx = \int_{-1}^{1} (10 + 10x^2 - 20x^4)\,dx = \frac{56}{3}.$$

### Reversing the order

Sometimes one order cannot be done at all, because an inner integral has no elementary antiderivative. Then describe the *same* region the other way and swap the order.

> **Worked example.** Evaluate $$\displaystyle\int_0^1 \int_y^1 \frac{\sin x}{x}\,dx\,dy$$.
>
> There is no elementary antiderivative of $$\frac{\sin x}{x}$$, so integrating in $$x$$ first is hopeless. Read the limits as a region: $$0 \le y \le 1$$ and $$y \le x \le 1$$, which is the triangle with vertices $$(0,0)$$, $$(1,0)$$, $$(1,1)$$. Describe it the other way: $$0 \le x \le 1$$ and $$0 \le y \le x$$. Now
>
> $$\int_0^1 \int_0^x \frac{\sin x}{x}\,dy\,dx = \int_0^1 \sin x\,dx = 1 - \cos 1 \approx 0.460.$$

### Volume of a solid

If $$f(x,y) \ge 0$$ on $$R$$, then $$\iint_R f\,dA$$ is the volume under the surface and over $$R$$.

> **Worked example.** Find the volume of the tetrahedron bounded by the coordinate planes and the plane $$2x + 3y + z = 6$$.
>
> The top is $$z = 6 - 2x - 3y$$. The base is where $$z = 0$$: the triangle with vertices $$(0,0)$$, $$(3,0)$$, $$(0,2)$$, which is Type I with $$0 \le x \le 3$$ and $$0 \le y \le 2 - \tfrac23 x$$. The inner integral is $$6 - 4x + \tfrac23 x^2$$, so
>
> $$V = \int_0^3 \left(6 - 4x + \tfrac23 x^2\right) dx = 18 - 18 + 6 = 6.$$
>
> Check with geometry: a pyramid has volume $$\tfrac13 (\text{base area})(\text{height}) = \tfrac13(3)(6) = 6$$.

### Properties

For continuous $$f, g$$ and a constant $$k$$: $$\iint_R kf\,dA = k\iint_R f\,dA$$ and $$\iint_R (f \pm g)\,dA = \iint_R f\,dA \pm \iint_R g\,dA$$. If $$f \ge g$$ on $$R$$ then $$\iint_R f\,dA \ge \iint_R g\,dA$$, and in particular $$f \ge 0$$ gives $$\iint_R f\,dA \ge 0$$. If $$R$$ splits into $$R_1$$ and $$R_2$$ that overlap only along a boundary, then $$\iint_R f\,dA = \iint_{R_1} f\,dA + \iint_{R_2} f\,dA$$. Setting $$f = 1$$ in the double integral gives the **area** of $$R$$.

### Practice & reflection

1. What does $$\iint_R 1\,dA$$ measure, and how does it connect to the area between two curves from single-variable calculus?
2. When would you integrate $$dy\,dx$$ rather than $$dx\,dy$$ over a region that is not a rectangle? What should you look for?
3. If $$f(x,y) < 0$$ everywhere on $$R$$, what is the sign of $$\iint_R f\,dA$$, and what does it represent as a volume?
4. Fubini's theorem asks for $$f$$ to be continuous. Why might a jump or a blow-up inside $$R$$ cause trouble? (Think back to improper integrals.)
