# §13.4: The Chain Rule for Multivariable Functions

In single-variable calculus, the Chain Rule says that if $$y = f(u)$$ and $$u = g(x)$$, then $$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{du}\frac{du}{dx}$$: you multiply the rates along the chain. Real quantities have more than one chain. A plate's temperature depends on where you stand on it, and where you stand changes as you walk. A cylinder's volume depends on its radius and height, and both change as a sand pile grows. The question stays the same, how does the outer quantity change while the inner ones move, but now there can be several inner quantities and several routes from the top down.

The multivariable Chain Rule gives one recipe for all of these: draw the routes, multiply the rates along each route, and add. A tree diagram keeps the routes straight so you don't miss one. The same recipe also gives you implicit differentiation, so you can find $$\frac{dy}{dx}$$ from an equation like $$x^3 - y^2 = \sin(xy)$$ without solving for $$y$$ first.

By the end of this section you should be able to:

1. Draw a tree diagram for a composite function and list every route from the top variable down to an independent variable.
2. Apply the Chain Rule for one parameter, $$\frac{dz}{dt}$$, for two independent variables, $$\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}$$, and in the general case with more intermediate variables.
3. Read the one-parameter Chain Rule as a dot product of a gradient with a velocity, and say what each term means.
4. Find $$\frac{dy}{dx}$$ and $$\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}$$ for functions defined implicitly by an equation.

### Case I: one parameter, two routes

Start with the most common setup. A quantity $$z = f(x,y)$$ depends on two variables, and both depend on one parameter: $$x = g(t)$$ and $$y = h(t)$$. The point $$(x(t), y(t))$$ traces a path in the plane, and $$z$$ is the height of the surface above that path. The tree shows the two routes from $$z$$ down to $$t$$:

```
       z
      / \
     x   y
    /     \
   t       t
```

**Theorem (Case I).** If $$z = f(x,y)$$, $$x = g(t)$$, and $$y = h(t)$$ are differentiable, then

$$\frac{dz}{dt} = \frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\frac{dx}{dt} + \frac{\partial z}{\partial y}\frac{dy}{dt}.$$

Use $$\partial$$ on the top branches, where $$z$$ depends on two variables, and $$d$$ on the bottom branches, where $$x$$ and $$y$$ depend on one. In vector form, $$\frac{dz}{dt} = \nabla f \cdot \mathbf{r}'(t)$$ with $$\nabla f = \langle f_x, f_y \rangle$$ and $$\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle x(t), y(t) \rangle$$, so the two terms are the two products inside that dot product. Think of your GPA as $$z$$, with two course grades $$x$$ and $$y$$ that each depend on your weekly study hours $$t$$. Then $$\frac{dz}{dt}$$ adds up the two indirect effects of studying.

**The common mistake** is stopping after one route. Writing only $$\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\frac{dx}{dt}$$ ignores the second route, which is wrong whenever that route contributes.

> **Worked example.** A cylinder has volume $$V = \pi r^2 h$$. Let $$r(t) = t$$ and $$h(t) = 6 - t$$ for $$1 \le t \le 5$$. Find $$\frac{dV}{dt}$$ and the time at which the volume is largest.
>
> The partials are $$\frac{\partial V}{\partial r} = 2\pi r h$$ and $$\frac{\partial V}{\partial h} = \pi r^2$$, with $$\frac{dr}{dt} = 1$$ and $$\frac{dh}{dt} = -1$$. So
>
> $$\frac{dV}{dt} = (2\pi r h)(1) + (\pi r^2)(-1) = 2\pi t(6-t) - \pi t^2 = 12\pi t - 3\pi t^2.$$
>
> Setting this to zero gives $$3\pi t(4-t) = 0$$, so $$t = 4$$ is the only critical time in $$[1,5]$$. Since $$V(1) = 5\pi$$, $$V(4) = 32\pi$$, and $$V(5) = 25\pi$$, the largest volume is $$32\pi$$, at $$t = 4$$.
>
> At $$t = 4$$ the two terms are $$(2\pi \cdot 4 \cdot 2)(1) = 16\pi$$ and $$(\pi \cdot 4^2)(-1) = -16\pi$$. Neither is zero, but they cancel exactly. You'll see that again in the demo.

### Case II: two independent variables

Now let $$z = f(u,v)$$ with $$u = g(x,y)$$ and $$v = h(x,y)$$. Each intermediate variable now branches to both $$x$$ and $$y$$:

```
         z
       /   \
      u     v
     / \   / \
    x   y x   y
```

To find $$\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}$$, trace every route from $$z$$ to $$x$$, multiply along each one, and add. Doing the same for $$y$$ gives

$$\frac{\partial z}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial z}{\partial u}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial z}{\partial v}\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}, \qquad \frac{\partial z}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial z}{\partial u}\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial z}{\partial v}\frac{\partial v}{\partial y}.$$

Both formulas reuse the same two outer partials, so compute those once and use them twice.

> **Worked example.** Let $$z = e^{u+2v}$$ with $$u = x^2 + y$$ and $$v = y^2 - x$$. Find $$\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}$$ and $$\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}$$.
>
> The outer partials are $$\frac{\partial z}{\partial u} = e^{u+2v}$$ and $$\frac{\partial z}{\partial v} = 2e^{u+2v}$$. The inner partials are $$\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = 2x$$, $$\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = 1$$, $$\frac{\partial v}{\partial x} = -1$$, and $$\frac{\partial v}{\partial y} = 2y$$. Therefore
>
> $$\frac{\partial z}{\partial x} = e^{u+2v}(2x) + 2e^{u+2v}(-1) = (2x-2)\,e^{x^2+2y^2+y-2x},$$
>
> $$\frac{\partial z}{\partial y} = e^{u+2v}(1) + 2e^{u+2v}(2y) = (1+4y)\,e^{x^2+2y^2+y-2x}.$$
>
> At $$(x,y) = (2,1)$$ the exponent is $$3$$, so $$\frac{\partial z}{\partial x} = 2e^3 \approx 40.2$$ and $$\frac{\partial z}{\partial y} = 5e^3 \approx 100.4$$.

### The general case: more intermediate variables

The pattern works at any depth. If $$y = f(u_1, \ldots, u_n)$$ and each $$u_j$$ depends on $$x_1, \ldots, x_m$$, then

$$\frac{\partial y}{\partial x_i} = \sum_{j=1}^{n} \frac{\partial y}{\partial u_j}\frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i},$$

with one product for every intermediate variable. Its vector form is $$\nabla f \cdot \frac{\partial \mathbf{u}}{\partial x_i}$$, the same dot-product pattern as before.

> **Worked example.** Let $$w = 2xy^3 + x^2 - yz^2$$, where $$x = s - t$$, $$y = s^2 + t^2$$, and $$z = st$$. Find $$\frac{\partial w}{\partial s}$$ and $$\frac{\partial w}{\partial t}$$.
>
> The outer partials form $$\nabla w = \langle 2y^3 + 2x,\; 6xy^2 - z^2,\; -2yz \rangle$$, and the inner partials are $$\mathbf{r}_s = \langle 1, 2s, t \rangle$$ and $$\mathbf{r}_t = \langle -1, 2t, s \rangle$$. Then
>
> $$\frac{\partial w}{\partial s} = \nabla w \cdot \mathbf{r}_s = 2y^3 + 2x + 12sxy^2 - 2sz^2 - 2tyz,$$
>
> $$\frac{\partial w}{\partial t} = \nabla w \cdot \mathbf{r}_t = -2y^3 - 2x + 12txy^2 - 2tz^2 - 2syz.$$
>
> At $$(s,t) = (1,1)$$ we have $$x = 0$$, $$y = 2$$, and $$z = 1$$, so $$\frac{\partial w}{\partial s} = 10$$ and $$\frac{\partial w}{\partial t} = -22$$.

### Implicit differentiation as a Chain Rule

If $$F(x,y) = 0$$ defines $$y$$ as a function of $$x$$, differentiate $$F(x, y(x))$$ with respect to $$x$$. The Chain Rule gives $$F_x + F_y\frac{dy}{dx} = 0$$, so

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{F_x}{F_y} \qquad (F_y \neq 0).$$

For $$F(x,y,z) = 0$$ defining $$z = f(x,y)$$, differentiate with respect to $$x$$ (where $$\frac{\partial y}{\partial x} = 0$$), then with respect to $$y$$ (where $$\frac{\partial x}{\partial y} = 0$$). You get $$\frac{\partial z}{\partial x} = -\frac{F_x}{F_z}$$ and $$\frac{\partial z}{\partial y} = -\frac{F_y}{F_z}$$ when $$F_z \neq 0$$.

> **Worked example.** Find $$\frac{dy}{dx}$$ if $$x^3 - y^2 = \sin(xy)$$.
>
> Let $$F(x,y) = x^3 - y^2 - \sin(xy) = 0$$. Then $$F_x = 3x^2 - y\cos(xy)$$ and $$F_y = -2y - x\cos(xy)$$, so
>
> $$\frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{F_x}{F_y} = \frac{3x^2 - y\cos(xy)}{2y + x\cos(xy)}.$$
>
> The same recipe handles three variables. For $$x^2 + yz + z^2 = 3$$, you get $$\frac{\partial z}{\partial x} = -\frac{2x}{y + 2z}$$ and $$\frac{\partial z}{\partial y} = -\frac{z}{y + 2z}$$.

### Practice & reflection

1. At $$t = 4$$ in the cylinder example, the two terms are $$+16\pi$$ and $$-16\pi$$. In words, what does each one measure at that instant, and why does their cancellation make sense at a maximum?
2. A classmate says that for $$z = f(x(t), y(t))$$ the rate is just $$\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\frac{dx}{dt}$$, because "the $$y$$ part doesn't move." Using the GPA picture, explain what is wrong with that reasoning, and say exactly when the classmate's formula would be correct.
3. If $$\nabla f \cdot \mathbf{r}'(t) = 0$$ at some instant, what does that say about the direction the path is moving relative to the level curves of $$f$$, even though neither $$\nabla f$$ nor $$\mathbf{r}'(t)$$ has to be zero?
4. The formula $$\frac{\partial z}{\partial x} = -\frac{F_x}{F_z}$$ needs $$F_z \neq 0$$. Describe what the surface $$F(x,y,z) = 0$$ looks like near a point where $$F_z = 0$$, and why you can't solve for $$z$$ as a function of $$x$$ and $$y$$ there.
