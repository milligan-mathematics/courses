Paper first, then check in. Give yourself 15 minutes for the five problems on paper, no calculator, the way the real test runs. Then open each card. If you already have an answer, press Check my answer from paper: one try. Right, and you can skip to Look back for a faster route or a variation. Not right, or not sure, and the walk-through takes it one step at a time: Understand, Plan, Carry out, Look back.

Every wrong choice has a story. The test writers build the wrong answers out of the slips people actually make. When you pick one here, the page tells you which slip leads there, so you can spot it next time.

Your place is saved in this browser, so you can stop and come back. Bring the one card that bugged you most to our check-in.

Walk-through · Set B13, Problem 1

A line integral around a circle

1. Let \(C\) be the circle \(x^2+y^2=4\), oriented counterclockwise. Evaluate: \(\displaystyle\oint_C (x^2-y)\,dx+(x+y^3)\,dy\)
(A) \(0\)
(B) \(2\pi\)
(C) \(4\pi\)
(D) \(8\pi\)
(E) \(16\pi\)

Goal: Turn the line integral into a double integral over the disk with Green’s theorem. Green’s theorem shows up on the MFT as a shortcut: a messy line integral around a closed curve becomes “constant times area.” Stokes’ and the divergence theorem are its bigger relatives.

Walk-through · Set B13, Problem 2

Which matrices diagonalize?

2. Which of the following matrices are diagonalizable over the real numbers?
  I. \(\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\)
  II. \(\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&2\end{pmatrix}\)
  III. \(\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\)
(A) I only
(B) II only
(C) III only
(D) II and III only
(E) I, II, and III

Goal: Decide each matrix by finding its eigenvalues and counting independent eigenvectors. Diagonalizability is a favorite MFT linear algebra item because it has three classic failure modes: a repeated eigenvalue short on eigenvectors, complex eigenvalues, and the mistaken shortcuts.

Walk-through · Set B13, Problem 3

The slope of an inverse function

3. Let \(f(x)=x^3+x+1\), and let \(g\) be the inverse function of \(f\). What is \(g’(3)\)?
(A) \(\frac{1}{31}\)
(B) \(\frac{1}{28}\)
(C) \(\frac{1}{4}\)
(D) \(4\)
(E) \(28\)

Goal: Find \(g’(3)\) without a formula for \(g\), using the slope of \(f\) at the matching point. The inverse-function rule \(g’(y) = \frac{1}{f’(g(y))}\) is a standard MFT item, usually with an \(f\) you can’t invert by algebra. The whole trick is finding the right point.

Walk-through · Set B13, Problem 4

Salt in a tank

4. A tank holds \(100\) liters of pure water. Brine containing \(2\) grams of salt per liter flows in at \(5\) liters per minute, and the well-mixed solution drains at \(5\) liters per minute. How many grams of salt are in the tank after \(20\) minutes?
(A) \(200\left(1-e^{-1}\right)\)
(B) \(200e^{-1}\)
(C) \(100\left(1-e^{-1}\right)\)
(D) \(100\)
(E) \(200\)

Goal: Set up “rate in minus rate out” for the salt, solve it, and evaluate at \(t = 20\). Mixing tanks are the MFT’s favorite applied first-order linear differential equation. The same equation models Newton’s law of cooling and an RC circuit charging.

Walk-through · Set B13, Problem 5

Powers of x on the unit interval

5. For each positive integer \(n\), let \(f_n(x)=x^n\) on \([0,1]\). Which of the following are true?
  I. \((f_n)\) converges pointwise on \([0,1]\).
  II. \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_0^1 f_n(x)\,dx=0\)
  III. \((f_n)\) converges uniformly on \(\left[0,\frac12\right]\).
(A) I only
(B) II only
(C) I and II only
(D) I and III only
(E) I, II, and III

Goal: Decide each statement from the definitions, paying attention to which interval each one is about. \(x^n\) on \([0,1]\) is the standard example separating pointwise from uniform convergence. MFT analysis items use it, and they like to change the interval.
