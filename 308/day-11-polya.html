How these work. Each walk-through takes one exercise through Pólya’s four phases. You answer one step at a time and get feedback right away; when something isn’t right, read why and try again. Hints are there when you ask for them. Your place is saved in this browser, so you can stop and come back.

The walk-throughs end in full solutions, so the work you present in class should still be your own, in your own words.

Pólya walk-through · Exercise 5.4.17

One pair that doesn’t commute

Exercise 5.4.17. Prove that \(S_n\) is nonabelian for \(n \geq 3\).

Goal: Find one pair of permutations that don’t commute, then see why the same pair still fails to commute in every \(S_n\) with \(n \ge 3\). It uses cycle notation and the composition rule from Section 5.1 (Theorem 5.1.1 says \(S_n\) is a group). Exercise 5.4.18 asks the same question for \(A_n\).

Pólya walk-through · Exercise 5.4.18

Even permutations that don’t commute

Exercise 5.4.18. Show that \(A_n\) is nonabelian for \(n \geq 4\).

Goal: Find two even permutations that don’t commute, and check that they really are even before you use them. It uses the alternating group (Theorem 5.1.16 says \(A_n\) is a subgroup of \(S_n\)) and the parity theorem (Theorem 5.1.15). It is the same question as Exercise 5.4.17, one level down.

Pólya walk-through · Exercise 5.4.29

Elements that commute with everything

Exercise 5.4.29. Recall that the center of a group \(G\) is \(Z(G) = \{g \in G : gx = xg \text{ for all } x \in G\}\). Find the center of \(D_8\). What about the center of \(D_{10}\)? What is the center of \(D_n\)?

Goal: Test each element of \(D_n\) against the two basic motions \(r\) and \(s\), then find the center for every \(n\). It uses the dihedral groups of Section 5.2: the order \(2n\) (Theorem 5.2.2) and the generators \(r\) and \(s\) with \(srs = r^{-1}\) (Theorem 5.2.3).

Pólya walk-through · Exercise 5.4.34

Even in, even out

Exercise 5.4.34. If \(\alpha\) is even, prove that \(\alpha^{-1}\) is also even. Does a corresponding result hold if \(\alpha\) is odd?

Goal: Use the definition of even and odd (after Theorem 5.1.15), then decide what reversing a product does to the number of its factors. It uses the definition of even and odd permutations (after Theorem 5.1.15), and the fact that a transposition is its own inverse (proof of Theorem 5.1.16). Proposition 5.1.17 gives the count in the Look back step.
