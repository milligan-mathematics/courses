Walk-through · Part A

The bell curve

Part A. Do Part A on paper first, then check in here.
The model from here on is \(\text{mpg} = \underbrace{mx + c}_{\text{the line}} + \underbrace{\varepsilon}_{\text{noise}}\), where the noise \(\varepsilon\) is different for every car and follows a bell curve (a Gaussian) centered at 0. The handout’s picture shows it: each car’s mpg is a draw from a bell standing on the line at that car’s weight.
The bell’s width is a number \(\sigma\) (“sigma”). We’ll use \(\sigma = 2\) mpg. The height of the bell at a miss of \(r\) mpg, relative to its peak, is \(h(r) = e^{-r^2/(2\sigma^2)} = e^{-r^2/8}\).
1. Fill in the table with a calculator.\[\begin{array}{c|c|c|c|c|c|c} r & 0 & \pm1 & \pm2 & \pm3 & \pm4 & \pm6\\ \hline h(r) & 1 & & & 0.32 & & \end{array}\]2. In words: how much less likely is a 2-mpg miss than no miss at all? A 6-mpg miss?
3. Why does \(h(-2)\) equal \(h(2)\)? What does that say about the noise?

Goal: Check your table of bell heights, and read what it says about small misses and big ones. Since Day 1 we’ve scored lines by squared error, and on Day 7 you invented a loss of your own. Today you’ll see where squaring comes from. Part A is the bell itself; Part B uses its heights to score whole lines.

Walk-through · Part B

Which line makes the data most likely?

Part B. Do Part B on paper first, then check in here.
Three cars: weights \(x = 2,\ 3,\ 4\) (thousand lb), mpg \(y = 32,\ 24,\ 18\). If the noise on each car is independent, the likelihood of a line is the product of the bell heights at its three misses. Bigger likelihood means the data is a more believable draw from that line.
1. Fill in the misses and heights for two candidate lines, Line A: \(45 - 7x\) and Line B: \(44 - 6x\). For each car: the predicted mpg, the miss \(r\), and \(h(r)\); then each line’s likelihood (the product).
2. Which line is more likely? Now compute each line’s total squared error \(\sum r^2\). What do you notice?
3. Here’s why it happens. Use the rule \(e^a e^b = e^{a+b}\) to show that, for any line, \(\text{likelihood} = e^{-\left(r_1^2 + r_2^2 + r_3^2\right)/8}\). Check it with your numbers for Line A (expect the last decimal place to differ a little, from rounding).
4. So the line with the biggest likelihood is the one with the smallest ________. The least-squares line for these three cars is \(45\tfrac23 - 7x\). Compute its likelihood. Does it beat Lines A and B?
5. Would the winner change if the noise were wider, say \(\sigma = 4\) so that \(h(r) = e^{-r^2/32}\)? Explain using the formula in 3, not by recomputing everything.
The big idea. Taking the logarithm turns the product into a sum: \(\ln(\text{likelihood}) = -\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum r_i^2\). Making the likelihood as large as possible is the same job as making the squared error as small as possible. This is maximum likelihood, and it is MML’s equation (9.10b) in disguise.

Goal: Score two lines by how likely they make the data, and see why the most likely line is always the least-squares line. Part A gave the bell heights. Here they score whole lines, and the product of heights turns into \(e^{-\sum r^2/8}\): squared error, straight out of a bell curve. Part C swaps in a different curve and gets a different loss.

Walk-through · Part C

A different bell, a different loss

Part C. Do Part C on paper first, then check in here.
The handout’s picture shows two curves of relative height against the miss \(r\): the bell, \(e^{-r^2/8}\), and a pointy curve, \(e^{-|r|/2}\). Suppose instead the noise followed the pointy curve, with height \(e^{-|r|/2}\).
1. Repeat the trick from Part B, problem 3: the likelihood is now \(e^{-(\text{something})/2}\). What is the something? So which loss goes with the pointy curve?
2. Compare the two curves at \(r = 6\). Under which one is a 6-mpg miss “nearly impossible”?
3. The simplest model predicts the same number \(k\) for every car. Five cars get \(21,\ 22,\ 23,\ 24,\ 45\) mpg (the 45 might be a typo). For \(k = 23\) and for \(k = 27\), compute the total squared error and the total absolute error \(\sum|r|\).
4. Which \(k\) does each loss prefer? One of them is the mean of the five numbers, and one is the median. Which describes a typical car better here?
5. Use the picture to explain why squared error gets pulled so hard by the 45.
Skeptic. “On Day 5 the mpg histogram leaned to the right, so it isn’t a bell curve. That means least squares is the wrong tool for Auto MPG.” Find the flaw. (Hint: what is supposed to be bell-shaped in our model: the mpg values themselves, or something else?) What plot would you make to check the real assumption?

Goal: Match a noise curve to its loss, and see what each loss does with one wild car. Part B got squared error out of a bell. A pointy curve gives absolute error, the score that shrugged off Day 6’s typo when there were enough points. Day 16 makes its data the textbook way: a curve plus bell-curve noise with \(\sigma = 0.2\).
