# §14.5: Triple Integrals in Cylindrical and Spherical Coordinates

Try to describe a ball, a cone, or a can with boxes and you get square roots everywhere. Those solids are round, and some coordinate systems are built for round. **Cylindrical coordinates** are polar coordinates with a height added, and they suit solids that have an axis of symmetry. **Spherical coordinates** measure distance from the origin and two angles, and they suit balls, cones, and anything described by $$x^2 + y^2 + z^2$$.

As in §14.3, the new idea is that a coordinate "box" is bent, so its volume is not the product of its three side widths. The correction is a factor you must not forget.

By the end of this section you should be able to:

1. Convert points and equations between Cartesian, cylindrical, and spherical coordinates.
2. Explain where the factors $$r$$ and $$\rho^2\sin\varphi$$ in $$dV$$ come from.
3. Set up and evaluate a triple integral in cylindrical coordinates.
4. Set up and evaluate a triple integral in spherical coordinates.
5. Decide which of the three coordinate systems suits a given solid and integrand.

### Cylindrical coordinates

A point is $$(r, \theta, z)$$: $$r \ge 0$$ is the distance from the $$z$$-axis, $$\theta$$ is the polar angle of the point's shadow in the $$xy$$-plane, and $$z$$ is the same height as before.

$$x = r\cos\theta,\qquad y = r\sin\theta,\qquad z = z,\qquad r^2 = x^2 + y^2.$$

Going back, $$\tan\theta = y/x$$ does not by itself fix $$\theta$$. Look at the quadrant of $$(x, y)$$ first.

A small cylindrical box has a radial side $$\Delta r$$, a height $$\Delta z$$, and an arc of length $$r\,\Delta\theta$$, so $$\Delta V \approx r\,\Delta r\,\Delta\theta\,\Delta z$$ and

$$\iiint_S f\,dV = \iiint f(r\cos\theta,\ r\sin\theta,\ z)\;r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta.$$

To set it up: sketch; write the bounding surfaces in terms of $$r$$ and $$z$$; the inner limits are the lower and upper surfaces $$h_1(r,\theta) \le z \le h_2(r,\theta)$$; project onto the $$xy$$-plane and describe that region in polar coordinates for the $$r$$ and $$\theta$$ limits.

> **Worked example.** Find the volume of the solid bounded below by $$z = x^2 + y^2$$ and above by $$z = 1$$.
>
> In cylindrical coordinates the paraboloid is $$z = r^2$$. The surfaces meet where $$r^2 = 1$$, so the projection is the unit disk, $$0 \le r \le 1$$, $$0 \le \theta \le 2\pi$$:
>
> $$V = \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^1 \int_{r^2}^{1} r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta = \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^1 (r - r^3)\,dr\,d\theta = 2\pi\cdot\tfrac14 = \frac{\pi}{2}.$$

> **Worked example.** The same solid has density $$\rho(x,y,z) = 1 - z$$. Find its mass and center of mass.
>
> The density depends only on $$z$$, so by symmetry $$\bar x = \bar y = 0$$. Then
>
> $$M = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^1\!\!\int_{r^2}^1 (1 - z)\,r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta = 2\pi\cdot\tfrac1{12} = \frac{\pi}{6}, \qquad M_{xy} = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^1\!\!\int_{r^2}^1 z(1 - z)\,r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta = 2\pi\cdot\tfrac1{24} = \frac{\pi}{12},$$
>
> so $$\bar z = M_{xy}/M = \tfrac12$$ and the center of mass is $$\left(0, 0, \tfrac12\right)$$. (With constant density it would be higher, at $$\bar z = \tfrac23$$. This density thins out toward the top, so the center of mass drops.)

> **Worked example.** Find the mass of the solid bounded by the cylinder $$(x - 1)^2 + y^2 = 1$$, the $$xy$$-plane, and the cone $$z = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$$, if its density is $$\rho(x,y,z) = x$$.
>
> The cylinder expands to $$x^2 + y^2 = 2x$$, which is $$r = 2\cos\theta$$. That circle is traced as $$\theta$$ goes from $$-\tfrac\pi2$$ to $$\tfrac\pi2$$, because $$r$$ cannot be negative. The cone is $$z = r$$ and the density is $$x = r\cos\theta$$:
>
> $$M = \int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2} \int_0^{2\cos\theta} \int_0^{r} (r\cos\theta)\,r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta = \int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2} 4\cos^5\theta\,d\theta = 8\cdot\frac{8}{15} = \frac{64}{15}.$$
>
> The last step writes $$\cos^5\theta = (1 - \sin^2\theta)^2\cos\theta$$ and substitutes $$u = \sin\theta$$.

### Spherical coordinates

A point is $$(\rho, \theta, \varphi)$$: $$\rho \ge 0$$ is the distance from the origin, $$\theta$$ is the same angle as before, and $$\varphi$$ is the angle down from the positive $$z$$-axis, with $$0 \le \varphi \le \pi$$.

$$x = \rho\sin\varphi\cos\theta,\qquad y = \rho\sin\varphi\sin\theta,\qquad z = \rho\cos\varphi,\qquad \rho^2 = x^2 + y^2 + z^2.$$

Notice $$r = \rho\sin\varphi$$ links spherical to cylindrical. Why does $$\varphi$$ stop at $$\pi$$ while $$\theta$$ goes to $$2\pi$$? Because $$\theta$$ already swings all the way around the $$z$$-axis; $$\varphi$$ only has to get from the north pole to the south pole.

A small spherical box has a radial side $$\Delta\rho$$, a side $$\rho\,\Delta\varphi$$ along a meridian, and a side $$\rho\sin\varphi\,\Delta\theta$$ along a circle of latitude. So

$$dV = \rho^2\sin\varphi\,d\rho\,d\varphi\,d\theta.$$

The $$\sin\varphi$$ is there because the circles of latitude get smaller near the poles. The usual order is $$d\rho\,d\varphi\,d\theta$$, with $$\rho$$ running from the origin (or an inner surface) out to a bounding surface, then $$\varphi$$, then $$\theta$$.

> **Worked example.** Write the sphere $$(x - 2)^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 4$$ in spherical coordinates, and set up the volume of its first-octant piece.
>
> Expanding gives $$x^2 + y^2 + z^2 - 4x = 0$$, so $$\rho^2 = 4\rho\sin\varphi\cos\theta$$, and (for $$\rho \ne 0$$) the sphere is $$\rho = 4\sin\varphi\cos\theta$$. In the first octant $$0 \le \theta \le \tfrac\pi2$$ and $$0 \le \varphi \le \tfrac\pi2$$, and $$\rho$$ runs from $$0$$ out to the sphere:
>
> $$V = \int_0^{\pi/2} \int_0^{\pi/2} \int_0^{4\sin\varphi\cos\theta} \rho^2\sin\varphi\,d\rho\,d\varphi\,d\theta.$$
>
> This is a quarter of a ball of radius $$2$$, and the integral does equal $$\tfrac14\cdot\tfrac43\pi\cdot 2^3 = \tfrac{8\pi}{3}$$, which is a good check on the limits.

> **Worked example.** Find the volume of the solid bounded below by the cone $$\varphi = \tfrac\pi6$$ and above by the sphere $$\rho = 1$$.
>
> $$V = \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^{\pi/6} \int_0^1 \rho^2\sin\varphi\,d\rho\,d\varphi\,d\theta = 2\pi\cdot\frac13\left(1 - \frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right) = \frac{\pi(2 - \sqrt3)}{3} \approx 0.281.$$

> **Worked example.** Evaluate $$\displaystyle\iiint_S (x^2 + y^2)\,dV$$ where $$S$$ is the part of the shell $$1 \le \rho \le 2$$ above the cone $$\varphi = \tfrac\pi3$$ (that is, $$0 \le \varphi \le \tfrac\pi3$$).
>
> Here $$x^2 + y^2 = \rho^2\sin^2\varphi$$, so the integrand is $$\rho^2\sin^2\varphi$$ and
>
> $$\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^{\pi/3}\int_1^2 \rho^4\sin^3\varphi\,d\rho\,d\varphi\,d\theta = 2\pi\cdot\frac{31}{5}\cdot\frac{5}{24} = \frac{31\pi}{12}.$$
>
> The $$\varphi$$-integral uses $$\sin^3\varphi = (1 - \cos^2\varphi)\sin\varphi$$ and $$u = \cos\varphi$$. In Cartesian coordinates this region would be a nightmare.

### Practice & reflection

1. You set up a cylindrical integral and forget the $$r$$, or a spherical one and forget $$\rho^2\sin\varphi$$. Will the answer be off by a sign, off by some factor, impossible to evaluate, or carry the wrong units? Test your answer on a solid whose volume you know.
2. Describe a solid and an integrand for which Cartesian coordinates are the easiest choice, one for cylindrical, and one for spherical. What features of the boundaries and of the integrand pushed you toward each?
3. Why does $$\varphi$$ only go from $$0$$ to $$\pi$$? What would go wrong if it went from $$0$$ to $$2\pi$$ while $$\theta$$ also went from $$0$$ to $$2\pi$$?
4. In the third cylindrical example, why do the $$\theta$$-limits run from $$-\tfrac\pi2$$ to $$\tfrac\pi2$$ and not from $$0$$ to $$2\pi$$? What does the sign of $$\cos\theta$$ have to do with it?
