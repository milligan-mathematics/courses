# §12.2: Arc Length and the Unit Tangent Vector

How long is a winding path? You cannot lay a ruler along a curve, but you can lay many short rulers: replace the curve by a chain of straight chords, add up their lengths, and use more and more chords. In Calculus II this gave you the arc length formula for a graph. In space the same idea works, and the velocity vector from §12.1 does the heavy lifting.

This section also asks a second question: if all you care about is the *shape* of the path, how do you separate it from how fast you happen to travel along it? The answer is two ideas that the rest of the chapter depends on: the arc-length parameter, and the unit tangent vector $$\mathbf{T}$$, which records direction and nothing else.

By the end of this section you should be able to:

1. Compute the arc length of a space curve by integrating speed.
2. Reparametrize a curve with respect to arc length when the integral allows it.
3. Find the unit tangent vector $$\mathbf{T}(t)$$ and explain what it does and does not tell you.
4. Use the vector function of a projectile to find its velocity, maximum height, and where it is at a given time.

### Arc length

Suppose $$\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle f(t), g(t), h(t) \rangle$$ for $$a \le t \le b$$, with $$f', g', h'$$ continuous, and the curve is traced out once. Its **arc length** is

$$L = \int_a^b \sqrt{[f'(t)]^2 + [g'(t)]^2 + [h'(t)]^2}\; dt = \int_a^b \lVert \mathbf{r}'(t) \rVert \, dt.$$

Why it works: over a tiny time $$\Delta t$$ the particle moves about $$\lVert \mathbf{r}'(t) \rVert\,\Delta t$$ along the curve, which is the length of one little chord. Adding the chords and letting $$\Delta t \to 0$$ gives the integral. You are integrating **speed** to get **distance traveled**.

The common mistake is to integrate the velocity *vector* instead of its length. That gives the net displacement, and a particle that returns to its starting point would have "traveled" a zero vector.

> **Worked example.** Find the length of the helix $$\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle \cos t,\ t/2,\ \sin t \rangle$$ for $$0 \le t \le 4\pi$$.
>
> Differentiate: $$\mathbf{r}'(t) = \langle -\sin t,\ \tfrac12,\ \cos t \rangle$$. The speed is
>
> $$\lVert \mathbf{r}'(t) \rVert = \sqrt{\sin^2 t + \tfrac14 + \cos^2 t} = \sqrt{\tfrac54} = \tfrac{\sqrt5}{2},$$
>
> a constant. So $$L = \int_0^{4\pi} \tfrac{\sqrt5}{2}\,dt = 2\pi\sqrt5 \approx 14.05$$.

### The arc-length parameter

Define $$s(t) = \int_{t_0}^{t} \lVert \mathbf{r}'(u) \rVert\,du$$, the distance traveled from the starting parameter $$t_0$$. By the Fundamental Theorem, $$\dfrac{ds}{dt} = \lVert \mathbf{r}'(t) \rVert$$: the rate at which distance grows is the speed. If you can solve $$s = s(t)$$ for $$t$$, substituting $$t = t(s)$$ into $$\mathbf{r}$$ gives a curve parametrized by arc length, and then

$$\left\lVert \frac{d\mathbf{r}}{ds} \right\rVert = \left\lVert \mathbf{r}'(t)\,\frac{dt}{ds} \right\rVert = \frac{\lVert \mathbf{r}'(t) \rVert}{\lVert \mathbf{r}'(t) \rVert} = 1.$$

The curve is traced at speed $$1$$, so one unit of $$s$$ is one unit of length along the curve. Most of the time you cannot do this in closed form, because $$s(t)$$ is an integral you cannot evaluate. It is easy when the speed is constant.

> **Worked example.** Reparametrize the helix $$\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle \cos t, t/2, \sin t \rangle$$ with respect to arc length, starting at $$t = 0$$.
>
> The speed is $$\tfrac{\sqrt5}{2}$$, so $$s(t) = \int_0^t \tfrac{\sqrt5}{2}\,du = \tfrac{\sqrt5}{2}\,t$$, which gives $$t = \tfrac{2s}{\sqrt5}$$. Substitute:
>
> $$\mathbf{r}(s) = \left\langle \cos\frac{2s}{\sqrt5},\ \frac{s}{\sqrt5},\ \sin\frac{2s}{\sqrt5} \right\rangle.$$
>
> At $$s = 2\pi\sqrt5$$ this is the point where $$t = 4\pi$$, the end of the helix, matching the length above.

### The unit tangent vector

The velocity vector carries direction *and* speed. To keep only the direction, divide by the speed. For a smooth curve ($$\mathbf{r}' \ne \mathbf{0}$$), the **unit tangent vector** is

$$\mathbf{T}(t) = \frac{\mathbf{r}'(t)}{\lVert \mathbf{r}'(t) \rVert}.$$

It always has length $$1$$ and points in the direction of motion. If the curve is parametrized by arc length, $$\mathbf{T} = \dfrac{d\mathbf{r}}{ds}$$ and the velocity vector already is $$\mathbf{T}$$.

> **Worked example.** Find $$\mathbf{T}(t)$$ for $$\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle \cos t,\ \sin t,\ \cos 3t \rangle$$, and evaluate it at $$t = \pi/6$$.
>
> $$\mathbf{r}'(t) = \langle -\sin t,\ \cos t,\ -3\sin 3t \rangle$$ and $$\lVert \mathbf{r}'(t) \rVert = \sqrt{1 + 9\sin^2 3t}$$. This speed is **not** constant, so reparametrizing by arc length would be hopeless here, but $$\mathbf{T}$$ is easy:
>
> $$\mathbf{T}(t) = \frac{\langle -\sin t,\ \cos t,\ -3\sin 3t \rangle}{\sqrt{1 + 9\sin^2 3t}}.$$
>
> At $$t = \pi/6$$ the speed is $$\sqrt{10}$$, so $$\mathbf{T}(\pi/6) = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{10}}\left\langle -\tfrac12,\ \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2},\ -3 \right\rangle$$.

### Projectile motion

Launch an object at speed $$v_0$$, at angle $$\theta$$ above the horizontal, from the point $$(x_0, y_0)$$, and ignore air resistance. Gravity is the only acceleration, $$\mathbf{a}(t) = \langle 0, -g \rangle$$ (with $$g \approx 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$$), and integrating twice with the starting conditions gives

$$\mathbf{r}(t) = \left\langle (v_0\cos\theta)\,t + x_0,\ \ -\tfrac12 g t^2 + (v_0\sin\theta)\,t + y_0 \right\rangle, \qquad \mathbf{v}(t) = \langle v_0\cos\theta,\ -gt + v_0\sin\theta \rangle.$$

The horizontal velocity never changes; only the vertical part does.

> **Worked example (the peak).** Find the time and the height of the highest point.
>
> The highest point is where the vertical velocity is $$0$$: $$-gt + v_0\sin\theta = 0$$, so $$t_{\max} = \dfrac{v_0\sin\theta}{g}$$. Substituting into the height,
>
> $$y_{\max} = -\frac{v_0^2\sin^2\theta}{2g} + \frac{v_0^2\sin^2\theta}{g} + y_0 = \frac{v_0^2\sin^2\theta}{2g} + y_0.$$

> **Worked example (a wall).** A ball is thrown from a height of $$1.5$$ m at $$20$$ m/s, $$40^\circ$$ above the horizontal. A $$4$$ m wall stands $$36$$ m away horizontally. Does the ball clear it, and is it rising or falling when it gets there?
>
> The horizontal speed is $$20\cos 40^\circ \approx 15.32$$ m/s, so the ball reaches $$x = 36$$ at $$t = 36/15.32 \approx 2.35$$ s. At that time
>
> $$y = 1.5 + (20\sin 40^\circ)(2.35) - 4.9(2.35)^2 \approx 4.65 \text{ m},$$
>
> which clears the wall by about $$0.65$$ m. Its peak came at $$t_{\max} = \dfrac{20\sin 40^\circ}{9.8} \approx 1.31$$ s, well before $$2.35$$ s, so it is falling: $$v_y = 20\sin 40^\circ - 9.8(2.35) \approx -10.2$$ m/s.

### Practice & reflection

1. A particle runs once around the unit circle $$\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle \cos t, \sin t \rangle$$, $$0 \le t \le 2\pi$$. What do you get if you integrate the velocity vector $$\mathbf{r}'(t)$$ over that interval? What do you get if you integrate the speed? What does each number mean?
2. After reparametrizing a curve by arc length, what is $$\lVert d\mathbf{r}/ds \rVert$$, and why does that make $$s$$ a natural "ruler" for the curve?
3. Two particles go around the same circle, one at twice the speed of the other. Which of these are the same for both at a given point: the position, the velocity vector, the unit tangent vector, the length of one lap?
4. In projectile motion, which part of the velocity vector changes and which stays constant, and why? What does $$\mathbf{T}$$ look like at the top of the flight?
