How these work. Each walk-through takes one problem through Pólya’s four phases. You answer one step at a time and get feedback right away; when something isn’t right, read why and try again. Hints are there when you ask for them. Your place is saved in this browser, so you can stop and come back.

Some steps ask you to choose, or to arrange the lines of a proof. The walk-throughs end in full proofs, so the proof you bring to class should still be your own, in your own words.

Pólya walk-through · Problem 2.0.3

A rational between two rationals

Problem 2.0.3. Let \(a, b, c, d \in \mathbb{N}\) and find a rational number between \(\frac{a}{b}\) and \(\frac{c}{d}\).

Goal: Find a rational number strictly between two rationals, for every choice of \(a, b, c, d\) with \(\frac ab \ne \frac cd\), and show that it works. It makes the reading’s claim that the rationals are dense precise: between any two of them there is another, and the proof must produce one for every \(a, b, c, d\) with \(\frac ab \ne \frac cd\), not only for the pairs in a picture.

Pólya walk-through · Problem 2.0.5

Measuring with one stick

Problem 2.0.5. Theorem 2.0.4 (the reading leaves out \(b, d \ne 0\), which \(\frac ab\) and \(\frac cd\) need to mean anything, so it is added here): for integers \(a, b, c, d\) with \(b, d \ne 0\), there is a number \(\alpha \in \mathbb{Q}\) such that \(M\alpha = \frac ab\) and \(N\alpha = \frac cd\), where \(M\) and \(N\) are also integers. In its proof, \(\alpha = \frac{1}{bd}\), \(M = ad\), and \(N = cb\). Confirm that \(\alpha\), \(M\), and \(N\) as given in the proof satisfy the requirements of the theorem.

Goal: Check that a common unit \(\alpha\) and two whole-number counts really measure \(\frac ab\) and \(\frac cd\), and say which denominators must be nonzero. It is the proof of Theorem 2.0.4: one “stick” that measures both numbers. The reading then asks whether every length has this property.

Pólya walk-through · Problem 2.0.7

Irrational roots, and i

Problem 2.0.7. Show that each of the following numbers is irrational: \(\sqrt3\), \(\sqrt5\), \(\sqrt[3]{2}\), \(i = \sqrt{-1}\), and the square root of every positive integer which is not the square of an integer.

Goal: Prove that a number is not a ratio of integers, by assuming it is one and finding a contradiction. For the general square root, prove the contrapositive. It makes the reading’s holes real: \(\sqrt2\) is not the only missing point.
