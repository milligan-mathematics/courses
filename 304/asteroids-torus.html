What this is showing

The left panel is the video screen from Figure 1.1.2: a rectangle whose top edge has been identified, point by point, with its bottom edge, and whose left edge has been identified with its right. The ship never reaches a wall, because there are no walls to reach. It also never escapes, because the total area is fixed. The world is finite and yet has no boundary points — every point, including the ones sitting on a marked edge, has a small neighborhood around it that looks like an ordinary disk of the plane. Watch an asteroid cross an edge: the two half-pictures on opposite sides are one asteroid, and nothing happens to it as it passes.

The right panel performs the gluing described in the reading. Bend the rectangle to bring the top edge down onto the bottom edge and you get a cylinder; the left and right edges have become the two boundary circles of that cylinder. Bend the cylinder around and glue those two circles and you get the torus. The green circle on the donut is where the horizontal edges ended up, and the blue circle is where the vertical edges ended up. Every one of the four corners of the rectangle is the same single point, where those two circles cross.

It is worth knowing which part of the screen is which part of the donut. The top and bottom edges of the rectangle both became the green circle running around the outer rim, so the most visible ridge of the donut is exactly the line your ship crosses when it wraps vertically — watch the two panels together at that moment. The middle row of the screen, by contrast, ends up on the inner wall of the hole. Objects the donut is hiding are still drawn, as a faint outline on top of the surface, so that a ship round the back reads as behind rather than as gone.

One honest caveat. The donut is a picture of the topology, not of the geometry. On the surface of a real donut in space, the outer rim is stretched and the inner rim is pinched — distances there are not the distances the game uses. The game's physics run on the flat rectangle, where a ship at the inner rim and a ship at the outer rim move at exactly the same speed. That flat torus cannot be placed in three-dimensional space without distortion, which is why we can only ever draw it as a donut. Chapters 7 and 8 come back to this.

Try this while you play

  1. Park on a corner. Drift the ship into a corner of the rectangle and stop. You will see four pieces of a ship, one in each corner. On the donut there is exactly one ship. Four pictures, one point.
  2. Fire straight along an edge. A bullet fired exactly horizontally comes back to where it started. Fired at a slight angle, it does not — it returns to the same horizontal line at a shifted position, then again, and again. When does such a path ever close up, and when does it wander forever without repeating?
  3. Find yourself. Turn off the spin, drive the ship around the screen, and keep track of where it is on the donut. Crossing the left edge takes you the long way around the hole. Crossing the top edge takes you around the tube.
  4. Change one gluing. Suppose the left edge were glued to the right edge upside down, so that leaving at the top left brought you back at the bottom right. That is still a finite world with no boundary, but it is no longer a torus. What is it, and why can it not be built in three-dimensional space?

Why any of this matters

Section 1.1 is asking you to stop assuming that "finite" and "has an edge" are the same claim, and to stop assuming that a universe must sit inside some larger space in order to be curved or connected up in a strange way. The two-dimensional beings on this screen can do complete geometry and complete physics without ever suspecting there is a third dimension to bend into. They could, however, discover the gluing from the inside: fly in a straight line long enough and you arrive back home, facing the same way. That experiment is available to us too, and is the reason cosmic topology is a subject at all.
