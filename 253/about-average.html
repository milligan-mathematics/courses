Purr-fectly Average

Mean, median, mode and range, with cats. Three cats, a few clues, and one question: is there any set of mouse counts that fits, or is this case impossible?

Built on the NRICH problem About Average and its teachers' extension: explore which sets of conditions make puzzles possible and impossible.

The Cat Café Census

At the café, every cat keeps count of the mice it has caught. The numbers are whole numbers: 1, 2, 3, … (Want a cat that caught nothing? Tick the box to allow 0.) You get clues about the whole group and have to work out what the cats could have caught.

Mean: the fair sharePool all the mice and deal them out evenly. Mean × number of cats = total mice.
Median: the middle catLine the cats up from fewest mice to most. The median is the one in the middle (or halfway between the two in the middle).
Mode: the popular haulThe number of mice that shows up most often. Here it must be the only most-common number, and at least two cats must share it.
Range: best minus worstThe top hunter's count minus the lowest hunter's count.

    or

    Stack cats on each number: press + for every cat that caught that many mice. This works for any size litter, even 100.

      For teachers

      This page and the handout turn the extension from the NRICH teachers' resources for About Average into a lesson: students first solve the original puzzles, then investigate when a set of conditions can be met and when it can't, and finally write impossible conditions that look possible.

      Student handout (PDF) Student handout (Word, editable)

      A possible flow

      1. Warm up (10 min). Pairs work the three warm-up cases on the handout (they are Cases 1, 4 and 8 on the Solve the cases tab). Share strategies. Nearly everyone finds that "mean × number = total" unlocks the first step.
      2. Predict, then test (15 min). Handout Part 2 gives eight cases. Students mark each possible or impossible before testing, then find a set or say why none exists.
      3. Investigate (15 min). Handout Part 3 changes one clue at a time. The Possibility map lets students see the pattern they found by hand, all at once.
      4. Create (15 min). Students design one possible case and one impossible-but-tempting case, swap with a partner, and justify the verdict. The Design a case tab checks their work.

      Things worth saying out loud

      • The mode rule matters. Here the mode must be a single most-common value appearing at least twice. That is the rule the NRICH solution counts follow (two answers to Case 7, three to Case 8). With Allow tied modes ticked, "4 cats, mean 5, mode 9" flips from impossible to possible (1, 1, 9, 9). A good moment to ask why definitions matter.
      • Case 5 needs a 0. With positive whole numbers there is no answer. Allowing 0 gives five. Students often try for a long time before asking whether it can be done at all.
      • "I couldn't find one" is not a proof. Push students to explain why no set can work: a total that isn't whole, a range bigger than the total allows, a mode that needs two copies of a number too big to fit.
      • Impossible because of the combination. The lab names the smallest group of clues that clash. The "Surprise me" button builds cases where every clue is fine alone and every smaller group is fine too, but all of them together are impossible.
      • Unique answers are precious. Blue squares on the map have exactly one answer. What do they have in common?
      Key questions (from NRICH)
      • What distinguishes each average from the others?
      • Are multiple solutions possible?
      • Does a consistent approach work across all the puzzles?
      • Which measure should you prioritise first, and why?
      How the lab decides "impossible"

      Every set of whole numbers that could possibly work is checked, inside a limit that is proven large enough: with a mean, no number can exceed the total minus the smallest possible amounts for the others; with a range, nothing is more than the range above the lowest number. Without a mean or range a puzzle can have endlessly many answers, so the lab lists some and says so. Everything runs in your browser and nothing is stored or sent anywhere.
