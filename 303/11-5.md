# §11.5: Describing Lines and Planes

A point pins down a single location in space. That's useful, but most of the interesting objects in $$\mathbb{R}^3$$ aren't points — they're *lines* threading through space and *planes* slicing across it. The good news is that both come from the exact same recipe: **a point to anchor them, and a vector to orient them.** Once you internalize that recipe, this whole section is really just bookkeeping.

By the end of this section you should be able to:

1. Write the vector and parametric forms of a line, given a point and a direction vector (or two points).
2. Write the vector and scalar (general linear) equations of a plane, given a point and a normal vector (or three points).
3. Decide whether two lines intersect, are parallel, or are skew — and find where a line meets a plane.
4. Compute the distance from a point to a plane.

### Lines: a point plus a direction

Think of a line as a particle's path: it starts at some point $$P_0(x_0, y_0, z_0)$$ and moves forever in the direction of a vector $$\vec{v} = \langle a, b, c \rangle$$. If $$t$$ is how far (and which way) we've traveled, the position at "time" $$t$$ is just the starting point plus $$t$$ copies of the direction vector:

$$\vec{r}(t) = \vec{r_0} + t\vec{v} = \langle x_0, y_0, z_0 \rangle + t\langle a, b, c \rangle$$

Matching up components gives the **parametric equations** of the line:

$$x = x_0 + at, \qquad y = y_0 + bt, \qquad z = z_0 + ct$$

That's the entire definition. The only real skill is *finding* $$\vec{v}$$ when it isn't handed to you — and the most common way is to take two points on the line, $$A$$ and $$B$$, and use $$\vec{v} = \vec{AB} = B - A$$.

> **Example.** Find parametric equations for the line through $$A(4,2,-1)$$ and $$B(1,2,3)$$.
>
> The direction vector is $$\vec{v} = B - A = \langle -3, 0, 4 \rangle$$. Anchoring at $$A$$, the line is
> $$\vec{r}(t) = \langle 4, 2, -1 \rangle + t\langle -3, 0, 4 \rangle,$$
> so
> $$x(t) = 4 - 3t, \qquad y(t) = 2, \qquad z(t) = -1 + 4t.$$
> Notice $$y$$ never changes — the line is flat inside the plane $$y = 2$$, which is why it never crosses the $$xz$$-plane ($$y=0$$). Reading the direction vector's zero components tells you a lot about a line's behavior before you compute anything else.

### Planes: a point plus a normal

A plane needs a different kind of vector. Instead of pointing *along* the plane, we use a vector $$\vec{n} = \langle a, b, c \rangle$$ that points *straight out of* it — the **normal vector**. Here's the key idea: if $$P_0$$ is a known point in the plane and $$P(x,y,z)$$ is any other point in the plane, then the vector connecting them, $$\vec{r} - \vec{r_0}$$, lies flat inside the plane. Since $$\vec{n}$$ is perpendicular to *everything* in the plane, it must be perpendicular to that connecting vector too. Perpendicular vectors have a dot product of zero, so:

$$\vec{n} \cdot (\vec{r} - \vec{r_0}) = 0$$

That single dot-product equation is the vector form of the plane. Expand it and you get the **scalar equation**

$$a(x - x_0) + b(y - y_0) + c(z - z_0) = 0,$$

which simplifies to the familiar **general linear equation** $$ax + by + cz = d$$ (with $$d = ax_0+by_0+cz_0$$). This is why an equation like $$2x - 4y + z = -5$$ is secretly geometric: the coefficients $$\langle 2, -4, 1 \rangle$$ *are* the normal vector, sitting right there in plain sight.

So how do you get $$\vec{n}$$ when you're only given points in the plane, rather than a normal vector directly? Take any two vectors that lie in the plane and cross them — the cross product is perpendicular to both, hence perpendicular to the whole plane.

> **Example.** Find an equation for the plane through $$A(-1,2,2)$$, $$B(2,-1,1)$$, and $$C(2,2,1)$$.
>
> Build two vectors that live in the plane: $$\vec{AB} = \langle 3,-3,-1 \rangle$$ and $$\vec{AC} = \langle 3,0,-1 \rangle$$. Their cross product is normal to both, hence normal to the plane:
> $$\vec{n} = \vec{AB} \times \vec{AC} = \langle 3, 0, 9 \rangle,$$
> which we can simplify to $$\langle 1, 0, 3 \rangle$$ (any nonzero scalar multiple of a normal vector is still normal). Using point $$A$$:
> $$1(x+1) + 0(y-2) + 3(z-2) = 0 \implies x + 3z = 5.$$
> Three points, one cross product, one plane. That's the whole move.

### Where things meet

Once you can write down lines and planes, the natural next question is: where do they cross?

- **Line meets plane.** Substitute the line's parametric $$x(t), y(t), z(t)$$ into the plane's equation $$ax+by+cz=d$$ and solve for $$t$$. One value of $$t$$ (the usual case) gives a single intersection point; no solution means the line is parallel to the plane; every $$t$$ working means the line lies entirely inside the plane.
- **Line meets line.** Set the two vector equations' components equal (using different parameters, like $$u$$ and $$v$$, since the lines needn't reach the point at the same "time"). Solve two of the three resulting equations for $$u$$ and $$v$$, then check the third. If it holds, the lines cross; if not, they're **skew** — they miss each other in space without being parallel, which can only happen in three (or more) dimensions.
- **Plane meets plane.** Two non-parallel planes intersect in a line. Its direction vector is $$\vec{n_1} \times \vec{n_2}$$ (perpendicular to both normals means parallel to both planes, hence along their shared line), and you can find one point on that line by setting a convenient variable to zero and solving the remaining $$2\times2$$ system.

### How far away is it?

Finally, a genuinely useful measurement: the distance from a point $$Q$$ to a plane. Pick any point $$P_0$$ already in the plane and form the vector $$\vec{P_0Q}$$. Most of that vector is "sideways" — irrelevant to distance — and only the piece pointing along $$\vec{n}$$ actually measures how far $$Q$$ has strayed from the plane. That piece is exactly the scalar projection of $$\vec{P_0Q}$$ onto $$\vec{n}$$:

$$D = \frac{\lvert \vec{P_0Q} \cdot \vec{n} \rvert}{\lVert \vec{n} \rVert}$$

### Practice & reflection

1. Two planes have equations with proportional coefficients, e.g. $$2x-y+z=3$$ and $$4x-2y+2z=9$$. What does that tell you about their normal vectors, and what does it imply about the planes themselves?
2. If two lines are each parallel to a third line, must they be parallel to each other? What if instead they're each perpendicular to a common *plane*?
3. Can two skew lines ever lie in a pair of parallel planes (one line per plane)? Think about what a direction vector for both planes' shared normal would need to satisfy.
4. A plane's equation has no $$z$$ term at all, e.g. $$3x + y = 6$$. What does that tell you about the plane's normal vector, and what does the plane look like geometrically?
5. Use the demo to build a line and a plane that are parallel but don't intersect — how can you tell from the numbers alone (without dragging anything) that this will happen?
