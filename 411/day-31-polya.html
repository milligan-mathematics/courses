How these work. Each walk-through takes one problem through Pólya’s four phases. You answer one step at a time and get feedback right away; when something isn’t right, read why and try again. Hints are there when you ask for them. Your place is saved in this browser, so you can stop and come back.

Something new: the subsequence is built, not named. No step here asks for a formula. The proofs choose terms one at a time, and the steps ask you to check each choice: why a half of an interval must keep infinitely many terms, why the next index can be taken larger than the last, and why the limit has to land in \([a,b]\).

The walk-throughs end in a full proof, so the proof you bring to class should still be your own, in your own words.

Pólya walk-through · Problem 9.3.5

Subsequences keep the limit

Problem 9.3.5. Suppose \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty} x_n = c\). Prove that \(\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty} x_{n_k} = c\) for any subsequence \((x_{n_k})\) of \((x_n)\).

Goal: Find the \(K\) for the subsequence from the \(N\) for the whole sequence, and show that every later term of the subsequence lands past \(N\). Definition 9.3.2 gives subsequences, and Definition 6.1.1 gives the limit. The index fact \(n_k \ge k\) proved here is what Problem 9.3.8 needs.

Pólya walk-through · Problem 9.3.7

Keeping the crowded half

Problem 9.3.7. Turn the ideas of the outline into a formal proof of the Bolzano–Weierstrass Theorem (Theorem 9.3.6): if \(x_n \in [a,b]\) for every \(n\), then there are \(c \in [a,b]\) and a subsequence \((x_{n_k})\) with \(\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty} x_{n_k} = c\).

Goal: Build the nested intervals by halving and keeping a half that contains \(x_n\) for infinitely many \(n\). Let the NIP produce \(c\), then choose the subsequence so that each chosen term lies in the interval that shrinks to \(c\). The bisection is the one Day 29 used, now with a subsequence to pick. Problem 9.3.8 uses the theorem to finish the proof that a continuous function is bounded.

Pólya walk-through · Problem 9.3.8

Bounded all the way across

Problem 9.3.8. Use the Bolzano–Weierstrass Theorem (9.3.6) to complete the proof of Theorem 9.3.1: if \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\), then there is a positive number \(B\) with \(|f(x)| \le B\) for all \(x \in [a,b]\).

Goal: Assume \(f\) is not bounded. The failed bound gives points whose values grow. Bolzano–Weierstrass and continuity then turn a convergent part of those points into a contradiction. This is the step the reading needed before the EVT. It gives a bound, but not a point where \(|f|\) is largest: that is the Extreme Value Theorem, on Day 32.
