How these work. Each walk-through takes one problem through Pólya’s four phases. You answer one step at a time and get feedback right away; when something isn’t right, read why and try again. Hints are there when you ask for them. Your place is saved in this browser, so you can stop and come back.

Typed formulas. Some steps ask you to type a formula, such as a differential or a power of \(x\). Type dx for \(\mathrm{d}x\), ^ for powers, and * and / for products and fractions. Any formula equal to the answer is right, so you don’t have to simplify the way the model does.

The walk-throughs end in a full derivation, so the one you bring to class should still be your own, in your own words.

Pólya walk-through · Problem 3.1.5

Power rule one factor at a time

Problem 3.1.5. (a) Use Leibniz’s product rule \(\mathrm{d}(xv) = x\,\mathrm{d}v + v\,\mathrm{d}x\) to show that if \(n\) is a positive integer, then \(\mathrm{d}(x^n) = nx^{n-1}\,\mathrm{d}x\).
(b) Use Leibniz’s product rule to derive the quotient rule \(\mathrm{d}\left(\frac{v}{y}\right) = \frac{y\,\mathrm{d}v - v\,\mathrm{d}y}{y^2}\).
(c) Use the quotient rule to show that if \(n\) is a positive integer, then \(\mathrm{d}(x^{-n}) = -nx^{-n-1}\,\mathrm{d}x\).
(The labels (a), (b) and (c) are ours; the book prints the three parts as separate paragraphs.)

Goal: Derive the power rule for positive integers from the product rule, then get the quotient rule, and the rule for negative powers from that. It builds on the reading’s two examples, \(\mathrm{d}(x^2) = 2x\,\mathrm{d}x\) and \(\mathrm{d}(x^3) = 3x^2\,\mathrm{d}x\), and it leads to 3.1.6, which takes the power rule to fractional exponents.

Pólya walk-through · Problem 3.1.6

Clear the fraction, then differentiate

Problem 3.1.6. Let \(p\) and \(q\) be integers with \(q \neq 0\). Show that \(\mathrm{d}\bigl(x^{p/q}\bigr) = \frac{p}{q}\,x^{\frac{p}{q}-1}\,\mathrm{d}x\).

Goal: Clear the fraction in the exponent, use the integer power rule on the cleared equation, and solve for \(\mathrm{d}y\). It builds on 3.1.5, which gave the power rule for integer exponents, and it extends the rule to every rational exponent \(\frac pq\).
