Boxes Under the Surface

§14.1 — fill the region under a surface with boxes, watch the Riemann sum close in on the double integral, and slice the solid to see Fubini’s theorem at work.

Pick a surface z = f(x, y) over a rectangle. The blue boxes are a double Riemann sum: each has the area of one small rectangle as its base and the height of the surface at its sample point. Slide n to cut the rectangle into n × n pieces. The orange sheet is a cross-section of the solid at x = x0; its area is A(x0), and adding up those areas is the iterated integral. Drag inside the scene to orbit it.

Boxes below the xy-plane (where f is negative) are drawn in gray and count as negative volume. The vertical direction is stretched or squeezed to fit the picture, so compare heights with the readouts, not by eye.

Things to try

  1. Pick surface 1 and the top-right corner, with n = 2. Predict the sum from the four heights, then check the readout. Now double n to 4, 8, 16: by what factor does the error shrink each time? Make a guess before you slide. These are the numbers in the Riemann-sum example on the section page.
  2. On surface 1 with n = 2, switch to the midpoint rule. Predict whether the sum will be too big or too small, then look at the sign of “Sum − exact”. Which rule is closer for the same n?
  3. Pick surface 3, f = x − y2. Before you turn on any boxes, predict whether the double integral is positive or negative. Then raise n and watch the gray boxes.
  4. On surface 1 tick the slice and slide x0 from 0 to 2. Predict where the slice area is largest, then check. The values of A(x0) follow the inner integral 52/3 − 2x02 from the Fubini example on the section page; their total over x0 from 0 to 2 is the double integral.
  5. Pick surface 2, set n = 30, and compare the corner, midpoint, and bottom-left sums. How many boxes does it take before all three agree to two decimal places?
