# §13.1: Functions of Several Variables

Almost everything we have done so far has had one input. A function took a number $$x$$ and returned a number $$y$$, and we could draw the whole story on a flat page. Most real quantities do not cooperate that neatly. The temperature at a spot on a map depends on two numbers, the latitude and the longitude. The pressure in a room depends on where you stand and on the time. To describe situations like these we need functions that take an ordered pair (or triple) of inputs and return a single real number.

The hard part is seeing them. A surface $$z = f(x,y)$$ is awkward to sketch from its equation, and from a single angle it is easy to misread. The useful trick is to slice the surface with horizontal planes $$z = c$$ and look at the slices from above. Those slices are the **level curves**, and they are exactly the lines on a topographic map. Most of this section is learning to read those flat pictures, and the demo at the bottom does the slicing for you.

By the end of this section you should be able to:

1. Find the domain and range of a function of two variables, evaluate it at a point, and write a surface given by an equation as $$z = f(x,y)$$.
2. Decide whether a region in the plane is open or closed, and whether it is bounded or unbounded.
3. Describe the level curves of a surface, and sketch a contour map from them.
4. Read a contour map to say where a surface is steep and where it is flat.
5. Describe the level surfaces of a function of three variables.

### Functions of two variables: domain, range, and evaluation

A function of two variables, $$f(x,y)$$, is a rule that assigns exactly one real number to each ordered pair in its **domain** $$D$$. Think of it as a machine with two input slots. The domain is the set of pairs the machine accepts, and the **range** is the set of outputs it can produce. If a problem does not name a domain, use the **natural domain**: every pair for which the formula makes sense.

In $$z = f(x,y)$$, the variables $$x$$ and $$y$$ are the independent variables, and $$z$$ is the dependent variable. Each input pair gets exactly one output, so a single point can never produce two different heights.

The usual trouble spots are the same as in one variable. A square root needs a nonnegative radicand, a logarithm needs a positive argument, and a denominator cannot be zero. Each of those is a condition on $$(x,y)$$, not on the output.

> **Worked example.** Find the domain and range of $$f(x,y) = \sqrt{y^2 - x}$$, and evaluate $$f$$ at $$(1,2)$$.
>
> The radicand must be nonnegative: $$y^2 - x \ge 0$$, which is $$x \le y^2$$. So the domain is every point on or to the left of the parabola $$x = y^2$$, with the parabola included. The range is $$[0, \infty)$$: for any $$t \ge 0$$, the point $$(0, \sqrt{t})$$ gives $$f = \sqrt{t}$$. Before you evaluate, check that $$(1,2)$$ is in the domain: $$1 \le 2^2$$, so it is. Then $$f(1,2) = \sqrt{2^2 - 1} = \sqrt{3} \approx 1.732$$.

### Open, closed, and bounded regions

In one variable you already know open and closed intervals: $$(a,b)$$ leaves out its endpoints, and $$[a,b]$$ includes them. In the plane we use disks where we used intervals. A point of a region $$R$$ is an **interior point** if some open disk centered at it lies entirely inside $$R$$, and that disk can be as small as you need. A **boundary point** is one where every disk around it contains points inside $$R$$ and points outside $$R$$. A boundary point may or may not belong to $$R$$ itself.

A region is **open** if every one of its points is an interior point, which means it contains none of its boundary points. It is **closed** if it contains all of its boundary points. An open disk leaves out its bounding circle, just as an open interval leaves out its endpoints. A region is **bounded** if you can enclose all of it in some disk centered at the origin, and **unbounded** if you cannot.

> **Worked example.** The domain of $$f(x,y) = \sqrt{y^2 - x}$$ is $$D = \{(x,y) \mid x \le y^2\}$$. Is $$D$$ open or closed? Bounded or unbounded?
>
> The boundary of $$D$$ is the parabola $$x = y^2$$, and $$D$$ contains it. So $$D$$ contains all of its boundary points, and it is **closed**. It is also **unbounded**. Take any radius $$d > 0$$ and the point $$(-d^2 - 1,\ d)$$. It lies in $$D$$ because $$-d^2 - 1 \le d^2$$, and its distance from the origin is $$\sqrt{(d^2+1)^2 + d^2} > d$$. So no disk centered at the origin contains all of $$D$$, however large its radius.

### Writing a surface as a function of x and y

Many surfaces arrive as equations rather than as $$z = f(x,y)$$. To write one as a function of $$x$$ and $$y$$, solve for $$z$$. Solving usually gives two branches, one with a plus sign and one with a minus sign, so the problem has to tell you which branch you want. Once you have the function, find its domain the same way as before: it is wherever the expression under the square root is nonnegative.

> **Worked example.** Write the half of the sphere $$x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 9$$ that lies above the $$xy$$-plane as a function of $$x$$ and $$y$$. Give its domain and range.
>
> Solving for $$z$$ gives $$z = \pm\sqrt{9 - x^2 - y^2}$$. Above the $$xy$$-plane means $$z \ge 0$$, so we keep the plus sign: $$f(x,y) = \sqrt{9 - x^2 - y^2}$$. The domain needs $$x^2 + y^2 \le 9$$, which is the closed disk of radius $$3$$ centered at the origin. The largest value is at the center, $$f(0,0) = 3$$, and the smallest is $$0$$ on the rim, so the range is $$[0, 3]$$. As a spot check, $$f(1,2) = \sqrt{9 - 1 - 4} = 2$$, and indeed $$1^2 + 2^2 + 2^2 = 9$$.

### Graphs and contour maps

The graph of $$f$$ is the set of points $$(x, y, f(x,y))$$ in space, a surface sitting over the domain. A contour map records the shape of that surface in the flat plane. A **level curve** (or **contour**) of $$f$$ at height $$c$$ is the set of points in $$D$$ where $$f(x,y) = c$$. Geometrically it is the shadow on the $$xy$$-plane of the curve where the horizontal plane $$z = c$$ cuts the surface. A contour map collects level curves for several values of $$c$$, just like a topographic map.

Two facts make contour maps useful. First, level curves for different heights never cross, because a point $$(x,y)$$ has only one value $$f(x,y)$$ and so lies on at most one of them. Second, the spacing tells you about steepness. Lines packed close together mean the surface climbs fast there, and lines spread out mean it is gentle. If you have ever read a hiking map, you have already used this idea.

Read a contour map as a set of snapshots. Each line answers one question: where on the ground is the height exactly $$c$$?

The most common mistake is to read close contours as "flat." It is the reverse: close contours mean steep ground.

> **Worked example.** Identify the surface $$z = 9x^2 + 4y^2$$ and describe its contour map.
>
> This is an elliptic paraboloid, a bowl with its vertex at the origin. To find its level curves, set $$9x^2 + 4y^2 = c$$ and take the cases in turn. If $$c < 0$$ there are no solutions, since the left side is never negative. If $$c = 0$$ the only solution is the origin. If $$c > 0$$, divide by $$c$$:
>
> $$\frac{x^2}{c/9} + \frac{y^2}{c/4} = 1,$$
>
> an ellipse with semi-axes $$\sqrt{c}/3$$ along $$x$$ and $$\sqrt{c}/2$$ along $$y$$. Since $$\sqrt{c}/2 > \sqrt{c}/3$$, the ellipse is stretched in the $$y$$ direction. For $$c = 1, 4, 9$$ the semi-axes are $$\left(\tfrac13, \tfrac12\right)$$, $$\left(\tfrac23, 1\right)$$, and $$\left(1, \tfrac32\right)$$. Equal steps in height give ellipses that crowd together as they grow, so the bowl gets steeper the farther you move from its vertex.

### Functions of three variables and level surfaces

A function of three variables, $$w = f(x,y,z)$$, has a graph in four dimensions, which we cannot draw. We do not need to. The same slicing idea works one dimension up. A **level surface** is the set of points where $$f(x,y,z) = c$$. Level surfaces are ordinary surfaces in space, and they are the three-dimensional version of contours. Think of the surfaces of constant temperature in a room: each one is a level surface of the temperature function, and $$x$$, $$y$$, and $$z$$ are the independent variables. It is the same move as before, one dimension higher.

> **Worked example.** Describe the level surfaces of $$f(x,y,z) = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}$$.
>
> The function measures the distance from the origin. Setting $$f = c$$ requires $$c \ge 0$$, and squaring gives $$x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = c^2$$. When $$c = 0$$ the level surface is the single point $$(0,0,0)$$. When $$c > 0$$ it is a sphere of radius $$c$$ centered at the origin. For $$c = 1, 2, 4$$ you get spheres of radius $$1$$, $$2$$, and $$4$$, nested like the layers of an onion.

### Practice & reflection

Try these on paper before you open the demo. They are about ideas, not computation.

1. Two level curves of the same function can never cross. Explain why, using what it means for something to be a function.
2. A contour map has its lines spaced exactly the same distance apart everywhere. What does that tell you about how steep the surface is, and what kind of surface could it be?
3. Suppose $$f = \sqrt{g(x,y)}$$. Explain why the edge of the domain of $$f$$ must lie on the level curve $$f = 0$$. Then check that claim for $$f(x,y) = \sqrt{y^2 - x}$$.
4. You are given a function of four variables, $$w = h(x,y,z,u)$$. What would its level sets be called, and what kind of object would each one be in four-dimensional space?

Play with the demo below to see all of this at once. Pick a surface and slide the height $$c$$. The green plane $$z = c$$ cuts the 3D graph, the cut appears in orange on the surface, and the same curve lights up as a level curve on the contour map. The readout names its shape. Before you move the slider, try to predict the shape; the "Things to try" list walks through a few cases, and the "Hide the answer" box lets you make your prediction before you see the answer.
