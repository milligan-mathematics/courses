Two Paths, One Field

§15.3 — draw two paths from A to B through a vector field, compare the work along each, and compare both with the change in the potential function.

Both paths run from A to B. Path 1 is the solid blue curve and path 2 is the dashed blue curve; drag any handle (or type its position). The readouts give the work ∫F·dr along each path. If the field is conservative, F = ∇f and the Fundamental Theorem says both works equal f(B) − f(A); the bars compare all three numbers. If the field is not conservative there is no potential, and the two works are free to disagree. Their difference is the work around the loop made by path 1 followed by path 2 backward.

Each path is a cubic Bézier curve, and every integral is computed numerically along it (Gauss–Legendre on 8 pieces), so the readouts are accurate to the digits shown. The picture shows −4 ≤ x, y ≤ 4.

Things to try

  1. Pick the field ⟨2x cos y, −x² sin y⟩ (the last example on the section page) and the pair “Same endpoints as the page example”. Predict the work on each path and on the f(B) − f(A) bar (the page says −1). Then drag any handle of either path somewhere wild. What changes and what doesn’t?
  2. Pick the Gaussian-bump gradient field. Make path 2 pass through the middle of the bump and path 1 go all the way around it. Predict whether the works agree before you read them.
  3. Pick the field ⟨y², x²⟩ (the not-conservative example on the page) and the first pair of paths. Predict which path will have more work, then check. Is there any way to place the handles so the two works agree? (Try it, and notice what that does and doesn’t prove.)
  4. Choose “A loop: A = B” and a conservative field. Predict the work on a closed path, then drag the handles to change the loop’s shape and size. Now switch to the swirl ⟨−y, x⟩ and predict again.
  5. With the swirl and the pair “Two arcs from A to B”, predict the sign of “path 1 − path 2” (which way does the loop go around?). Then swap by dragging the handles of path 1 to the other side of path 2.
