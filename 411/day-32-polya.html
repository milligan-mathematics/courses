How these work. Each walk-through takes one problem through Pólya’s four phases. You answer one step at a time and get feedback right away; when something isn’t right, read why and try again. Hints are there when you ask for them. Your place is saved in this browser, so you can stop and come back.

Something new: the supremum is built. Today the least upper bound comes out of the Nested Interval Property, and the Extreme Value Theorem comes out of the least upper bound. Some steps ask for a number, such as a midpoint or a term; the page checks it.

The walk-throughs end in a full proof, so the proof you bring to class should still be your own, in your own words.

Pólya walk-through · Problem 9.4.4

Halving toward a least upper bound

Problem 9.4.4. Complete the above ideas to provide a formal proof of Theorem 9.4.3.
Theorem 9.4.3 (The Least Upper Bound Property). Let \(S\) be a non-empty subset of \(\mathbb{R}\) which is bounded above. Then \(S\) has a supremum.

Goal: Build a number \(c\) from \(S\) by halving, with the NIP giving \(c\). Then show \(c\) is the least upper bound: it bounds \(S\), and nothing smaller does. This is the bisection from Day 29 with a fifth property, and here property 5 is about the set \(S\), not about a square. The supremum this gives is the one the next problems use.

Pólya walk-through · Problem 9.4.6

A bounded increasing sequence settles

Problem 9.4.6. Prove Corollary 9.4.5 using the supremum.
Corollary 9.4.5. Let \((x_n)\) be a bounded, increasing sequence of real numbers. That is, \(x_1 \le x_2 \le x_3 \le \cdots\). Then \((x_n)\) converges to some real number \(c\).

Goal: Find the limit with the supremum: let \(c\) be the least upper bound of the terms, and show that for every \(\varepsilon > 0\) some term lands above \(c - \varepsilon\). Monotonicity keeps every later term there. This corollary is the tool for Problem 9.4.7. Its proof uses the LUBP from Problem 9.4.4 once, and then monotonicity does the rest.

Pólya walk-through · Problem 9.4.7

A nested radical that settles

Problem 9.4.7. Consider the sequence \((x_n)\) defined by \(x_1 = \sqrt{2}\) and \(x_{n+1} = \sqrt{2 + x_n}\). This makes precise the expression \(\sqrt{2+\sqrt{2+\sqrt{2+\cdots}}}\). a. Use induction to show that \(x_n < 2\) for \(n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots\). b. Use the result from part (a) to show that \(x_n < x_{n+1}\) for \(n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots\). c. From Corollary 9.4.5, \((x_n)\) converges to some number \(c\). Use the fact that \((x_{n+1})\) converges to \(c\) as well to compute what \(c\) must be.

Goal: Make the nested radical precise, show the terms settle down by the corollary, and then find the limit from the equation the limit must satisfy. Part (a) gives a bound, part (b) gives monotonicity, and part (c) uses the corollary and a limit equation. Keep track of which tool each part needs.

Pólya walk-through · Problem 9.4.9

The maximum that must be reached

Problem 9.4.9. Formalize the above ideas into a proof of Theorem 9.4.8.
Theorem 9.4.8 (Extreme Value Theorem). Suppose \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\). Then there exist \(c, d \in [a,b]\) such that \(f(d) \le f(x) \le f(c)\), for all \(x \in [a,b]\).

Goal: Show that the largest value of \(f\) is attained: find a point \(c\) in \([a,b]\) with \(f(c) = s\), where \(s\) is the supremum of the values. Then say how the minimum follows. This is where the chapter comes together: Theorem 9.3.1, the LUBP, Bolzano–Weierstrass, continuity and the Squeeze Theorem, in that order. Each step uses one result from before.

Pólya walk-through · Problem 9.4.10

Bolzano–Weierstrass proves the NIP

Problem 9.4.10. Use the Bolzano–Weierstrass Theorem to prove the NIP. That is, assume that the Bolzano–Weierstrass Theorem holds, and suppose we have two sequences of real numbers, \((x_n)\) and \((y_n)\), satisfying: (1) \(x_1 \le x_2 \le x_3 \le \cdots\); (2) \(y_1 \ge y_2 \ge y_3 \ge \cdots\); (3) \(x_n \le y_n\) for all \(n\); (4) \(\lim_{n \to \infty}(y_n - x_n) = 0\). Prove that there is a real number \(c\) such that \(x_n \le c \le y_n\) for all \(n\).

Goal: Use Bolzano–Weierstrass to get a subsequence of \((x_n)\) with a limit \(c\), and show that \(c\) lies between the two sequences at every index. The gap \(y_n - x_n \to 0\) is what makes it work. Day 31 proved Bolzano–Weierstrass from the NIP, and Problem 9.4.4 went from the NIP to the LUBP. This problem goes from Bolzano–Weierstrass back to the NIP, so the chain of implications is starting to close.
