How these work. Each walk-through takes one problem through Pólya’s four phases. You answer one step at a time and get feedback right away; when something isn’t right, read why and try again. Hints are there when you ask for them. Your place is saved in this browser, so you can stop and come back.

Something new: you type formulas. Some steps ask you for a formula in \(n\). Type n for the index, pi for \(\pi\), and ^ for powers. The page checks your formula at several values of \(n\), so any formula that gives the right values is right, even when it’s written another way. If yours is off, you’ll see the comment for the slip it matches.

The walk-throughs end in full arguments, so the proof you bring to class should still be your own, in your own words.

Pólya walk-through · Problem 5.1.2

Which sign does cooling need?

Problem 5.1.2. In the reading, \(u(x,t) = X(x)T(t)\) solves the heat equation \(\rho^2\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} = \frac{\partial u}{\partial t}\) with \(u(0,t) = u(1,t) = 0\), and separation of variables gives \(T^{\prime} = \rho^2 k T\).
Show that \(T = Ce^{\rho^2 k t}\) satisfies the equation \(T^{\prime} = \rho^2 k T\), where \(C\) and \(\rho\) are arbitrary constants. Use the physics of the problem to show that if \(u\) is not constantly zero, then \(k < 0\).

Goal: Check a formula by differentiating it, then use the physics of the problem to decide the sign of \(k\). It builds on the separation of variables in the reading, and leads to 5.1.3. The Look back here shows that the boundary conditions give the same sign.

Pólya walk-through · Problem 5.1.3

Which frequencies fit the ends?

Problem 5.1.3. In the reading, \(k = -p^2\) (from 5.1.2), so \(X^{\prime\prime} = -p^2 X\), and \(u(x,t) = X(x)T(t)\).
Show that \(X = A\sin(px) + B\cos(px)\) satisfies the equation \(X^{\prime\prime} = -p^2 X\), where \(A\) and \(B\) are arbitrary constants. Use the boundary conditions \(u(0,t) = u(1,t) = 0\), for all \(t \ge 0\), to show that \(B = 0\) and \(A\sin p = 0\). Conclude that if \(u\) is not constantly zero, then \(p = n\pi\), where \(n\) is any integer.

Goal: Find the functions that solve the equation in \(x\), then let the two ends decide which values of \(p\) are allowed. It builds on 5.1.2, which gave \(k < 0\), and leads to 5.1.5, where the sines \(\sin(n\pi x)\) found here are the ones a sine series is built from.

Pólya walk-through · Problem 5.1.4

Adding solutions to the heat equation

Problem 5.1.4. Show that if \(u_1\) and \(u_2\) satisfy the equations \(\rho^2\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} = \frac{\partial u}{\partial t}\) and \(u(0,t) = u(1,t) = 0\), for all \(t \ge 0\), then \(u = A_1u_1 + A_2u_2\) satisfies these as well, where \(A_1\) and \(A_2\) are arbitrary constants.

Goal: Check that a combination of two solutions is again a solution, and see which property of the heat equation makes that work. It builds on the solutions \(e^{-\rho^2\pi^2 t}\sin(\pi x)\) from 5.1.3. It is the superposition the reading uses for an infinite sum.

Pólya walk-through · Problem 5.1.5

Why the sines don’t interfere

Problem 5.1.5. Let \(n\) and \(m\) be positive integers. Show that
\[\int_0^1 \sin(n\pi x)\sin(m\pi x)\,dx = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } n \ne m, \\ \tfrac12 & \text{if } n = m. \end{cases}\]

Goal: Turn the product of two sines into cosines with a product-to-sum identity, then integrate, one case at a time. The reading uses this integral to find the coefficients of a sine series. The last steps of this walk-through compute one of them.
