# §14.2: Applications of Double Integrals

Once you can compute a double integral, you can ask what it is *for*. Four questions come up again and again in engineering: how much area does a region have, what is the average value of a quantity spread over it, where is its balance point, and how hard is it to spin? Each one is a double integral, and each reduces to the same skill: set up the limits, then integrate.

These are the quantities behind the design of beams, flywheels, and sheet-metal parts. A plate that balances on a pencil tip, or a wheel that is easy or hard to speed up, is described by the formulas in this section.

By the end of this section you should be able to:

1. Find the area of a region with a double integral.
2. Find the average value of a function over a region.
3. Find the mass, first moments, and center of mass of a plate with density $$\rho(x,y)$$.
4. Find the second moments $$I_x$$, $$I_y$$, $$I_0$$ and the radii of gyration.
5. Use symmetry and superposition to shorten a calculation.

### Area and average value

Taking $$f(x,y) = 1$$ turns the double integral into the area of the base: $$A = \iint_R dA$$. The **average value** of a continuous $$f$$ over $$R$$ is the volume under the surface divided by the area of the base, the same idea as "level the mountain into a flat plateau":

$$\bar f = \frac{1}{\operatorname{Area}(R)} \iint_R f(x,y)\,dA.$$

> **Worked example.** Find the area of the region $$R$$ between $$y = 3x^2$$ and $$y = 2 + x^2$$, and the average value of $$f(x,y) = 5y$$ over $$R$$.
>
> The curves meet at $$x = \pm 1$$, with $$y = 2 + x^2$$ on top, so $$R$$ is Type I and
>
> $$A = \int_{-1}^{1} \int_{3x^2}^{2+x^2} dy\,dx = \int_{-1}^{1} (2 - 2x^2)\,dx = \frac{8}{3}.$$
>
> The integral of $$5y$$ over this region is $$\frac{56}{3}$$ (the same integral as the Type I example in §14.1), so
>
> $$\bar f = \frac{1}{8/3} \cdot \frac{56}{3} = 7.$$
>
> A sanity check: $$y$$ runs from $$0$$ to $$3$$ on $$R$$, so $$5y$$ runs from $$0$$ to $$15$$, and an average of $$7$$ is believable.

### Mass, first moments, and center of mass

Think of $$R$$ as a thin plate whose density (mass per unit area) at $$(x,y)$$ is $$\rho(x,y)$$. Adding up density times area gives:

$$M = \iint_R \rho\,dA, \qquad M_x = \iint_R y\,\rho\,dA, \qquad M_y = \iint_R x\,\rho\,dA.$$

$$M_x$$ is the **moment about the $$x$$-axis** (the lever arm of a bit of mass is its $$y$$-coordinate), and $$M_y$$ is the moment about the $$y$$-axis (lever arm $$x$$). The **center of mass** is the balance point,

$$\bar x = \frac{M_y}{M}, \qquad \bar y = \frac{M_x}{M}.$$

The common mistake: it is $$\bar x = M_y / M$$, the moment about the *$$y$$-axis* over the mass, not $$M_x / M$$. Remember that $$M_y$$ multiplies by $$x$$.

> **Worked example.** Find the center of mass of the triangular plate bounded by $$y = 0$$, $$x = 1$$, and $$y = x$$ if the density is $$\rho(x,y) = x^2 + y$$.
>
> The plate is Type I: $$0 \le x \le 1$$, $$0 \le y \le x$$.
>
> $$M = \int_0^1 \int_0^x (x^2 + y)\,dy\,dx = \int_0^1 \left(x^3 + \tfrac12 x^2\right) dx = \frac{5}{12},$$
>
> $$M_x = \int_0^1 \int_0^x y(x^2 + y)\,dy\,dx = \int_0^1 \left(\tfrac12 x^4 + \tfrac13 x^3\right) dx = \frac{11}{60},$$
>
> $$M_y = \int_0^1 \int_0^x x(x^2 + y)\,dy\,dx = \int_0^1 \left(x^4 + \tfrac12 x^3\right) dx = \frac{13}{40}.$$
>
> So $$\bar x = \dfrac{13/40}{5/12} = \dfrac{39}{50}$$ and $$\bar y = \dfrac{11/60}{5/12} = \dfrac{11}{25}$$. The balance point $$(0.78, 0.44)$$ is inside the triangle, and it sits toward the right, where the plate is both wider and denser.

### Second moments and radii of gyration

A first moment weights mass by distance; a **second moment** weights it by distance *squared*, which measures how hard the plate is to spin:

$$I_x = \iint_R y^2\rho\,dA, \qquad I_y = \iint_R x^2\rho\,dA, \qquad I_0 = \iint_R (x^2 + y^2)\rho\,dA = I_x + I_y.$$

$$I_x$$ is the resistance to turning about the $$x$$-axis, and $$I_0$$ (the *polar* moment) is the resistance to turning about the origin, the axis perpendicular to the plate. The **radii of gyration** answer: how far from the axis would the whole mass have to sit, as a single point, to give the same moment?

$$r_x = \sqrt{I_x / M}, \qquad r_y = \sqrt{I_y / M}, \qquad r_0 = \sqrt{I_0 / M}.$$

> **Worked example.** A disk of radius $$a$$ and constant density $$\rho$$ is centered at the origin. Find $$M$$, $$I_x$$, $$I_y$$, $$I_0$$ and the radii of gyration.
>
> The mass is $$M = \rho \cdot \pi a^2$$. A disk is much easier in polar coordinates (coming in §14.3), where $$x^2 + y^2 = r^2$$ and $$dA = r\,dr\,d\theta$$:
>
> $$I_0 = \rho \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^a r^2 \cdot r\,dr\,d\theta = \rho \cdot 2\pi \cdot \frac{a^4}{4} = \frac{\pi \rho a^4}{2}.$$
>
> By symmetry $$I_x = I_y$$, and $$I_0 = I_x + I_y$$, so $$I_x = I_y = \dfrac{\pi \rho a^4}{4}$$. (In Cartesian coordinates the same $$I_x$$ needs the substitution $$x = a\sin\theta$$ and gives the same answer.) The radii of gyration are
>
> $$r_x = r_y = \sqrt{\frac{\pi\rho a^4/4}{\pi\rho a^2}} = \frac{a}{2}, \qquad r_0 = \sqrt{\frac{\pi\rho a^4/2}{\pi\rho a^2}} = \frac{a}{\sqrt2}.$$

### Superposition

Integrals add, so a plate with a hole is the full plate minus the hole. This is the **principle of superposition**.

> **Worked example.** An annulus has inner radius $$a_1$$, outer radius $$a_2$$, constant density $$\rho$$ and mass $$M$$. Find $$I_0$$.
>
> Take the disk of radius $$a_2$$ and subtract the disk of radius $$a_1$$:
>
> $$I_0 = \frac{\pi\rho a_2^4}{2} - \frac{\pi\rho a_1^4}{2} = \frac{\pi\rho}{2}(a_2^2 - a_1^2)(a_2^2 + a_1^2).$$
>
> Since $$M = \pi\rho(a_2^2 - a_1^2)$$, this is
>
> $$I_0 = \tfrac12 M (a_1^2 + a_2^2).$$

### Practice & reflection

1. For which regions and densities would polar coordinates make $$M$$, the moments, or the radii of gyration much easier? When would you stay in Cartesian coordinates?
2. If you compute $$M_x = 0$$ for a plate, what does it tell you about $$\bar y$$? What symmetry would make that happen?
3. How does a nonconstant density $$\rho(x,y)$$ change the formulas for mass and moments compared with constant density? Why is that physically sensible?
4. For the disk, $$r_0 = a/\sqrt2$$ is larger than $$r_x = a/2$$. Why does that make sense?
5. Can the center of mass of an object lie *outside* the object? Give an example.
