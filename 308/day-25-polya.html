How these work. Each walk-through takes one exercise through Pólya’s four phases. You answer one step at a time and get feedback right away; when something isn’t right, read why and try again. Hints are there when you ask for them. Your place is saved in this browser, so you can stop and come back.

The walk-throughs end in full solutions, so the work you present in class should still be your own, in your own words.

Pólya walk-through · Exercise 12.4.4

Naming the symmetry group of a figure

Exercise 12.4.4. Determine the symmetry group of each of the figures below.
(a) A rectangle containing an oval in the lower left and a solid black disk in the upper right.
(b) A square drawn with its two diagonals, and inside it the square whose vertices are the midpoints of its sides.
(c) Three circles of the same radius, with centers at the corners of an equilateral triangle, overlapping in the middle.
(The figures are in the exercise in the online book, Exercise 12.4.4.)

Goal: Find, for each figure, which turns and flips fit it, and name the group with Theorem 12.2.4. The pictures that look most symmetrical are not always the ones that are. It uses the dihedral and cyclic groups from Chapters 3 and 4, isometries and symmetry groups from Section 12.2, and Theorem 12.2.4 (the only finite symmetry groups in the plane are \(\mathbb Z_n\) and \(D_n\)).

Pólya walk-through · Exercise 12.4.7

Two bases, one lattice

Exercise 12.4.7. Prove that \(\{(2, 1), (1, 1)\}\) and \(\{(12, 5), (7, 3)\}\) are bases for the same lattice.

Goal: Turn “same lattice” into two statements you can check by solving small systems, and see why both are needed. It uses the definition of a lattice and its bases from Section 12.2, and the matrix \(U\) with \(\det U = \pm 1\) that relates two bases. The linear systems are \(2 \times 2\) systems from linear algebra.

Pólya walk-through · Exercise 12.4.18

Rotations of a dodecahedron

Exercise 12.4.18. Find the rotation group of a dodecahedron.

Goal: Count the rotations by where they send a face, check the count another way, and then recognize the group you have counted. It uses symmetry groups from Section 12.2, orders of elements from Chapter 4, and the alternating group \(A_5\) from Chapter 5. The answer is the first time a geometric group turns out to be a group of permutations you already know.
