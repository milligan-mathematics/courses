Paper first, then check in. This page goes with the Day 18 handout, one card per Part. Do Part A on paper, then open the Part A card and tell it what you got: numbers, vectors, matrices. If something is off, the page tries to name the slip, and hints are there when you want them (each one a little more specific). Then on to Part B, and so on.

The paper is what you turn in. You’ll see answers here as you check your work. That’s on purpose: they’re for checking, not copying. Your handout, with your own reasoning and both figures, is what goes on Canvas, along with the two Skeptic answers.

Typing tips: a vector is a column of boxes, one entry per box. Decimals like 2.5, fractions like 5/2 and roots like sqrt(2)/2 all work. Eigenvectors can be any length and either sign unless a step asks for a unit vector: the page knows that \((1, 1)\) and \((-3, -3)\) point along the same line. Walk-through · Part A

A matrix acting on a circle

Part A. Do Part A on paper first, then check in here.
Take the matrix \(A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}\). The figure shows eight points \(P_0,\dots,P_7\) on the unit circle, every \(45^\circ\). Use \(0.71\) for \(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\), so for example \(P_1=(0.71,\,0.71)\).
1. Compute \(A\mathbf p\) for \(P_0\), \(P_1\), \(P_2\), and \(P_3\).
2. \(P_4,\dots,P_7\) are the negatives of \(P_0,\dots,P_3\). Explain why that means you already know \(A\mathbf p\) for them.
3. Plot all eight images \(A\mathbf p\) on the figure and sketch a smooth curve through them. What shape does the circle become?
4. For most points, \(A\mathbf p\) points in a new direction: the matrix turns the vector as well as stretching it. Which of the eight points come out on the same line through the origin as they went in? By what factor is each of those stretched?
A nonzero vector \(\mathbf v\) that \(A\) only stretches, \(A\mathbf v=\lambda\mathbf v\), is an eigenvector of \(A\), and the stretch factor \(\lambda\) (“lambda”) is its eigenvalue.
Skeptic. “Every \(2\times2\) matrix has some direction that it only stretches.” Test this on \(R=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\). Compute \(R\mathbf p\) for \(P_0\) and \(P_1\) and describe what \(R\) does to every vector. Is the claim true?

Goal: Check your images of the circle, and find the two directions that \(A\) only stretches. Day 4 showed car features that move together. Unit 2 looks for the few directions that describe such data, and those directions are eigenvectors. Here you meet them in a picture; Part B finds them with algebra.

Walk-through · Part B

Stretch directions by algebra

Part B. Do Part B on paper first, then check in here.
Reading the directions off a picture won’t work in five dimensions. Here is the algebra. \(A\mathbf v=\lambda\mathbf v\) means \((A-\lambda I)\mathbf v=\mathbf 0\) for a nonzero \(\mathbf v\). A square matrix sends a nonzero vector to \(\mathbf 0\) exactly when its determinant is 0 (MML §4.1), so the eigenvalues are the solutions of \(\det(A-\lambda I)=0\).
1. For \(A\) from Part A, write out \(\det\begin{pmatrix}2-\lambda&1\\1&2-\lambda\end{pmatrix}\) and solve for \(\lambda\). For each eigenvalue, find an eigenvector by solving \((A-\lambda I)\mathbf v=\mathbf 0\). Do they match what you found in Part A?
2. Check two shortcuts: the eigenvalues add up to the trace of \(A\) (the sum of its diagonal) and multiply to \(\det A\).
3. Now try a matrix that is not symmetric: \(C=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\). Find its two eigenvalues and an eigenvector for each. What is the angle between the two eigenvector lines? What was the angle between \(A\)’s?
A fact worth remembering (the spectral theorem, MML Theorem 4.15): if a matrix is symmetric, its eigenvalues are real and it has eigenvectors that are perpendicular to each other. Covariance matrices, coming up next, are always symmetric.

Goal: Find eigenvalues and eigenvectors with \(\det(A - \lambda I) = 0\), and see what symmetry buys you. Part A found the stretch directions by looking. Here one equation finds them in any dimension, and the spectral theorem promises that a symmetric matrix’s directions are perpendicular. Part C’s covariance matrix is symmetric.

Walk-through · Part C

The covariance matrix of a tilted cloud

Part C. Do Part C on paper first, then check in here.
Four data points, each with two features: A \((5, 6)\), B \((1, 4)\), C \((4, 7)\), D \((2, 3)\) (\(x_1\) first, then \(x_2\)).
1. On the figure, draw by eye the line through the middle of the cloud along which the points are most spread out.
2. Find the mean point \((\bar x_1,\bar x_2)\), then center the data: subtract the mean from every point. Write the four centered points.
For centered data with \(N\) points, the variance of a feature is the average of its squares, and the covariance of two features is the average of their products (MML §6.4; we divide by \(N\), as on Day 4): \(\operatorname{Var}(x_1)=\frac1N\sum_{n} x_{1n}^2\), \(\operatorname{Cov}(x_1,x_2)=\frac1N\sum_{n} x_{1n}\,x_{2n}\), and \(S=\begin{pmatrix}\operatorname{Var}(x_1)&\operatorname{Cov}(x_1,x_2)\\ \operatorname{Cov}(x_2,x_1)&\operatorname{Var}(x_2)\end{pmatrix}\).
3. Compute \(\operatorname{Var}(x_1)\), \(\operatorname{Var}(x_2)\), and \(\operatorname{Cov}(x_1,x_2)\) from your centered points, and write the covariance matrix \(S\). Why must \(S\) be symmetric? What does the sign of the covariance say about the cloud?
4. Find the eigenvalues of \(S\) and a unit eigenvector for each (length 1: divide by the length). Call the one with the larger eigenvalue \(\mathbf b_1\) and the other \(\mathbf b_2\). Draw both on the figure, starting at the mean. Compare \(\mathbf b_1\) with your line from Step 1.
5. Variance along a direction. Projecting a centered point \(\mathbf x_n\) onto a unit vector \(\mathbf u\) gives one number, \(z_n=\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf x_n\) (Day 9). Compute the four \(z_n\) for \(\mathbf u=\mathbf b_1\), and their variance \(\frac1N\sum z_n^2\). Do the same for \(\mathbf u=\mathbf b_2\), and for \(\mathbf u=(1,0)\). Compare each variance with the numbers in \(S\) and with the eigenvalues.

Goal: Build the covariance matrix of a small cloud, and see that its top eigenvector points along the cloud, with the variance in that direction as its eigenvalue. Parts A and B were about any symmetric matrix. Here the matrix comes from data, and its stretch directions turn out to be the cloud’s own directions. Part D shows why no direction beats \(\mathbf b_1\).

Walk-through · Part D

The best direction

Part D. Do Part D on paper first, then check in here.
For any unit vector \(\mathbf u\), the variance of the projections works out to \(\mathbf u^{T}S\,\mathbf u\) (you can check this against your three answers in C5). Every unit vector in the plane is \(\mathbf u=(\cos\theta,\ \sin\theta)\) for some angle \(\theta\).
1. Multiply out \(\mathbf u^{T}S\,\mathbf u\) for your \(S\) and simplify. (Use \(\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta=1\) and \(2\sin\theta\cos\theta=\sin2\theta\). You should get \(2.5+2\sin2\theta\).)
2. At what angle is the variance biggest? Smallest? What are those two values, and where have you seen them before?
3. Finish the sentence: “The direction in which the data varies most is ______ of the covariance matrix, and the variance in that direction is ______.”
That sentence is principal component analysis (PCA). On Wednesday the computer does Parts C and D for 200 points, and on Friday for five car features at once.
Skeptic. “The covariance here is positive and the best direction came out at \(45^\circ\). So a positive covariance always means the main direction is \(45^\circ\).” Test it on \(S^\prime=\begin{pmatrix}4&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}\): compute \(\mathbf u^{T}S^\prime\mathbf u\) for \(\theta=0^\circ\) and for \(\theta=45^\circ\). Which is bigger? What made \(45^\circ\) special for our \(S\)?

Goal: Find the direction of most variance for every angle at once, and see that it is the top eigenvector, with its eigenvalue as the variance: PCA. C5 tried three directions. Here one formula covers them all, and its peak lands on \(\mathbf b_1\). Wednesday’s lab does the same with NumPy for 200 points.
