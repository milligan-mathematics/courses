# §13.3: Partial Derivatives

In Calc I, a derivative measured how fast a function changes when one input moves. Most functions that matter in engineering have several inputs. The temperature at a point on a plate depends on where you stand in two directions, and the height of a hillside depends on how far east and how far north you are. One derivative can't describe that. You need to know how steep the hill is if you walk due east, and separately how steep it is if you walk due north.

That's the whole idea of a partial derivative. You pick one input, freeze every other input, and differentiate with respect to the one you kept moving. Everything from Calc I still works, because once the other inputs are frozen you are doing a one-variable derivative. What's new is deciding what to hold fixed, and noticing that the answer depends on which direction you choose.

By the end of this section you should be able to:

1. Compute first-order partial derivatives of functions of two or three variables by treating the other variables as constants.
2. Interpret $$f_x(a,b)$$ and $$f_y(a,b)$$ as the slopes of two slices of the surface $$z = f(x,y)$$ through the point $$(a,b,f(a,b))$$.
3. Compute second-order and mixed partial derivatives, and use Clairaut's Theorem to decide when the order of differentiation doesn't matter.
4. Explain why existing partial derivatives don't guarantee continuity, and use the Increment Theorem to test differentiability.

### Partial derivatives are slopes of slices

Let $$f(x,y)$$ be a function of two variables and fix a point $$(a,b)$$. The **partial derivative with respect to $$x$$** at $$(a,b)$$ measures how fast $$f$$ changes as $$x$$ moves while $$y$$ stays at $$y = b$$:

$$f_x(a,b) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a+h,\, b) - f(a,b)}{h}$$

The partial derivative with respect to $$y$$ does the same job with $$x$$ held at $$x = a$$:

$$f_y(a,b) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a,\, b+h) - f(a,b)}{h}$$

You'll also see $$\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}$$ or $$f_1$$ for the first, and $$z_x$$ when the function is written as $$z = f(x,y)$$. They all mean the same thing.

Geometrically, picture the surface $$z = f(x,y)$$ and slice it with the plane $$y = b$$. The slice is a curve, $$z = f(x,b)$$, lying in that plane. The number $$f_x(a,b)$$ is the slope of the tangent line to that curve at $$(a,b,f(a,b))$$. Slicing with $$x = a$$ instead gives a second curve, $$z = f(a,y)$$, and its slope at the same point is $$f_y(a,b)$$. Those two slices and their tangent lines are exactly what the demo below draws.

In practice you won't use the limit. To find $$f_x$$, treat $$y$$ as a constant and differentiate with respect to $$x$$ using the rules you already know. To find $$f_y$$, do the reverse. Here's a full example.

> **Worked example.** Let $$f(x,y) = x^3y^2 - 2x^2y - 3x + y$$. Find $$f_x(1,1)$$ and $$f_y(1,1)$$, then find the two lines tangent to the surface at $$(1,1,-3)$$: $$L_1$$ parallel to the $$xz$$-plane and $$L_2$$ parallel to the $$yz$$-plane.
>
> Treating $$y$$ as a constant gives $$f_x = 3x^2y^2 - 4xy - 3$$, so $$f_x(1,1) = -4$$. Treating $$x$$ as a constant gives $$f_y = 2x^3y - 2x^2 + 1$$, so $$f_y(1,1) = 1$$. The height at the point is $$f(1,1) = -3$$.
>
> $$L_1$$ lies in the plane $$y = 1$$ with slope $$-4$$ in the $$x$$-direction, so $$z = -4x + 1$$, or $$\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle 1, 1, -3 \rangle + t\langle 1, 0, -4 \rangle$$. $$L_2$$ lies in the plane $$x = 1$$ with slope $$1$$ in the $$y$$-direction, so $$z = y - 4$$, or $$\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle 1, 1, -3 \rangle + t\langle 0, 1, 1 \rangle$$.

The most common slip is to treat the frozen variable as if it had disappeared. When you differentiate $$xy^2$$ with respect to $$x$$, the $$y^2$$ is a constant factor that stays. The answer is $$y^2$$, not $$0$$ and not $$2xy$$.

### Functions of more than two variables

The same rule works with any number of variables: freeze everything except the one you're differentiating with respect to. Nothing new happens, but the bookkeeping gets longer, so give each partial its own line.

> **Worked example.** Assume $$x$$, $$y$$, and $$z$$ are independent, and let $$f(x,y,z) = x^2\sin(yz^3) - \ln(xyz)$$. Then
>
> $$f_x = 2x\sin(yz^3) - \frac{1}{x}, \qquad f_y = x^2z^3\cos(yz^3) - \frac{1}{y}, \qquad f_z = 3x^2yz^2\cos(yz^3) - \frac{1}{z}.$$
>
> In each case the logarithm contributes a reciprocal. For instance, $$\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\ln(xyz) = \frac{yz}{xyz} = \frac{1}{x}$$, because $$y$$ and $$z$$ are constants when you differentiate with respect to $$x$$.

### Higher-order partials and Clairaut's Theorem

Partial derivatives are functions too, so you can differentiate them again. A second derivative in the same variable is written $$f_{xx}$$ or $$f_{yy}$$. Mixed partials differentiate with respect to both variables, and subscripts are read left to right in the order you differentiate. So $$f_{xy}$$ means differentiate with respect to $$x$$ first, then with respect to $$y$$. The Leibniz form $$\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y\,\partial x}$$ means the same thing, written in the opposite order.

> **Worked example.** Let $$f(x,y) = x\sin y - e^{xy^2}$$. The first partials are
>
> $$f_x = \sin y - y^2e^{xy^2}, \qquad f_y = x\cos y - 2xy\,e^{xy^2}.$$
>
> Differentiating again gives $$f_{xx} = -y^4e^{xy^2}$$ and $$f_{yy} = -x\sin y - 2xe^{xy^2}(1 + 2xy^2)$$. The two mixed partials are both $$f_{xy} = f_{yx} = \cos y - 2ye^{xy^2}(1 + xy^2)$$.
>
> The two mixed partials agree. That's not a coincidence, and the next theorem says when it's guaranteed.

**Clairaut's Theorem** (also called Schwarz's Theorem). Suppose $$f$$, $$f_x$$, $$f_y$$, $$f_{xy}$$, and $$f_{yx}$$ are all defined on an open region containing $$(a,b)$$, and $$f_{xy}$$ and $$f_{yx}$$ are continuous at $$(a,b)$$. Then $$f_{xy}(a,b) = f_{yx}(a,b)$$.

For nearly every function you'll meet in this course, the order of differentiation never changes the answer. It can change how much work you do, though.

> **Worked example.** Let $$z = x^3y + \dfrac{y\sin(y^2)}{y^2+3}$$, and find $$z_{yx}$$. Differentiating with respect to $$y$$ first gives
>
> $$z_y = x^3 + \frac{(\sin y^2 + 2y^2\cos y^2)(y^2+3) - 2y^2\sin y^2}{(y^2+3)^2}.$$
>
> The big fraction has no $$x$$ in it, so the $$x$$-derivative wipes it out and leaves $$z_{yx} = 3x^2$$. You never have to simplify that fraction.

### Differentiability and continuity

Here's the surprise. In one variable, a derivative at a point forces the function to be continuous there. In two variables, the existence of both partial derivatives at a point is not enough to make the function continuous, let alone smooth. Each partial derivative looks only along one straight line through the point, so it can miss what happens in every other direction.

What we actually want is differentiability. A function $$f$$ is **differentiable at $$(a,b)$$** when its change can be written as a linear part plus a small error:

$$\Delta f = f_x(a,b)\,\Delta x + f_y(a,b)\,\Delta y + \varepsilon_1\,\Delta x + \varepsilon_2\,\Delta y,$$

where $$\varepsilon_1$$ and $$\varepsilon_2$$ go to $$0$$ as $$(\Delta x, \Delta y) \to (0,0)$$. In plain words, the linear approximation $$f_x\,\Delta x + f_y\,\Delta y$$ is good enough that its error shrinks faster than the step does.

The practical test is the **Increment Theorem**: if $$f_x$$ and $$f_y$$ exist on an open region containing $$(a,b)$$ and are both continuous at $$(a,b)$$, then $$f$$ is differentiable at $$(a,b)$$. Differentiability in turn implies continuity, because as $$(\Delta x, \Delta y) \to (0,0)$$ the right-hand side goes to $$0$$, so $$\Delta f \to 0$$.

> **Worked example.** Is $$f(x,y) = x\sin y - e^{xy^2}$$ differentiable at $$(1,1)$$? Its first partials, from the worked example above, are $$f_x = \sin y - y^2e^{xy^2}$$ and $$f_y = x\cos y - 2xye^{xy^2}$$. Both are built from polynomials, $$\sin$$, $$\cos$$, and exponentials, with no denominators, so both are continuous everywhere, including at $$(1,1)$$. By the Increment Theorem, $$f$$ is differentiable at $$(1,1)$$. Near that point,
>
> $$\Delta f \approx (\sin 1 - e)\,\Delta x + (\cos 1 - 2e)\,\Delta y \approx -1.877\,\Delta x - 4.896\,\Delta y.$$

### Practice & reflection

1. In your own words, what changes when you go from an ordinary derivative to a partial derivative? Think about a temperature map: what does it mean to keep your longitude fixed and move north?
2. Clairaut's Theorem assumes the mixed partials are continuous, not just that they exist. Why would existence alone fail to guarantee that $$f_{xy}(a,b) = f_{yx}(a,b)$$?
3. Why does knowing $$f_x(a,b)$$ and $$f_y(a,b)$$ tell you nothing direct about how $$f$$ changes along a diagonal direction? Which directions do the two partial derivatives actually see?
4. Suppose $$f_x(a,b) = 0$$. What does that say about the slice through $$(a,b)$$ in the $$x$$-direction? Can $$f_y(a,b)$$ still be nonzero? Sketch the picture and explain.
