Balance the Plate

§14.2 — find the balance point of a thin plate by moving a pivot, then compare your guess with the center of mass and the moments of inertia from the double integrals.

Each plate is a flat region with a density ρ(x, y); darker blue means denser. Click or drag in the picture, or use the number boxes, to put the blue pivot where you think the plate would balance. The two torque readouts say how hard the plate tips about the vertical and the horizontal line through the pivot (they are My − pxM and Mx − pyM). When both are zero the plate balances. The numbers for the moments of inertia and radii of gyration do not depend on the pivot.

The center of mass is hidden until you tick “Reveal the center of mass”, so you can guess first.

Things to try

  1. Pick plate 1, the triangle with density x2 + y. Before you reveal anything, drag the pivot to your best guess for the balance point. Is it nearer the right side or the left, and why? Then read the two torques: which way do they say it tips? Reveal the center of mass to see how close you were. (The section page computes this one by hand.)
  2. On plate 4 (density 1 + x), the plate is symmetric top to bottom. Predict the y coordinate of the center of mass without any torque readout, then check. What does the symmetry tell you about Mx?
  3. Pick plate 5, the annulus. Predict where its center of mass is, then reveal it. Is the pivot placed there actually touching the plate? What does that say about the center of mass of an object?
  4. Compare plate 6 (a disk) with plate 5 (an annulus). Both have constant density 1 and the same outer radius. Predict which has the bigger I0 and which has the bigger r0, then check the readouts. For the annulus, test the formula I0 = ½M(a12 + a22) from the section page.
  5. Pick plate 3, the half-disk. Its center of mass is not at the middle of the flat edge and not at the middle of the whole region. Guess its height above the flat edge, then reveal it and compare with the radius 2.
